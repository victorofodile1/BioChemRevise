Nutrient Cycles

Energy flows through an ecosystem once and is lost as heat — but chemical elements can't be lost, so nitrogen and phosphorus have to be constantly recycled. This page covers the microorganisms that do the recycling, the five named processes of the nitrogen cycle, the shorter phosphorus cycle with no gas phase, and what goes wrong — leaching and eutrophication — when fertiliser use disrupts the balance.

⚠️

The single habit that protects the most marks on this spec point: every named process is marked as process name plus correctly-directed conversion, together — "nitrification" alone, or "ammonium → nitrate" with no process name, scores nothing on its own. The most common mistake nationally is misattributing which conditions favour which step — confusing aerobic nitrification with anaerobic denitrification, or reversing the nitrite/nitrate order. And "nutrients" alone is never credited — always name the specific ion or molecule (ammonium ions, nitrate ions, amino acids).

Section 1 · spec content point

🌍 Why nutrients must be recycled

This is the synoptic starting point for the whole spec point: unlike energy, which flows through an ecosystem once, chemical elements are finite and have to be reused. Everything else in 3.5.4 is really the supply chain behind this one idea.

📖

What the specification says: "Nutrients are recycled within natural ecosystems, exemplified by the nitrogen cycle and the phosphorus cycle."

1
2
3

Energy is like a candle burning — once it's used up as heat, it's gone for good. But atoms are like Lego bricks: they get passed from creature to creature and reused over and over, never actually disappearing. Nitrogen bricks get built into proteins and DNA; phosphorus bricks get built into DNA and the cell's energy currency, ATP. To pull these bricks out of the soil, a plant's roots have to actively "pump" them in against the flow, which costs energy — a bit like pumping water uphill instead of letting it flow downhill on its own.

  • Active transport = moving something using energy, against its natural direction of flow.
  • ATP = the cell's energy "currency," made by respiration.
  • Concentration gradient = the difference in concentration between two places — things naturally flow from high to low, unless pushed the other way.

Energy passes through an ecosystem just once and ends up lost as heat, but atoms don't disappear — nitrogen and phosphorus atoms get reused again and again. Living things build lots of important molecules out of nitrogen: proteins, DNA and RNA, and energy molecules like ATP all contain nitrogen, and so does the green pigment chlorophyll. Phosphorus turns up in many of the same molecules — ATP, DNA, RNA — plus cell membranes (phospholipids) and some photosynthesis molecules. Plant roots pull mineral ions like nitrate and phosphate out of the soil using active transport, which costs energy because there's usually more of the ion inside the root than in the soil around it, so the plant has to work against the concentration gradient.

Energy entering an ecosystem flows through it once and is eventually lost from the system as heat (see 3.5.3) — but the atoms that make up living organisms are conserved. Chemical elements such as nitrogen and phosphorus are finite, so they must be captured, used, released and reused indefinitely; microorganisms are what drive this recycling, returning elements locked up in dead organic matter and waste back into a form plants can absorb again.

Nitrogen-containing molecules worth being able to name

  • Amino acids / proteins / polypeptides
  • Nucleic acids / nucleotides / DNA / RNA
  • ATP / ADP
  • NAD / NADP
  • Cyclic AMP
  • Chlorophyll

Phosphorus-containing molecules worth being able to name

  • ATP / ADP
  • Phospholipids
  • DNA / RNA
  • RuBP
  • Triose phosphate / GP
▶ 🚩 Trap — the inorganic ions themselves don't count here

The inorganic ions — nitrate, nitrite, ammonia, ammonium, urea, phosphate — are explicitly rejected as answers to "name a nitrogen- or phosphorus-containing molecule." The question wants the organic molecules these ions get built into once absorbed, not the ions themselves.

How mineral ions actually get into a root

Ions are absorbed into root hair cells by active transport, which requires ATP supplied by (aerobic) respiration in the root cells. This is necessary because the concentration of a mineral ion in the soil solution is usually lower than inside the root hair cell, so ions have to move against their concentration gradient — diffusion alone could never achieve this.

▶ 🚩 Trap — don't bring bacteria into this specific answer

Reference to bacteria is explicitly rejected when the question is specifically about the mechanism of ion uptake by roots — that demand is about active transport, not about the microbial supply chain that produced the ions in the first place.

Section 2 · spec content point

🍂 Saprobionts and decomposition

Saprobionts are how nutrients get back out of dead matter in the first place — the very first link in the recycling chain, before any of the named nitrogen-cycle processes can happen.

📖

What the specification says: "Microorganisms play a vital role in recycling chemical elements such as phosphorus and nitrogen. The role of saprobionts in decomposition."

1
2
3

Saprobionts are nature's recyclers. Instead of eating something whole, they spit digestive juices onto dead things, wait for them to dissolve into mush, and then soak the mush up like a sponge. When the dead thing contains nitrogen (which most living things do, because they're built from protein), this soaking-up process releases a nitrogen-containing chemical called ammonium into the soil — ready to be used again.

  • Saprobiont = an organism (bacterium or fungus) that feeds on dead organic matter.
  • Extracellular = outside the cell.
  • Ammonification = the special name for this process when nitrogen-containing waste is being broken down.

Saprobionts — decomposer bacteria and fungi — don't eat solid food. They release enzymes onto dead plants and animals, and onto waste like urea and faeces, digest it outside their own bodies, and then absorb the soluble products that are released. When the material has nitrogen in it, this process gets a special name: ammonification, and what's released is ammonium ions — the raw material the rest of the nitrogen cycle depends on.

Saprobiotic nutrition is how decomposers feed: saprobiotic bacteria and fungi secrete extracellular enzymes onto dead organic matter, digesting it outside their own cells, then absorb the soluble products of that digestion. Where the material being broken down is nitrogen-containing, this same process has a more specific name — ammonification: the decomposition of nitrogen-containing compounds such as proteins and urea in dead organisms and waste, releasing ammonium ions into the soil.

What's digestedWhat's released
Protein / DNA / RNA / urea (secreted enzymes act extracellularly)Ammonium ions / ammonium compounds — this is ammonification
▶ 🚩 The single costliest imprecision on this section

This content point is very often written as "saprobionts release nutrients for growth" — vague, generic language that scores nothing. Always name what is digested and what is released — for example, digestion of protein releases amino acids, and ammonification specifically releases ammonium ions.

Section 3 · spec content point

🍄 Mycorrhizae

A genuinely mutualistic partnership, examined more as an experimental-design and data-evaluation question than a pure recall question.

📖

What the specification says: "The role of mycorrhizae in facilitating the uptake of water and inorganic ions by plants."

1
2
3

Think of mycorrhizal fungi as an extension cable for a plant's roots — the fungus's thread-like body spreads much further into the soil than roots alone can reach, hoovering up extra water and minerals (especially phosphate) and handing them to the plant. In exchange, the plant pays the fungus in sugar, made using sunlight. It's a fair trade, not a one-sided favour.

  • Mycorrhizae = the partnership between a fungus and a plant's roots.
  • Hyphae = the fine, thread-like filaments a fungus is made of.
  • Mutualistic = both partners benefit.

Some plant roots team up with fungi in a partnership called mycorrhizae. The fungus's thread-like hyphae spread out much further than the roots alone could reach, so together they can soak up far more water and dissolved minerals — especially phosphate, which barely moves through soil on its own. The plant pays the fungus back with sugars it makes by photosynthesis, so both sides benefit. When this is tested experimentally, growth is compared between plants with a living fungus, a dead (heat-treated) fungus, and no fungus at all, so a real effect of the living fungus can be told apart from other explanations.

Fungal hyphae form a mutualistic association with plant roots, greatly increasing the surface area available for water and inorganic-ion uptake — especially phosphate, which is relatively immobile in soil and otherwise hard for a plant to access in useful quantities. In exchange, the plant supplies the fungus with organic compounds/sugars made by photosynthesis — a genuine two-way trade, not a one-sided benefit to either partner.

🧪

Practical-skills link: the specification names "devise investigations into the effect of named minerals on plant growth" as a skill opportunity here — in practice this is examined through the mycorrhizae/fertiliser growth experiment below, so expect to be asked to design or critique such an investigation, not just recall its result.

Designing the mycorrhizal growth experiment

  • Sterilise seeds/soil first — removes confounding fungi/bacteria already present, so only the deliberately added fungus can affect growth.
  • Use a single fertiliser/mineral type at a time — isolates that one variable.
  • Include a heat-treated (dead) fungus control — separates the fungal effect from the fertiliser effect, and from the fungus's mere physical presence.
  • Keep all other variables constant — light, temperature, water, pot size.
  • Take a large enough sample with repeats — to allow a statistical test.
▶ Evaluating the growth-experiment data

Overlapping standard deviations/error bars is credited as reasoning towards "probably no significant difference between treatments" — a bare comparison of the mean growth values, with no reference to the spread of the data, is not. Other valid evaluation points include: no formal statistical test was carried out; the experiment ran for a limited time period only (so long-term effects are unknown); and only shoot growth was measured, so any difference in root growth underground is not known.

Section 4 · spec content point

🔄 The nitrogen cycle: five bacterial processes

The full cycle, redrawn as one diagram — every process paired with the conditions it needs, and the direction its conversion runs in.

📖

What the specification says: "The role of bacteria in the nitrogen cycle in sufficient detail to illustrate the processes of saprobiotic nutrition, ammonification, nitrification, nitrogen fixation and denitrification. (The names of individual species of bacteria are not required.)"

1
2
3

Nitrogen gets "in" to living things by two doorways: bacteria in the soil that grab it from the air, and bacteria living inside special swellings on some plants' roots that do the same thing. Dead things and waste also feed nitrogen back in, once decomposers have broken them down. Once it's in the soil as ammonium, other bacteria upgrade it — first to nitrite, then to nitrate — but only if there's enough oxygen. Plants then soak up mostly nitrate. Nitrogen only leaves again by one doorway: bacteria in waterlogged, oxygen-free soil that turn nitrate back into a gas and release it to the air.

  • Nitrification = ammonium upgraded to nitrite, then nitrate (needs oxygen)
  • Nitrogen fixation = nitrogen gas turned into ammonium (costs energy)
  • Denitrification = nitrate turned back into nitrogen gas (needs no oxygen)

Five processes make up the nitrogen cycle. Saprobiotic nutrition and ammonification (covered in Section 2) release ammonium ions from dead matter and waste. Nitrification then oxidises that ammonium in two steps — first to nitrite, then to nitrate — and needs well-oxygenated, aerobic soil. Nitrogen fixation is a separate way nitrogen enters the system: bacteria (some living freely in soil, others living inside root nodules of bean-family plants) convert atmospheric nitrogen gas into ammonia, using a lot of energy to do it. Denitrification is the only way nitrogen leaves again — bacteria in waterlogged, oxygen-free soil convert nitrate back into nitrogen gas, which is bad news for farmers because it removes nitrate their crops need.

📌

The specification is explicit that individual bacterial genus/species names are not required. The names Nitrosomonas (nitrite step), Nitrobacter (nitrate step), Azotobacter (free-living fixation) and Rhizobium (mutualistic fixation, in legume root nodules) recur constantly in mark schemes and diagrams — but a correctly-named process plus the correctly-directed conversion always scores the marks on its own, with or without a genus name.

Nitrogen enters usable biology by two fixation routes and by ammonification of dead matter; it leaves again by only one route, denitrification, and only under anaerobic conditions. Nitrification always runs ammonium → nitrite → nitrate — a very commonly reversed pair.

Applying the cycle: waterlogging

Waterlogged soil is anaerobic, so nitrification (which needs oxygen) is reduced, meaning less ammonium is oxidised to nitrite and then to nitrate. Anaerobic conditions simultaneously favour denitrifying bacteria, so more nitrate is converted back to nitrogen gas by denitrification. Both effects reduce the nitrate available for plant uptake.

▶ 🚩 Trap — the order must always be nitrite, then nitrate

Wherever nitrification is described, the order nitrite-then-nitrate is required — reversing it loses the mark even if both words are present somewhere in the answer. Also, do not confuse nitrification with nitrogen fixation: they start from different materials — nitrification starts from ammonium already in the soil, while fixation starts from atmospheric N₂.

Applying the cycle: why nitrogen fixation is energetically expensive

Nitrogen gas is converted to ammonia/ammonium, requiring ATP to drive the enzyme nitrogenase and reduce the very stable N≡N triple bond. This ATP is diverted away from growth and protein synthesis — which is why legumes given fertiliser directly often grow faster than those relying solely on fixation.

Applying the cycle: ecological succession

As succession proceeds, dead organic matter, humus and decomposition all increase — providing more substrate for ammonifying and nitrifying bacteria, and improving soil conditions in ways that favour nitrogen-fixing bacteria too. So both nitrification and nitrogen fixation increase over time as succession advances.

Section 5 · spec content point

🪨 The phosphorus cycle

The same recycling logic as nitrogen, but with a single, sharply-examined difference: no gaseous phase at all.

📖

What the specification says: "Nutrients are recycled within natural ecosystems, exemplified by the nitrogen cycle and the phosphorus cycle."

1
2
3

Rain and weather slowly wear away rock, releasing phosphate into the soil and water. Plant roots (helped by their fungal partners) soak it up and build it into their bodies. Animals get it by eating plants. When anything dies, or poos, decomposers release the phosphate back into the soil so it can be used again. The one big difference from nitrogen: phosphate never becomes a gas, so this whole loop moves much more slowly — it all depends on rock wearing away, which takes a very long time.

Phosphate ions are released from rock as it weathers, dissolving into soil and water. Plant roots absorb it by active transport (helped by mycorrhizal fungi, since phosphate barely moves through soil on its own), and use it to build ATP, DNA, RNA and phospholipids. It then passes along the food chain as animals eat plants, and returns to the soil when organisms excrete, egest or die and saprobionts decompose the waste. Some phosphate ends up lost to ocean sediment, only returning after geological timescales. Unlike nitrogen, phosphorus never enters the atmosphere as a gas — there's no phosphorus equivalent of fixation or denitrification — so the whole cycle runs much more slowly.

  1. Weathering of rock releases phosphate ions into the soil and water.
  2. Root uptake by active transport, assisted by mycorrhizae (see Section 3), since phosphate is otherwise relatively immobile in soil.
  3. Assimilation — used to build ATP, DNA, RNA and phospholipids.
  4. Feeding — passed along the food chain as organisms are eaten.
  5. Excretion, egestion & death — returned to soil/water via saprobiont action (the same decomposer role as in Section 2).
  6. Some phosphate is lost to ocean sediment, returning only over geological time.
⚖️

The key examined contrast with the nitrogen cycle: the phosphorus cycle has no gaseous phase — phosphorus never enters the atmosphere, so the cycle is slower and depends on rock weathering and sedimentary deposits, rather than the microbial gas exchange that drives the nitrogen cycle.

▶ 🚩 Trap — don't invent a phosphorus equivalent of fixation or denitrification

There is no phosphorus equivalent of nitrogen fixation or denitrification — phosphate is never converted to or from a gas. Keep the phosphorus-cycle steps (weathering, uptake, assimilation, feeding, decomposition, sediment loss) entirely separate from the gas-driven nitrogen-cycle steps; don't borrow vocabulary between the two cycles.

Section 6 · spec content point

🌾 Fertilisers: replacing what farming removes

Farming interrupts the natural recycling loop — this section is about why, and about the trade-off between the two ways of putting nutrients back.

📖

What the specification says: "The use of natural and artificial fertilisers to replace the nitrates and phosphates lost by harvesting plants and removing livestock."

1
2
3

In the wild, when a plant or animal dies, its nutrients go straight back into the soil to be reused. But on a farm, crops get picked and driven away, and animals get moved off the field — so the nutrients trapped inside them leave with them, and never get the chance to go back into the soil. Fertiliser is how farmers top the soil back up. One kind (natural, like manure) releases slowly and is a bit like a slow-release capsule. The other kind (artificial, like a pure chemical salt) is a bit like a fast-dissolving tablet — quick, but easy to wash away in the rain.

In a natural ecosystem, dead organisms return their nutrients to the soil. On a farm, crops are harvested and livestock removed — taking the nitrogen and phosphorus they contain away with them — so the soil gradually gets depleted, and fertiliser is used to put it back. Natural (organic) fertiliser — manure, slurry, compost — releases its nutrients slowly and improves soil structure, but exactly how much of each nutrient it contains is variable and hard to know precisely. Artificial (inorganic) fertiliser has a known, exact chemical composition and works fast, but because it's so soluble it gets washed out of the soil (leached) easily, and can affect soil pH.

In a natural ecosystem, the nutrients contained in dead organisms are returned to the soil by decomposition. In farming, crops are harvested and livestock are removed from the land, taking the nitrates and phosphates they contain away with them — the soil becomes progressively depleted, and fertilisers are applied to replace what is lost.

Fertiliser typeWhat it isAdvantagesDisadvantages
Natural / organicManure, slurry, compost, bone mealReleased slowly; improves soil structure; less leachingVariable / unknown exact composition
Artificial / inorganicPure inorganic salts, e.g. ammonium nitrateKnown exact composition; rapidly availableHighly soluble, easily leached; can change soil pH and structure

Why excess fertiliser can actually reduce plant growth

Excess fertiliser lowers the water potential of the soil solution. If this falls below the water potential of the root cells, water moves out of the root by osmosis instead of in — the plant takes up less water, and roots may even become damaged. A reference to the direction of the water-potential gradient, or the direction water moves in, is what makes this a complete answer; a reference to minerals themselves moving by osmosis is incorrect, since osmosis only describes water movement.

▶ Evaluating fertiliser dose-response data

When asked to evaluate data on crop yield against fertiliser dose over time, look for: diminishing returns — using more fertiliser but getting a smaller and smaller yield increase as time goes on; the correlation-does-not-prove-causation caveat — the graph shows a relationship, but other factors could be responsible for a change in yield; and declining cost-effectiveness — once yield plateaus, extra fertiliser costs money without extra benefit.

Section 7 · spec content point

🌊 Leaching and eutrophication

A strictly-ordered causal chain — every step is examined by its mechanism, and the mark scheme insists on the sequence being right, not just the facts being present somewhere.

📖

What the specification says: "The environmental issues arising from the use of fertilisers including leaching and eutrophication."

1
2
3

Imagine rain washing spare fertiliser off a field and into a river. There's suddenly loads of extra "food" for algae, so they explode in number and cover the surface like a green blanket. That blanket blocks sunlight from reaching the plants underneath, so those plants can't photosynthesise and they die. Now there's a pile of dead stuff for decomposer bacteria to feast on — and as they multiply and breathe, they use up nearly all the oxygen dissolved in the water. With the oxygen gone, fish and other water animals can't breathe, and they die too. One event at the start causes a whole chain reaction.

Leaching is soluble fertiliser being washed out of the soil by rain into a waterway. Once there, the extra nitrate and phosphate cause an algal bloom — rapid growth of algae at the surface, because nutrient availability was what had been limiting their growth. The bloom physically blocks light from reaching plants growing below the surface, so those plants die from lack of photosynthesis. Decomposers then break down all this extra dead material, and as they respire aerobically to do so, they use up dissolved oxygen in the water — this rising oxygen demand is called biochemical oxygen demand, or BOD. With oxygen depleted, fish and other aerobic organisms in the water die too.

Each step below is examined by its mechanism — why it causes the next step — not just as a labelled event, and the sequence itself must be given in this order.

Leaching → algal bloom → light blocked → plants die → decomposer activity rises (BOD rises) → oxygen depleted → aerobic organisms die. The mark scheme requires this order, not just the individual facts stated anywhere in the answer.
▶ 🚩 The costliest error — scrambling the order

The sequence must be correct, not just the individual facts in any order — scrambling the causal chain loses marks even where every fact stated is individually true. Light must be blocked before plants die; plants must die before decomposer activity (and the resulting oxygen depletion) can increase.

Measuring nutrient concentration practically

Use a colorimeter to measure absorbance or percentage transmission of light through the sample. Standardise the method: use the same volume of sample each time, zero/calibrate the colorimeter against a blank, and use the same wavelength/filter throughout.

▶ 🚩 Trap — colorimeter, not calorimeter

The instrument that measures nutrient concentration by absorbance is the colorimeter, not the calorimeter — a calorimeter measures energy released (examined back in 3.5.3). This spelling/concept mix-up recurs often enough to be worth double-checking every time you write it.

Section 8 · exam technique

🎯 Common mistakes & precise wording

This spec point is difficult less because the biology is conceptually hard, and more because so much of it rides on naming the right organism, the right condition, and the right ion, every time.

▶ Why this spec point is ranked hard

This is A2-only content, not tested at AS, with a high memorisation load: five named nitrogen-cycle processes, each with its own conditions, plus a separately-ordered phosphorus cycle and a separately-ordered eutrophication chain. Application depth is also high — the hardest recurring skill is not recalling the five processes in isolation, but applying them correctly to an unfamiliar scenario (waterlogging, succession, or an unfamiliar organism) while keeping every organism/condition pairing correct. For any scenario question, work through the processes in a fixed mental order — fixation, ammonification, nitrification, uptake/assimilation, denitrification — and ask which one(s) the scenario is changing, and why (usually via oxygen availability).

▶ Command words used on this spec point

Describe is the dominant command here — "describe leaching and eutrophication" or "describe the phosphorus cycle" wants the full ordered sequence, not isolated facts. Explain wants reasoning linked to each process (e.g. the oxygen-availability reasoning behind waterlogging), not just process names. Name is scored on the correct technical term alone, but the conversion must still be correctly identified to prove understanding. Evaluate appears for fertiliser dose-response data and mycorrhizal growth-experiment data, weighing effect size against experimental limitations. Suggest appears for unfamiliar nutrient-cycling scenarios that still rest on the same five named processes.

Section 9 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

3.5.4 is a common closing paragraph in essay-style questions about nitrogen or phosphorus — it supplies the element that every other paragraph in the essay assumes a continual supply of.

▶ Example paragraph — a nitrogen-themed essay

Nitrogen is constantly recycled through an ecosystem by named microbial processes: ammonification releases ammonium ions from dead organisms and waste, by saprobionts; nitrification then oxidises this ammonium to nitrite and then nitrate, requiring oxygen; nitrogen fixation converts atmospheric nitrogen gas to ammonia, using the enzyme nitrogenase and a great deal of ATP; denitrification returns nitrate to nitrogen gas in anaerobic soils; and assimilation sees nitrate absorbed by active transport and built into amino acids, proteins, DNA and ATP. This spec point makes the nitrogen theme explicit at the ecosystem level — it explains where the nitrogen in all of an essay's other nitrogen-containing molecules ultimately comes from, and why it must be constantly recycled.

▶ Example paragraph — a phosphorus-themed essay

Phosphate ions in rocks are released by weathering into soil and water, absorbed by plant roots (assisted by mycorrhizae) by active transport, and used to make ATP, DNA, RNA and phospholipids; phosphate then passes along food chains and is returned to the soil by saprobiotic decomposition. Unlike the nitrogen cycle, the phosphorus cycle has no gaseous phase, so it is slower and depends on the weathering of rock; excess phosphate fertiliser can be leached into watercourses, causing eutrophication — an ecological cost of disrupting the natural supply of the element.

Whichever theme the essay is built around, treat 3.5.4 as the "supply" paragraph: state the recycling processes precisely, naming the ion or molecule at each step, then close with one sentence linking fertiliser misuse — leaching, eutrophication — to the ecological cost of disrupting that supply.

Memory hook — in by two routes, out by one. 5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Why nutrients must be recycled

  • Energy flows through an ecosystem once and is lost as heat; chemical elements (N, P) are finite and must be recycled.
  • Nitrogen-containing molecules: amino acids/proteins, nucleic acids/DNA/RNA, ATP/ADP, NAD/NADP, cyclic AMP, chlorophyll. Not the ions themselves.
  • Root uptake of ions is by active transport, needing ATP from aerobic respiration, against the concentration gradient.

2 · Saprobionts & decomposition

  • Saprobionts secrete extracellular enzymes onto dead matter, then absorb the soluble products.
  • Ammonification = the nitrogen-specific version — releases ammonium ions.
  • Never write "releases nutrients" — always name what's digested and what's released.

3 · Mycorrhizae

  • Fungal hyphae + plant roots = mutualistic partnership; massively increases surface area for water/ion (especially phosphate) uptake.
  • Plant repays the fungus with sugars from photosynthesis — a two-way trade.
  • Growth-experiment design: sterilise soil first, single fertiliser type, heat-treated dead-fungus control. Overlapping SDs → "probably no significant difference".

4 · The nitrogen cycle

  • Nitrification: NH₄⁺ → NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻, aerobic, two steps — order must be nitrite then nitrate.
  • Nitrogen fixation: N₂ → ammonia/ammonium, needs ATP + nitrogenase; free-living AND mutualistic (legume root nodules) routes.
  • Denitrification: NO₃⁻ → N₂, anaerobic/waterlogged — the only route out.
  • Waterlogging → less nitrification (needs O₂), more denitrification (anaerobic favoured).
  • Bacterial genus names are NOT required by the spec.

5 · The phosphorus cycle

  • Weathering → root uptake (active transport, mycorrhizae help) → assimilation (ATP/DNA/RNA/phospholipids) → food chain → decomposition → some lost to ocean sediment.
  • No gaseous phase — this is the headline contrast with nitrogen. No phosphorus "fixation" or "denitrification".

6 · Fertilisers

  • Needed because harvesting crops/removing livestock takes N and P away, depleting soil.
  • Natural: slow-release, improves soil structure, less leaching, but variable composition. Artificial: known composition, fast, but highly soluble/easily leached.
  • Excess fertiliser lowers soil water potential → water leaves roots by osmosis → less water uptake, reduced growth.

7 · Leaching & eutrophication

  • Strict order: leaching → algal bloom → light blocked → plants die → decomposers increase → oxygen depleted (BOD rises) → organisms die.
  • Scrambled-but-complete answers still lose marks — the sequence itself is examined.
  • Measure nutrient concentration with a colorimeter (absorbance), not a calorimeter (energy).
🧠

In by two routes, out by one. Fixation (free-living + mutualistic) and ammonification bring nitrogen in; only denitrification (anaerobic) takes it out. Nitrification always goes NH₄⁺ → NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻. Phosphorus runs the same loop with no gas phase at all.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Why nutrients must be recycled

❓ Explain why nitrogen and phosphorus need to be recycled within an ecosystem, but energy does not.
Energy entering an ecosystem is transformed and ultimately lost as heat with each transfer, so it cannot be reused; nitrogen and phosphorus are chemical elements, which are neither created nor destroyed, so they must be constantly recycled between organisms and the abiotic environment for life to continue.
❓ State two nitrogen-containing molecules and two phosphorus-containing molecules a plant might build from ions absorbed by its roots.
Nitrogen-containing: amino acids/proteins, nucleic acids/DNA/RNA (also accept ATP/ADP, NAD/NADP, cyclic AMP, chlorophyll). Phosphorus-containing: ATP/ADP, phospholipids (also accept DNA/RNA, RuBP, triose phosphate/GP).
❓ Explain how nitrate ions move into a root hair cell, given that their concentration is usually higher inside the cell than in the surrounding soil.
Ions are absorbed by active transport; this requires ATP, supplied by aerobic respiration in the root hair cells; active transport is needed because the ions are moving against their concentration gradient, which diffusion alone could not achieve.

Saprobionts & decomposition

❓ Describe how saprobiotic bacteria and fungi obtain nutrients from dead organic matter.
They secrete extracellular enzymes onto the dead organic matter, digesting it outside their own cells; the soluble products of this digestion (e.g. amino acids) are then absorbed by the saprobiont.
❓ Explain what is meant by ammonification, and state what it releases into the soil.
Ammonification is the decomposition of nitrogen-containing compounds (such as proteins and urea) in dead organisms and waste, by saprobiotic bacteria and fungi; it releases ammonium ions into the soil, making nitrogen available again for the rest of the nitrogen cycle.
❓ A student answers a question on decomposition by writing "saprobionts release nutrients for growth." Explain why this would not gain full credit, and rewrite it correctly.
The answer is too vague — "nutrients" is not a specific enough term to be credited. A correct version names the actual molecule digested and ion released, e.g.: saprobionts digest nitrogen-containing waste extracellularly and release ammonium ions into the soil.

Mycorrhizae

❓ Describe the role of mycorrhizae in a plant's uptake of water and inorganic ions.
Fungal hyphae form a mutualistic association with the plant's roots, greatly increasing the surface area available for absorption; this increases uptake of water and inorganic ions, especially phosphate, which is otherwise relatively immobile in the soil; in return, the fungus receives organic compounds/sugars from the plant.
❓ Describe how you would design an experiment to test the effect of mycorrhizal fungi on plant growth, and explain one precaution needed.
Grow plants with living fungus, with a heat-treated (dead) fungus, and with no fungus, keeping fertiliser type, light, temperature, water and pot size constant across all groups, with enough repeats for a statistical test. Sterilising the seeds/soil beforehand is necessary to remove any fungi or bacteria already present, so only the deliberately added fungus can affect growth.
❓ In a mycorrhizal growth experiment, the standard deviations for the living-fungus and dead-fungus treatments overlap. What can be concluded?
Overlapping standard deviations suggest there is probably no significant difference between the two treatments — a bare comparison of the mean growth values alone, without reference to the spread of the data, would not be sufficient to support this conclusion.

The nitrogen cycle

❓ Name the process in which ammonium ions are converted to nitrite ions and then to nitrate ions, and state the conditions it requires.
This is nitrification; it is a two-step oxidation, requiring aerobic/well-aerated, oxygenated soil conditions.
❓ Explain why waterlogged soil reduces the nitrate available to plants.
Waterlogged soil is anaerobic, so nitrification (which requires oxygen) is reduced, meaning less ammonium is oxidised to nitrite and then to nitrate; anaerobic conditions also favour denitrifying bacteria, so more nitrate is converted to nitrogen gas by denitrification — both effects reduce the nitrate available for plant uptake.
❓ Explain why nitrogen fixation is described as energetically expensive to a plant.
Fixation requires ATP to drive the enzyme nitrogenase, which reduces the very stable triple bond in nitrogen gas to form ammonia/ammonium; this ATP is diverted away from growth and protein synthesis, which is why legumes given fertiliser directly often grow faster than those relying solely on fixation.

The phosphorus cycle

❓ Describe the sequence of the phosphorus cycle, from rock to a plant's roots.
Phosphate ions are released from rock by weathering into the soil and water; they are then absorbed by plant roots by active transport, a process assisted by mycorrhizae; the phosphate is then used to build molecules such as ATP, DNA, RNA and phospholipids.
❓ Explain the key difference between the phosphorus cycle and the nitrogen cycle.
The phosphorus cycle has no gaseous phase — phosphorus never enters the atmosphere — whereas the nitrogen cycle moves nitrogen into and out of the atmosphere via fixation and denitrification; this makes the phosphorus cycle slower, since it depends on the weathering of rock and sedimentary deposits rather than microbial gas exchange.
❓ A student writes that "phosphorus fixation" converts phosphate gas into phosphate ions. Explain why this is incorrect.
There is no gaseous phase, and therefore no equivalent of fixation, in the phosphorus cycle; phosphate ions are released from rock by weathering, not from a gas.

Fertilisers

❓ Explain why fertilisers are needed on farmland but not in a natural, undisturbed ecosystem.
In a natural ecosystem, nutrients in dead organisms and waste are returned to the soil by decomposition; on farmland, crops are harvested and livestock removed, taking the nitrates and phosphates they contain away with them, so the soil becomes depleted and fertilisers are needed to replace these lost nutrients.
❓ Compare one advantage and one disadvantage of natural fertiliser with one advantage and one disadvantage of artificial fertiliser.
Natural/organic fertiliser is released slowly and improves soil structure, but its exact composition is variable and unknown. Artificial/inorganic fertiliser has a known, exact composition and is rapidly available, but is highly soluble and therefore easily leached.
❓ Explain why applying excess fertiliser can reduce a plant's growth.
Excess fertiliser lowers the water potential of the soil solution; if this falls below the water potential of the root cells, water moves out of the root by osmosis instead of into it, so the plant takes up less water and its roots may become damaged.

Leaching & eutrophication

❓ Describe the sequence of events in eutrophication, starting with leaching.
Soluble nitrate and phosphate ions are leached from the soil into a waterway, causing an algal bloom; the bloom blocks light from reaching plants below the surface, so those plants die; decomposers/saprobiotic bacteria break down the increased dead organic matter, using up oxygen as they respire, so the biochemical oxygen demand rises sharply and fish and other aerobic organisms die from lack of oxygen.
❓ Explain why the order of events matters when describing eutrophication, using an example.
The mark scheme requires the steps in the correct causal order, since each step is examined by its mechanism — for example, light must be blocked before plants die, and plants must die before decomposer activity (and the resulting oxygen depletion) can increase; stating the same facts in the wrong order does not show the causal understanding being tested.
❓ Describe how you would use a colorimeter to measure the concentration of a nutrient such as nitrate in a water sample.
Use a colorimeter to measure the absorbance (or percentage transmission) of light through the sample; standardise the method by using the same volume of sample each time, zeroing/calibrating the colorimeter against a blank, and using the same wavelength/filter throughout.
