3.1Biological molecules

The chemistry the whole course is built on — monomers, polymers, proteins, enzymes, DNA, ATP, water and ions.

🧬3.1.1

Monomers and Polymers

The six ideas every biological-molecules topic is built on: what a monomer and a polymer actually are, the three families of monomer named on the spec, and how condensation and hydrolysis join and break them apart. Short on paper, but it resurfaces in almost every other topic in the course.
🍞3.1.2

Carbohydrates

Three monosaccharides, three disaccharide pairs, and one flipped hydroxyl group that decides whether a chain of glucose becomes an energy store or a cell wall — plus the three biochemical tests examiners mark stepwise, method and colour together.
🧈3.1.3

Lipids

How triglycerides and phospholipids are each built by condensation, why swapping one fatty acid for a phosphate group turns an energy store into a membrane, and how AQA tests the exact location of the C=C double bond, the wording "phosphate" (never "phosphorus"), and the full digestion-and-absorption pathway.
🧬3.1.4.1

General Properties of Proteins

How one monomer — the amino acid — builds every protein in the body: the peptide bond that joins them, the four levels of structure and exactly which bond is named at each one, why two proteins ever end up a different shape, and the biuret test used to detect a protein at all.
🧬3.1.4.2

Many Proteins Are Enzymes

How an enzyme lowers activation energy through induced fit, why each enzyme is locked to one complementary substrate, the six factors — including two very different types of inhibitor — that speed up or slow down an enzyme-controlled reaction, and the required practical that ties the whole topic together with a real investigation.
🧬3.1.5.1

Structure of DNA and RNA

What each nucleic acid actually does, the three-part nucleotide pattern both share, how two strands twist into DNA's double helix while RNA stays single and short, why DNA was ever doubted as the genetic material, and the one maths skill AQA names for this point — Chargaff's rule.
🧬3.1.5.2

DNA Replication

A small but vicious topic — only two official content points, yet one of the highest-difficulty spec points in the whole of 3.1. Helicase versus polymerase, the 5–6 mark mechanism sequence, why replication only runs one way, and the Meselson–Stahl evidence that proved the whole model, all need to be word-perfect.
🔋3.1.6

ATP

A small, three-fact topic — what ATP is built from, how it's broken down to release energy, and how it gets built back up again — whose real difficulty is a single wording trap that resurfaces in almost every energy-related topic on the course.
💧3.1.7

Water

One cause — water's polarity, and the hydrogen bonds it forms between molecules — produces five separately examined biological properties, plus the water-potential vocabulary that carries straight into osmosis, transport and homeostasis later in the course.
⚛️3.1.8

Inorganic Ions

Four named ions, four named jobs: hydrogen ions and pH, iron ions in haemoglobin, sodium ions in co-transport, and phosphate ions in DNA and ATP. A short spec point that only pays out when you can name the mechanism behind each ion, not just the association.
🔬

3.2Cells

Cell structure, microscopy, the cell cycle, transport across membranes and the immune system.

🔬3.2.1.1

Structure of Eukaryotic Cells

The ten named organelles every eukaryotic-cell question is built from — what each one actually looks like, the precise A-level function wording AQA credits (not the GCSE version), how a protein's whole journey through the cell links them together, and how the same organelles explain why specialised cells look the way they do.
🦠3.2.1.2

Structure of Prokaryotic Cells and of Viruses

A short spec point with a high confusion rate: two structure checklists — features in every bacterium versus features in only some — and three look-alike word pairs (capsid/capsule, DNA/RNA, attachment protein/receptor) between them account for most of the marks lost here.
🔬3.2.1.3

Methods of Studying Cells

How microscopes decide what can and can't be seen — resolution, not magnification — how to measure a structure and calculate its actual size from an image, and how ultracentrifugation takes a cell apart to study its organelles separately.
🧬3.2.2

All Cells Arise From Other Cells

How a eukaryotic cell divides by mitosis, why that division has to stay tightly controlled, how a prokaryotic cell divides by the simpler process of binary fission, and how the root-tip squash practical puts a number on how fast a tissue is dividing.
🚪3.2.3

Transport Across Cell Membranes

The fluid-mosaic model, all five ways a substance can cross a membrane, and the co-transport mechanism that examiners rate as the single hardest application skill on the AS specification — plus the two required practicals that carry roughly half of every mark on this page.
🦠3.2.4

Cell Recognition and the Immune System

How the body tells self from non-self, destroys pathogens by phagocytosis, mounts the cellular and humoral responses, and how vaccines, HIV and monoclonal antibodies all trade on the same recognition logic — the second most heavily-tested spec point on the whole course.
🫁

3.3Organisms exchange substances with their environment

Surface area to volume ratio, gas exchange, digestion, and mass transport in animals and plants.

📐3.3.1

Surface Area to Volume Ratio

Why the ratio between an organism's surface and its volume falls as it gets bigger, how to calculate that ratio for real shapes, and why the falling ratio is the single reason every exchange surface, transport system and ventilation mechanism in this course exists at all.
🫁3.3.2

Gas Exchange

How single-celled organisms, insects, fish and dicot leaves all solve the same problem — getting oxygen in and carbon dioxide out fast enough — and how the human gas exchange system does it at scale, from alveolus to ventilation mechanism to lung disease.
🍽️3.3.3

Digestion and Absorption

How mammals hydrolyse starch, lipid and protein into absorbable monomers, and exactly how the ileum epithelium gets those monomers into the blood — including the single most heavily-tested distinction on this page: which step of absorption is genuinely active transport, and which steps only look like it.
🫀3.3.4.1

Mass Transport in Animals

The human circulatory system end to end: how haemoglobin loads, carries and unloads oxygen; how the heart and its valves keep blood flowing one way; how artery, vein and capillary structure fits their job; and how tissue fluid forms and returns.
💧3.3.4.2

Mass Transport in Plants

Two mechanisms either side of one hard vocabulary boundary: the passive, tension-driven cohesion-tension theory pulls water up the xylem, while the active, ATP-dependent mass flow hypothesis pushes sucrose through the phloem from source to sink. Mixing the two tissues' vocabulary is the single most common error nationally on this spec point.
🧬

3.4Genetic information, variation and relationships between organisms

Genes and chromosomes, protein synthesis, meiosis, natural selection, taxonomy and biodiversity.

🧬3.4.1

DNA, Genes and Chromosomes

How DNA is organised into genes and chromosomes, why eukaryotic and prokaryotic DNA look so different down a microscope, and the three rules that make the genetic code work — the vocabulary every later 3.4 and 3.8 spec point assumes you already know precisely.
🧬3.4.2

DNA and Protein Synthesis

How the base sequence in DNA becomes a working protein: transcription in the nucleus builds mRNA from a DNA template, then translation at the ribosome reads that mRNA to build a polypeptide. AQA treats this as one of the hardest AS-level spec points — not because the ideas are conceptually difficult, but because it demands strict, molecule-specific terminology every single time.
🧬3.4.3

Genetic Diversity, Mutation and Meiosis

How brand-new alleles arise by mutation, and how meiosis reshuffles the alleles that already exist into new combinations — the two nuclear divisions, independent segregation, crossing over, and the random-fertilisation maths that ties it all together.
🧬3.4.4

Genetic Diversity and Adaptation

How genetic diversity gives natural selection something to act on, the fixed five-step chain that answers almost every selection question on this spec point, the two named types of selection — directional and stabilising — and how an adaptation gets classified as anatomical, physiological or behavioural.
🌳3.4.5

Species and Taxonomy

What actually makes two organisms the same species, why courtship behaviour matters, the eight-taxon hierarchy that classifies every living thing, how binomial names work, and how modern molecular evidence lets biologists test relatedness directly instead of guessing from appearance.
🌿3.4.6

Biodiversity Within a Community

How to measure how varied a community really is — species richness vs the index of diversity, a full worked calculation, valid random sampling technique, and the genuine two-sided balance between farming and conservation.
🧬3.4.7

Investigating Diversity

Two toolkits examined side by side: comparing how genetically diverse organisms are — from observable characteristics through to DNA, mRNA and amino acid sequence — and designing, then statistically testing, a quantitative investigation of variation. Short in content, but marked on exact wording throughout.
⚡

3.5Energy transfers in and between organisms

Photosynthesis, respiration, energy flow through ecosystems and the nitrogen and phosphorus cycles.

🌿3.5.1

Photosynthesis

How a chloroplast turns light energy into chemical energy — trapping light in the thylakoid membrane to build ATP and reduced NADP, then spending both in the stroma to fix carbon dioxide into sugar, plus the two required practicals that measure each half of the process in the lab.
🔋3.5.2

Respiration

How a cell actually releases the energy locked in glucose and packages it as ATP — from glycolysis in the cytoplasm, through the link reaction and Krebs cycle in the mitochondrial matrix, to oxidative phosphorylation on the cristae, plus what happens to the pathway when oxygen runs out and how fat and protein feed into the same system. Entirely A-level-only content.
🌾3.5.3

Energy and Ecosystems

How energy enters a food chain through photosynthesis, why nearly all of it leaks away at every single transfer, and how to rearrange the two given equations — NPP = GPP − R and N = I − (F + R) — and calculate percentage efficiency without losing a power of ten.
♻️3.5.4

Nutrient Cycles

Energy flows through an ecosystem once and is lost as heat — but chemical elements can't be lost, so nitrogen and phosphorus have to be constantly recycled. This page covers the microorganisms that do the recycling, the five named processes of the nitrogen cycle, the shorter phosphorus cycle with no gas phase, and what goes wrong — leaching and eutrophication — when fertiliser use disrupts the balance.
🧠

3.6Organisms respond to changes in their internal and external environments

Tropisms and reflexes, receptors, nerve impulses, synapses, muscles and homeostatic control.

🌱3.6.1.1

Survival and Response

Why organisms have to respond to their environment to survive — how plants bend towards light and away from gravity using unequal auxin, how the simplest animals of all keep themselves in a favourable place using taxis and kinesis, and how a three-neurone reflex arc turns a stimulus into a fast, protective response.
🔌3.6.1.2

Receptors

How a receptor turns a stimulus into a nervous impulse — the Pacinian corpuscle's stretch-mediated channels and generator potential, then how the wiring of rods and cones in the retina trades sensitivity against acuity.
❤️3.6.1.3

Control of Heart Rate

How the heart keeps its own beat — the myogenic conduction pathway (SAN → AVN → bundle of His → Purkyne fibres) that sets the rhythm from inside — and how chemoreceptors and baroreceptors, working through the medulla and the sympathetic and parasympathetic nerves, adjust that rhythm to match the body's changing needs.
⚡3.6.2.1

Nerve Impulses

How a neurone generates, propagates and speeds up an electrical impulse — the resting potential, the action potential, the all-or-nothing principle, the refractory period, and what actually makes some axons faster than others.
⚡3.6.2.2

Synaptic Transmission

How an electrical impulse in one neurone is converted into a chemical message, sent across a microscopic gap, and turned back into an electrical signal in the next cell — plus how weak signals can add up to a strong one, how a synapse can be told to stay quiet, and how to reason through a never-before-seen drug scenario using the same underlying chain.
💪3.6.3

Skeletal Muscles as Effectors

How a nerve impulse becomes a physical pull: antagonistic muscle pairs, the structural chain from whole muscle down to a single sarcomere, the calcium-triggered cross-bridge cycle that actually shortens it, phosphocreatine's rapid ATP top-up, and why slow- and fast-twitch fibres are built so differently.
⚖️3.6.4.1

Principles of Homeostasis and Negative Feedback

The control-system template every later homeostasis topic reuses: what homeostasis actually means, why cells need stable conditions at all, why blood glucose specifically has to stay within limits, how the receptor → coordinator → effector negative-feedback chain corrects a deviation in either direction, and why having two separate mechanisms — not one — gives a greater degree of control.
🩸3.6.4.2

Control of Blood Glucose Concentration

How insulin and glucagon run two completely separate, mirror-image negative-feedback loops to hold blood glucose within narrow limits, how adrenaline shares glucagon's second-messenger machinery but not its full toolkit, and how type I and type II diabetes fail this system in two genuinely different ways.
🫘3.6.4.3

Control of Blood Water Potential

How the nephron keeps the water potential of the blood inside a narrow range: bulk filtration under pressure at the glomerulus, selective reabsorption in the proximal convoluted tubule, a salt gradient built and maintained by the loop of Henle, and a final, moment-to-moment adjustment of water reabsorption at the distal convoluted tubule and collecting duct, switched by ADH.

3.7Genetics, populations, evolution and ecosystems

Monohybrid and dihybrid crosses, Hardy–Weinberg, speciation, succession and population ecology.

🧬3.7.1

Inheritance

How a genotype becomes a phenotype: the vocabulary underneath every genetics question, building a fully labelled genetic diagram for monohybrid and dihybrid crosses, and the three complications — sex linkage, autosomal linkage and epistasis — that stop a cross giving the textbook ratio. Finishes with the chi-squared test used to check whether a set of results really fits what you predicted.
🧬3.7.2

Populations

How a population's genetics is actually defined — its gene pool and allele frequency — and the Hardy–Weinberg model that predicts those frequencies will stay constant, provided five conditions hold. Plus what happens when they don't: how chance alone, through genetic drift, a founder event or a bottleneck, can reshape a population's genetics without any selection happening at all.
🧬3.7.3

Evolution May Lead to Speciation

How variation, selection and isolation combine to turn one population into two separate species — the fixed order AQA marks the causal chain in, the two named routes (allopatric and sympatric), and why chance — genetic drift — matters most when a population is small.
🌿3.7.4

Populations in Ecosystems

How ecologists actually measure and explain population change: the vocabulary that everything else nests inside, why populations level off at a carrying capacity, the full causal loop behind a predator–prey cycle, how quadrats, transects and mark–release–recapture turn a habitat into a number, and the staged process of succession from bare ground to a climax community — plus how conservation deliberately works against that process.
🧫

3.8The control of gene expression

Mutations, stem cells, epigenetics, cancer, genome projects and recombinant DNA technology.

🧬3.8.1

Alteration of the Sequence of Bases in DNA

How a single typo in a DNA sequence — one swapped, added, deleted, reversed, duplicated or relocated base — can ripple all the way through to a genuinely different protein, and why the exam almost always wants you to finish that chain rather than stop halfway.
🧬3.8.2.1

Most of a Cell's DNA is Not Translated

Every cell in the body is built from an identical genome, yet a neurone, a heart-muscle cell and a skin cell end up looking and behaving completely differently — this is the genetic basis of specialisation, and the reason totipotent, pluripotent, multipotent and unipotent stem cells all behave so differently from one another. This page also covers how induced pluripotent stem cells are made in the lab, and how to evaluate stem-cell treatments.
🧬3.8.2.2

Regulation of Transcription and Translation

A-level-only content on three separate ways a eukaryotic cell controls which of its genes actually get expressed: transcription factors — with oestrogen as the worked steroid-hormone example — epigenetic control by DNA methylation and histone acetylation, and control after transcription has already happened, at the translation stage, by RNA interference.
🧬3.8.2.3

Gene Expression and Cancer

How faults in two opposite gene types — and in the methylation that controls them — remove the normal checks on cell division, how the resulting tumours are told apart, and how understanding the two-gene logic feeds into prevention, diagnosis and treatment.
🧬3.8.3

Using Genome Projects

How the genome and proteome are precisely defined, how sequencing a simple organism's genome lets you derive its proteome (and find vaccine antigens), why complex organisms are much harder to decode, and how comparing genomes — between species, within a species, and against an organism's own proteome — drives evolutionary, medical and ethical questions.
🧬3.8.4.1

Recombinant DNA Technology

How a gene is cut out, copied, and inserted into another organism so it works there too — the small enzyme toolkit (restriction endonuclease, DNA ligase, reverse transcriptase, Taq polymerase), the polymerase chain reaction, and the full in vivo cloning pipeline from plasmid to marker gene.
🧬3.8.4.2

DNA Probes, Screening & Personalised Medicine

How a labelled DNA probe uses complementary base pairing to locate one exact allele — and how that single mechanism feeds three exam-tested applications: screening patients for heritable conditions, drug responses and health risks, genetic counselling, and personalised medicine.
🧬3.8.4.3

Genetic Fingerprinting

How PCR, restriction digestion and gel electrophoresis turn the non-coding repeats scattered through your genome into an individual-specific band pattern — and how that pattern gets read in paternity testing, forensic science, medical diagnosis and breeding. This is the final spec point of the full A-level Biology specification.
