Genetic Fingerprinting

How PCR, restriction digestion and gel electrophoresis turn the non-coding repeats scattered through your genome into an individual-specific band pattern — and how that pattern gets read in paternity testing, forensic science, medical diagnosis and breeding. This is the final spec point of the full A-level Biology specification.

⚠️

The two habits that protect the most marks on this spec point: a VNTR definition must name both ideas together — a short sequence repeated many times, in non-coding DNA, where the number of repeats at a locus is variable between individuals — giving only one of the two loses the mark, and confusing a VNTR with a coding gene sequence is a recorded common error. And whenever you compare gel patterns, structure the answer around specific, named bands and lanes — a vague "the patterns look similar" loses marks even when the final conclusion reached is correct.

Section 1 · spec content point

🔁 VNTRs: the repeats that make everyone's pattern unique

Everything else in this spec point — the technique, the applications, the interpretation — only works because of one underlying fact about the genome. Get this section watertight first.

📖

What the specification says: "An organism's genome contains many variable number tandem repeats (VNTRs). The probability of two individuals having the same VNTRs is very low."

1
2
3

Between your genes there are stretches of DNA that don't code for anything — think of them as blank filler. In that filler, some short patterns of bases repeat over and over, like a bead necklace with the same little charm strung on again and again. Everyone has these "bead necklaces," but the number of beads on each one is different from person to person — some people have a necklace with 4 repeats at a certain spot, someone else has 9 at that same spot. Because there are loads of these necklaces scattered through the genome, and loads of possible bead-counts at each one, the chance that two random people have exactly the same bead-count at every single necklace tested is tiny — unless they're identical twins, who share everything.

  • VNTR = "variable number tandem repeat" — a stretch of repeating DNA where the repeat count varies between people
  • Non-coding DNA = DNA that doesn't code for a protein — sits between genes
  • Locus (plural: loci) = the specific spot on the DNA where a particular VNTR sits
  • Tandem = repeated one after another, in a row

A VNTR is a short sequence of bases that repeats itself many times in a row, sitting in the non-coding DNA between genes. At any given VNTR locus, the number of repeats is different from person to person — that difference in repeat number is the entire basis of genetic fingerprinting. There isn't just one VNTR locus in the genome; there are many, and each one has many possible repeat numbers. Because the chance of matching at any single locus is already fairly low, and the loci behave independently of each other, the chance of two unrelated people matching at every locus tested multiplies down to something extremely small — except for identical twins, who share the same VNTRs because they came from the same fertilised egg. Because VNTRs are passed on half from each parent, closely related people also share more bands than unrelated people do, which is what makes the technique useful for testing relatedness as well as identity.

Defining a VNTR precisely

A VNTR is a short base sequence repeated many times "in tandem" — one copy directly after another — found mainly in the non-coding DNA between genes. The number of repeats at a given locus is variable between individuals: that variation is the entire basis of the technique. Both ideas have to be present in a definition — non-coding and variable in number — or the mark is lost.

Why the probability of a chance match is so low

  • There are many VNTR loci scattered through the genome, and at each one there are many possible numbers of repeats.
  • The probability that two unrelated people match at a single locus is already small; because different loci vary independently of one another, the probability of matching at every locus tested is the product of each individual locus probability.
  • Multiplying several small, independent probabilities together produces an extremely small overall probability — this is why several VNTR loci are tested together, not just one.
  • The one exception is identical twins, who share the same VNTRs because they developed from the same zygote.

Why VNTRs reveal genetic relationships

VNTRs are inherited, half from each parent, exactly like any other locus — so every band in a child's profile should trace back to one parent or the other. Closely related individuals (parent–child, siblings) therefore share more bands than unrelated individuals do, which is the biological basis of paternity testing and relatedness studies (Section 3).

📌

Because it's non-coding: variation in repeat number doesn't change any protein and so isn't acted on by natural selection — which is exactly why so many different repeat numbers can persist in a population instead of being selected out, keeping the technique powerful.

▶ 🚩 Trap — an incomplete VNTR definition

A definition that only says "the number of repeats differs between people," without also naming the sequence as non-coding, is incomplete. Confusing a VNTR with a coding gene sequence is a genuinely common error — a VNTR sits between genes and does not code for a protein at all.

Section 2 · spec content point

🧪 The five-stage technique: from DNA sample to band pattern

Extraction & PCR → digestion → separation → hybridisation → development. Nearly every question on this spec point is a subset of these five stages, so learn the headings and the mechanism behind each one, in order.

📖

What the specification says: "The technique of genetic fingerprinting in analysing DNA fragments that have been cloned by PCR, and its use in determining genetic relationships and in determining the genetic variability within a population." (This section covers the technique itself — Section 3 covers this same content point's two named uses.)

1
2
3

Five steps, like a recipe. First, get a DNA sample and photocopy the interesting bit millions of times (PCR) so there's enough to work with. Second, use molecular scissors to snip either side of the repeat section. Third, sort the snipped pieces by size using an electric current in a gel — short pieces zoom further, long pieces don't get as far. Fourth, split the DNA into single strands and let a labelled "sticky tag" glue itself onto the repeat sequence. Fifth, reveal where all the tags ended up, which gives you a pattern of bands — a genetic fingerprint.

  • PCR = a way of photocopying a small piece of DNA millions of times
  • Restriction endonuclease = a "molecular scissors" enzyme that cuts DNA at a specific spot
  • Gel electrophoresis = sorting DNA pieces by size using electricity
  • Probe = a labelled, sticky-tag piece of DNA that finds and binds its matching sequence

DNA is first extracted from the sample and the VNTR region is copied many times over using PCR, so that even a tiny sample gives enough DNA to work with. Restriction enzymes then cut the DNA at fixed points either side of the repeat region, so the length of each resulting fragment depends only on how many repeats were sitting between the two cuts. Those fragments are sorted by gel electrophoresis: DNA carries an overall negative charge, so it moves through the gel towards the positive electrode, and shorter fragments move further than longer ones in the same amount of time. The DNA is then split into single strands so that labelled probes — pieces of DNA carrying a radioactive or fluorescent tag — can bind to the target sequence by matching up complementary bases. Finally, the pattern is made visible, either by exposing photographic film to the radioactive label or by shining UV light on the fluorescent one, giving a pattern of bands that can be compared between people.

4. HybridisationThe DNA is made single-stranded; labelled probes bind by complementary base pairing / DNA hybridisation.A double-stranded fragment already has its bases paired to each other, so a probe cannot attach until the strands are separated.

DNA is negatively charged, so it runs from the wells towards the anode. Fragment A (fewer repeats, shorter) meets less resistance from the gel matrix and travels further in the same time than Fragment B (more repeats, longer). Comparing this banding pattern across several VNTR loci at once builds an individual's genetic fingerprint.

Why the DNA must be made single-stranded before probing

A probe locates its target by complementary base pairing. A double-stranded fragment already has its bases hydrogen-bonded to each other, so there are no free bases for a probe to bind to. Making the DNA single-stranded exposes the bases, allowing the labelled probe to hybridise to its complementary target sequence.

▶ 🚩 Trap — vague PCR temperature steps

If asked to explain the PCR stage, name all three temperature steps precisely, and say what each one does — not just its name: denaturation ≈95°C (separates the two DNA strands by breaking hydrogen bonds), annealing ≈50–65°C (primers bind to the single strands), extension ≈72°C (DNA polymerase extends new complementary strands). Vague, non-specific answers about "heating and cooling" lose marks here.

🧪

Practical skills link (AT g): there's no required practical numbered against this spec point, but the specification names a transferable skill here: gel electrophoresis can be used to produce "fingerprints" of food dyes. The principle is identical — charged molecules (dye molecules, rather than DNA fragments) are loaded into wells and separated under an electric current according to size/charge. But be careful: if a question specifies protein or dye separation rather than DNA, an answer that defaults to discussing base sequences or VNTRs scores nothing — separation there is by molecular size/charge in general, not by base sequence.

Section 3 · spec content point

👪 Using fingerprints to determine relationships & population diversity

Once you have a band pattern, what do you actually do with it? This section covers the two named uses built into this content point: working out how individuals are related, and working out how genetically diverse a whole population is.

📖

What the specification says: "The technique of genetic fingerprinting in analysing DNA fragments that have been cloned by PCR, and its use in determining genetic relationships and in determining the genetic variability within a population." (Same content point as Section 2 — this half covers the two named uses.)

1
2
3

Because you get half your DNA repeats from your mum and half from your dad, every single band in your fingerprint has to have come from one of them. So if you line up a mum's, a dad's and a child's fingerprints side by side, every one of the child's bands should show up in either the mum's row or the dad's row — that's how you check whether someone really is the parent. Zoom out to a whole population instead of just one family: if lots of individuals all share almost exactly the same bands, that group doesn't have much variety in its DNA — which is a warning sign, a bit like a small gene pool. If everyone's bands look quite different from each other, that's a healthy, varied population.

Because VNTRs are inherited half from each parent, every band that shows up in a child's fingerprint should also show up in either the mother's fingerprint or the father's — that's the basis of paternity testing. If a band appears in a parent but not in the child, that's just the other allele at that locus, the one that wasn't passed on — it doesn't disprove parentage. When comparing gels like this, always talk about specific bands in specific lanes rather than saying the patterns "look similar," since that vague comparison doesn't earn credit even if the right conclusion is reached. The same idea scales up to a whole population: comparing the fingerprints of many individuals shows how many bands they share. A population where individuals share lots of the same bands has low genetic diversity, which is a warning sign — often linked to inbreeding and a reduced ability to cope with disease or a changing environment. A population with more varied banding patterns has greater genetic diversity.

Determining genetic relationships

  • Every band in a child's profile must appear in either the mother's or the father's profile, since VNTRs are inherited half from each parent.
  • A parental band that is not seen in the child is simply the other, non-inherited allele at that locus — it does not challenge parentage.
  • The same logic underpins immigration disputes and wider relatedness studies: closely related individuals share more bands than unrelated individuals.
Dashed lines = a shared band (that locus was inherited). Every child band traces to the mother or the father. The father's row-2 band, not seen in the child, is simply the other allele at that locus — not evidence against parentage.

Determining genetic variability within a population

  • Compare the banding patterns of many individuals sampled from the same population.
  • A population showing more distinct, varied banding patterns has greater genetic diversity at the loci tested.
  • A population where individuals share many of the same bands has lower genetic diversity — a signal of vulnerability, such as reduced ability to withstand disease or environmental change, and often a warning sign of inbreeding.
📌

The interpretation rule AQA rewards: structure every gel-comparison answer around specific, named bands/lanes — e.g. "band X in lane 2 matches band X in lane 4." For probability-based questions ("what's the probability two unrelated people share this pattern?"), write out each independent locus probability separately before multiplying them together — errors usually come from misjudging which probabilities are actually independent, not from the multiplication itself.

▶ 🚩 Trap — vague gel comparisons

A vague "the patterns look similar" loses marks even when the right overall conclusion is reached. Always name the specific band and the specific lane it appears in, on both sides of the comparison.

Section 4 · spec content point

🕵️ Real-world uses: forensic science, medical diagnosis & breeding

Three named fields, each with its own reasoning for why fingerprinting is useful there — "explain" questions want the reasoning, not just the field named.

📖

What the specification says: "The use of genetic fingerprinting in the fields of forensic science, medical diagnosis, animal and plant breeding." Students should be able to: explain the biological principles that underpin genetic fingerprinting techniques; interpret data showing the results of gel electrophoresis to separate DNA fragments; explain why scientists might use genetic fingerprinting in each of these three fields.

1
2
3

Think of a fingerprint gel like a detective's evidence board. In forensics, if a suspect's bands match a crime-scene sample at every single spot checked, that's strong evidence they were there — but it isn't proof they committed a crime, and one mismatched band clears them completely. In hospitals, doctors use the same idea to check whether a donated organ is a good genetic match for the person receiving it — a closer match means the body is less likely to reject it. And on farms or in zoos breeding endangered animals, keepers use fingerprinting like a family tree check, to avoid pairing up animals that are too closely related to each other.

In forensic science, DNA from a crime scene — blood, semen, a hair root, saliva — is fingerprinted and compared against a suspect's own sample. A full band match across many independent VNTR loci is strong evidence that the suspect's DNA is present at the scene, though it isn't proof of guilt on its own, while a mismatch at even one locus rules that suspect out. In medical diagnosis, fingerprinting can spot disease-linked alleles or an abnormal number of repeats directly from a patient's DNA, and it's also used to check tissue compatibility before an organ transplant, since a closer genetic match between donor and recipient lowers the risk of rejection. In breeding, fingerprinting confirms pedigree and parentage, and it shows how many bands are shared across a population — many shared bands means low genetic diversity, a warning sign for inbreeding — so breeders and conservationists use it to pair up less closely related individuals and protect genetic diversity, which matters both for endangered-species conservation and for pedigree crops and livestock.

Interpreting a forensic gel — worked reasoning

Method: compare the position of every band between lanes, locus by locus. Suppose a crime-scene lane matches Suspect A at all four tested loci, but Suspect B's lane shows a mismatched, extra band not present in the crime-scene sample. Suspect A's full match across four independent loci is strong evidence their DNA is present at the scene; Suspect B is excluded, since one unmatched band is enough to rule out a contributor. Caution should always be added: a partial match could indicate a relative rather than the suspect themselves, the sample may be contaminated or degraded, and too few loci tested weakens the strength of any match.

🔎

Two named skills to keep separate: "interpret data" questions want named-band, named-lane reasoning applied to a gel you're shown; "explain why scientists use fingerprinting in [field]" questions want the underlying reasoning for that field specifically — simply naming the field without reasons earns little credit.

▶ 🚩 Trap — treating a match as proof of guilt

A full band match shows a suspect's DNA is likely present at a scene — it is not proof that the suspect committed the crime. Also never write "compare mRNA/amino acid sequence" as if it were equivalent to genetic fingerprinting: the technique compares DNA fragment length directly, not mRNA or protein sequence.

▶ Where this point sits in difficulty terms, and the hardest transferable skill

Content-point profile: memorisation load is medium–high (five ordered stages, each with its own mechanism), application depth is high (data interpretation across forensic, medical and breeding contexts), and misconception frequency is high. This is assessed only within the A2/A-level tier — none of it is AS content. The role of PCR in amplifying DNA is usually recalled correctly, but the temperature-cycling steps are described with variable precision, and VNTR explanations are inconsistently linked to why repeat number varies between individuals and why that makes fingerprinting useful. Practical application scenarios are usually approached well when band patterns are compared methodically, but marks are lost when comparisons stay vague rather than naming specific bands and lanes. The single hardest transferable skill is resisting that vague, whole-pattern "these look similar" judgement in favour of a systematic, named comparison — a correct final conclusion earns little credit without band-by-band justification. For probability questions, write each independent locus probability out separately before multiplying, since errors usually come from misidentifying which probabilities are genuinely independent, not from the arithmetic itself.

▶ Command words used on this spec point

Explain dominates the applications section — "explain why scientists use fingerprinting in forensic science/medical diagnosis/breeding" wants the reasoning, not just the named field. Describe ("describe the technique of genetic fingerprinting") wants the five stages in order, not just a list of named tools. Interpret is the spec's own explicit skill — "interpret data showing the results of gel electrophoresis" wants named-band, named-lane reasoning. Compare is applied directly to comparing band patterns between lanes/individuals to reach a match, exclusion or relatedness conclusion.

Section 6 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

Genetic fingerprinting shows up as a supporting paragraph across several essay titles — the trick each time is closing the paragraph with a sentence that ties the mechanism back to the specific theme of the title, rather than repeating a generic summary of the technique.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around enzyme function

Restriction endonucleases cut DNA at fixed recognition sites either side of a VNTR, producing fragments whose length depends only on the number of repeats between the two cuts, while DNA polymerase, used during PCR, copies the target region millions of times over from even a tiny starting sample. This shows enzymes performing two very different jobs within the same technique — one cutting with precision at a specific sequence, the other synthesising new DNA rapidly and repeatedly — both essential before a band pattern can ever be produced.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around ions and charge

DNA fragments carry an overall negative charge because of their phosphate groups, so under an applied electric current they migrate towards the anode, with shorter fragments travelling further than longer ones through the gel matrix in the same time. This is a clear example of an electrical property of a molecule — its charge — being exploited directly as a separation tool, turning a physical property of DNA into the basis of a fingerprint pattern that can be compared between individuals.

▶ Closing paragraph — "The importance of chromosomes in living organisms"

An organism's chromosomes contain many variable number tandem repeats in the non-coding DNA between genes, and the probability of two unrelated individuals sharing the same VNTR pattern at several loci is extremely low. DNA is extracted and amplified by PCR, cut either side of the VNTRs by restriction endonucleases, and the fragments separated by gel electrophoresis before being probed and revealed by autoradiography or UV light. Because VNTRs are inherited half from each parent, a child's bands should all appear in one parent or the other — the basis of paternity testing and forensic identification. It is precisely the small, non-coding variation between individuals' chromosomes, arising from the whole process of replication and inheritance, that makes genetic fingerprinting possible — turning an abstract idea, that chromosomes carry variation, into a concrete, real-world identification technology.

Whichever theme this content supports, treat it as the "mechanism" paragraph: state the process precisely — naming the enzyme, the charge, or the inheritance pattern depending on what the title actually asks about — then add one sentence explicitly linking it back to that theme.

Memory hook — cut, copy, sort, stick, see. Restriction endonucleases cut either side of the VNTR; PCR copies the region up to a usable amount; gel electrophoresis sorts the fragments by size (negative charge → anode, small → far); a labelled probe sticks to its complementary target once the DNA is single-stranded; and autoradiography/UV lets you see the resulting band pattern — the individual's genetic fingerprint.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · VNTRs & why the technique works

  • VNTR = short sequence repeated many times, in non-coding DNA — say both words or lose the mark.
  • Number of repeats at a locus varies between individuals — that's the whole basis of the technique.
  • Probability of matching at every locus tested = product of many independent small probabilities → extremely low (except identical twins).
  • VNTRs inherited half from each parent → basis of paternity testing and relatedness.

2 · The five-stage technique

  • Extraction & PCR → Digestion (restriction endonucleases) → Separation (gel electrophoresis) → Hybridisation (labelled probes) → Development (autoradiography/UV).
  • DNA is negatively charged (phosphate groups) → moves to the anode; shorter fragments travel further.
  • DNA must be made single-stranded before probes bind — double-stranded DNA already has its bases paired.
  • PCR temps: denaturation ≈95°C, annealing ≈50–65°C, extension ≈72°C — name all three with what each does.

3 · Relationships & population diversity

  • Every band in a child's profile must appear in the mother or the father.
  • A parental band absent from the child = the other, non-inherited allele — not an error.
  • Always name specific bands/lanes when comparing gels — never just "patterns look similar."
  • More varied banding across a population = greater genetic diversity; more shared bands = lower diversity, warns of inbreeding.

4 · Forensic, medical & breeding uses

  • Forensic: full band match across many loci = strong evidence of presence at scene (not proof of guilt); one mismatched locus excludes a suspect.
  • Medical: identifies disease alleles/abnormal repeat numbers; checks donor–recipient tissue compatibility before transplant.
  • Breeding: verifies pedigree/parentage; low band diversity flags inbreeding risk — used in conservation and pedigree livestock/crops.
  • Never say "compare mRNA/amino acid sequence" — the technique compares DNA fragment length directly.

VNTRs & why the technique works

❓ Define what is meant by a VNTR, and explain why this exact definition matters for full marks.
A VNTR is a short sequence of bases repeated many times in tandem in non-coding DNA between genes; the number of repeats at a given locus varies between individuals. Both ideas — non-coding, and variable in number — must be stated; a definition giving only "the number of repeats differs" is incomplete, and confusing a VNTR with a coding gene sequence is a recorded common error.
❓ Two unrelated individuals are tested at six independent VNTR loci. Explain why the probability that they share the same pattern at all six loci is extremely low.
At each locus there are many possible numbers of repeats, so the probability of a match at any one locus is already low. Because the loci are independent of one another, the overall probability of matching at all six is the product of each individual locus probability, making a coincidental match extremely unlikely — identical twins are the exception, since they share the same VNTRs.
❓ Explain why VNTRs, rather than coding gene sequences, form the basis of genetic fingerprinting.
VNTRs lie in non-coding DNA between genes, so variation in repeat number does not affect the phenotype and is not removed by natural selection, allowing many different repeat numbers to persist within a population. A coding sequence would be far more constrained by selection and so less variable between individuals, making it a poor basis for identification.

The five-stage technique

❓ Describe the technique of genetic fingerprinting, from obtaining a DNA sample through to producing a band pattern.
DNA is extracted from the sample and the VNTR region is amplified by PCR. Restriction endonucleases cut the DNA at fixed sites either side of the VNTRs, producing fragments whose length depends on the number of repeats present. The fragments are separated by gel electrophoresis: DNA is negatively charged, so it moves towards the anode, with shorter fragments travelling further. The DNA is made single-stranded, and labelled probes bind to the target fragments by complementary base pairing (DNA hybridisation). The resulting pattern is revealed by autoradiography (radioactive label) or under UV light (fluorescent label), producing a band pattern that can be compared between individuals.
❓ A student's answer states that DNA fragments are separated "because the fragments have different charges." Rewrite this using precise, credited wording.
All DNA fragments carry the same overall negative charge, from their phosphate groups; they are separated by size, not charge. Shorter fragments experience less resistance from the gel matrix and so travel further towards the anode, in the same time, than longer fragments.
❓ Explain why the DNA is made single-stranded before labelled probes are added, and why hybridisation would fail without this step.
A probe locates its target sequence by complementary base pairing. A double-stranded fragment already has its bases paired to each other, so no free bases are available for a probe to bind to. Making the DNA single-stranded exposes the bases, allowing the labelled probe to hybridise to the complementary target sequence.

Relationships & population diversity

❓ In a paternity gel, the child shows a band at a position seen in the father's lane but not the mother's, plus a band seen in the mother's lane only. The father's lane also shows one extra band not seen in the child. Explain what this pattern shows.
The child has inherited one band from the father and one from the mother, consistent with parentage, since every band in the child's profile traces back to one parent or the other. The extra band present in the father's lane but not the child's is simply the other allele at that locus, which was not passed on — it does not challenge parentage.
❓ Explain how genetic fingerprinting can be used to assess the genetic diversity of a population of an endangered species.
Banding patterns from many individuals in the population are compared. A population showing many distinct, varied bands has high genetic diversity, whereas a population where most individuals share the same bands has low genetic diversity, indicating inbreeding and a reduced ability to withstand disease or environmental change.
❓ State the interpretation rule that should structure every gel-comparison answer, and explain why a vague answer loses marks.
Every comparison should be structured around specific, named bands and lanes — for example, stating that band X in lane 2 matches band X in lane 4 — because a general statement such as "the patterns look similar" does not demonstrate that a genuine comparison has been carried out, even where the final conclusion reached happens to be correct.

Forensic, medical & breeding uses

❓ Crime-scene DNA is compared with DNA from two suspects. Suspect A's profile matches the crime-scene sample at every locus tested; Suspect B's profile shows one unmatched band. Explain what conclusions can be drawn, and state one limitation of the technique.
A full match across many independent loci for Suspect A is strong evidence that their DNA is present at the scene, though this alone does not prove guilt. Suspect B is excluded, since a mismatch at even one locus rules out a contributor. A limitation is that the sample may have been contaminated or degraded, which could affect the reliability of the result.
❓ Explain why genetic fingerprinting is carried out before an organ transplant takes place.
The donor's and recipient's genetic fingerprints are compared to check tissue compatibility. A closer match between donor and recipient lowers the risk of the recipient's immune system rejecting the transplanted tissue.
❓ Explain how genetic fingerprinting helps conservationists reduce inbreeding in an endangered species breeding programme.
Fingerprinting reveals how closely related individuals are, based on how many bands they share. Breeders select pairs of individuals that are less closely related, shown by fewer shared bands, to breed together, maintaining genetic diversity within the population and reducing the risks associated with inbreeding.
