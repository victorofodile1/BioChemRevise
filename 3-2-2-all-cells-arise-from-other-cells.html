All Cells Arise From Other Cells

How a eukaryotic cell divides by mitosis, why that division has to stay tightly controlled, how a prokaryotic cell divides by the simpler process of binary fission, and how the root-tip squash practical puts a number on how fast a tissue is dividing.

⚠️

The single rule that costs the most marks on this spec point: in mitosis it is always sister chromatids (identical copies) that separate — never write "homologous chromosomes separate," since that's meiosis-only vocabulary. A close second: interphase is not "a stage of mitosis" — it's a stage of the wider cell cycle that happens before mitosis begins.

Section 1 · spec content point

🌱 Not every cell keeps the ability to divide

This one-sentence spec point is easy to skim past, but it's the reason a root tip has far more actively dividing cells than a leaf, and it sets up why the mitotic index (Section 9) varies so much between tissues.

📖

What the specification says: "Within multicellular organisms, not all cells retain the ability to divide."

1
2
3

Imagine a factory where most workers get trained for one very specific job and then stay on that job forever — they never go back to "general training." But a small number of workers stay in the training pool permanently, ready to become whatever kind of worker is needed next, including making brand-new workers. Most cells in your body are like the specialised workers — once they've become a nerve cell or a red blood cell, they don't divide again. Only a few special groups of cells stay in the "training pool" and keep dividing.

  • Specialised, non-dividing cell = like the trained-for-one-job worker
  • Cell that keeps dividing = like a worker still in the training pool

As cells become more specialised — a process called differentiation — most permanently lose the ability to divide again. A mature red blood cell, most neurones, and mature xylem vessels never divide once they've reached their final, specialised form. Only certain cells or tissues keep dividing throughout an organism's life: in plants this is the meristem (found at root tips and shoot tips), and in animals it's certain stem-cell populations, such as those in bone marrow or the base of the skin and gut lining.

Once a cell has fully differentiated into a specialised type — a mature red blood cell, most neurones, xylem vessels — it typically loses the ability to divide again permanently. The cells that do retain that ability are concentrated in specific dividing tissues:

  • Meristems in plants — undifferentiated, permanently dividing tissue found at root tips and shoot tips. This is exactly the tissue Required Practical 2 (Section 9) samples, because it's where mitosis is most concentrated and easiest to catch in the act.
  • Stem cells in animals — e.g. in bone marrow (producing blood cells), and in the basal layers of skin and gut epithelium (continually replacing worn-out cells).

This distinction is why a mitotic index (the proportion of cells actively dividing in a sample) differs so much between tissues: a sample taken from the root-tip meristem will have a far higher mitotic index than one taken from a fully differentiated region further up the root.

📌

Don't confuse "not all cells retain the ability to divide" with an organism's ability to reproduce sexually — that relies on a completely separate, specialised division process (meiosis, in the gonads only) covered elsewhere on the spec, not on this general mitotic-division rule.

Section 2 · spec content point

🔁 The cell cycle & where DNA replication happens

The cycle has two very unequal chunks: a long interphase (growth, organelle increase, and — critically — DNA replication) and a much shorter division phase. Reading a DNA-content-against-time graph correctly depends entirely on knowing which chunk is which.

📖

What the specification says: "Eukaryotic cells that do retain the ability to divide show a cell cycle. DNA replication occurs during the interphase of the cell cycle. Mitosis is the part of the cell cycle in which a eukaryotic cell divides to produce two daughter cells, each with the identical copies of DNA produced by the parent cell during DNA replication."

1
2
3

Think of the cell cycle like preparing to split a stack of paperwork between two people fairly. First you spend a long time photocopying every page so there are two full copies of everything (this is like interphase — the cell is growing and copying its DNA). Only once every page is copied do you actually split the two piles apart and hand one to each person — that quick splitting-up bit is mitosis.

The cell cycle is a repeating loop with two very different-sized parts. Interphase is the long part: the cell grows, makes more organelles, and — crucially — copies (replicates) its DNA. Mitosis is the short part: the cell actually splits its copied DNA between two new cells, each getting an identical set. Interphase itself has three named sub-stages: G1 (growth), S (DNA replication happens here specifically), and G2 (further growth and final checks before dividing).

Not every cell in a multicellular organism keeps the ability to divide (Section 1) — but those that do go through a repeating cell cycle. The cycle has two unequal chunks: a long interphase (growth, organelle increase, and DNA replication), then the much shorter division phase, mitosis (M-phase), usually followed straight away by cytokinesis (Section 4). Interphase is often split into G1 (growth), S (the sub-stage where DNA replicates), and G2 (further growth and checks before division) — the spec's own wording just says "interphase," but knowing the S-phase sub-stage matters for reading a DNA-content graph.

Interphase (G1, S, G2) is by far the longest part of the cycle — flat or rising. Only the actual division of the cell (telophase/cytokinesis) causes DNA content per cell to fall.

Reading a DNA-content-against-time graph

  • A flat, low section of the graph is interphase before S phase — DNA content stays constant.
  • A rising section is S phase — DNA is actively replicating.
  • A flat, high (doubled) section covers G2, prophase, metaphase and anaphase — every chromosome has already been duplicated into 2 sister chromatids, so the total DNA content per cell stays at its doubled value right up until division actually happens.
  • A sudden fall in DNA content means the cell has physically finished dividing — that's telophase/cytokinesis, because DNA has now been shared out between two daughter cells.
▶ 🚩 Trap — naming the falling section

Never name "prophase," "metaphase," or "anaphase" alone for the falling section of a DNA-content graph — DNA content doesn't drop until the cell physically divides. The earlier stages just redistribute DNA that's already been copied; they don't reduce the amount per cell. Only telophase/cytokinesis produces the actual fall.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "chromosomes replicate" isn't precise enough

For a mark that asks what happens to cause doubled DNA content, the precise term needed is that the DNA replicates — "chromosomes replicate" on its own is not accepted as precise enough. Doubled DNA content is true from late interphase (after S phase) all the way through to anaphase, because by then each chromosome consists of 2 sister chromatids; original (un-doubled) DNA content means either replication hasn't happened yet (early interphase/G1), or the cell has just divided and started the next interphase.

▶ Finding the length of the cell cycle from a graph

Measure the time between two successive starts (or two successive ends) of DNA replication on the graph — not the gap between two random points, and not just the length of one named stage. This works because DNA replication happens exactly once per cell cycle, so the shape of the curve repeats with a period equal to the cycle length.

Section 3 · spec content point

🧫 The five stages of mitosis & the role of spindle fibres

IPMAT — Interphase, Prophase, Metaphase, Anaphase, Telophase — always in that order. Every mark on this page is stage-name plus correctly attached event: the biology has to be pinned to the right label, or it scores nothing even if it's true.

📖

What the specification says: "The behaviour of chromosomes during interphase, prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase of mitosis. The role of spindle fibres attached to centromeres in the separation of chromatids."

1
2
3

Picture packing up to move to two new houses. First you pack every box neatly and label it (prophase — chromosomes condense so they're easy to handle). Then every box gets lined up in the hallway in one straight row so nothing gets mixed up (metaphase). Then each labelled pair of matching boxes splits into two single boxes, and one of each pair heads to house A while the other heads to house B (anaphase). Finally, house A and house B both get properly unpacked and settled (telophase).

Mitosis has four working stages after interphase: prophase (the DNA, already copied in interphase, coils up tightly into visible chromosomes, and the membrane around the nucleus breaks down), metaphase (the chromosomes line up along the middle of the cell), anaphase (the two identical halves of each chromosome — called sister chromatids — get pulled apart to opposite ends of the cell), and telophase (a new membrane forms around each new set, and the DNA relaxes again). Throughout, thread-like spindle fibres attach to a point on each chromosome called the centromere, and it's these fibres doing the pulling in anaphase.

⚠️

The rule that costs the most marks: a "describe the stages of mitosis" answer is marked as five stage-by-stage pairs — getting a stage name wrong caps that whole part of the answer (errors aren't carried forward within a stage's own description). Name the stage right first, then describe it.

Naming a stage from an image, and explaining it

Identify anaphase from chromatids visibly being pulled apart toward opposite poles. To explain it fully: identical (sister) chromatids separate, and move to opposite poles. Any reference to the chromatids being identical is accepted — e.g. "same DNA" — but "homologous chromosomes separate" is never credited here; homologous pairs separate only in meiosis I, a different topic entirely.

The effect of a drug that disrupts the spindle

These questions are mark-paired: a cause must be linked explicitly to its consequence, never left to stand alone.

  • No spindle fibres form or attach to centromeres → chromosomes can't line up at the equator → no metaphase.
  • No spindle fibres to pull chromatids apart → no anaphase (no separation of chromatids).
  • Fuller version: mitosis can't complete, so no new cells are added; if separation fails only partially, chromatids can end up unevenly distributed — non-disjunction — with one daughter cell gaining extra chromosomes and the other losing them (never a cell left with zero chromosomes).
▶ Try one yourself — spindle-poison scenario

Scenario: a chemical prevents spindle fibres from ever attaching to centromeres. Predict the effect on a dividing cell.

Reasoning: without spindle fibres attached to centromeres, chromosomes cannot be pulled to line up at the equator, so the cell cannot progress into metaphase. Since anaphase depends on spindle fibres shortening to separate sister chromatids, that step also cannot occur — the cell cannot complete mitosis, so no new cells are produced, and the cell is likely to remain arrested indefinitely.

Section 4 · spec content point

✂️ Cytokinesis completes cell division

Mitosis (Section 3) divides the nucleus; a separate step, cytokinesis, then physically splits the rest of the cell. It's a short spec point, but it's the actual moment "one cell" becomes "two cells."

📖

What the specification says: "Division of the cytoplasm (cytokinesis) usually occurs, producing two new cells."

1
2
3

Splitting a cell in two is a bit like splitting a room in a house. If the house is flexible (an animal cell, with a bendy outer membrane), you can just pinch the middle of the room until it's two separate rooms. If the house has rigid brick walls already built (a plant cell, with a stiff cell wall), pinching won't work — instead you have to build a brand-new wall straight down the middle.

Once mitosis has finished sorting the chromosomes into two identical sets, cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm itself, physically producing two separate daughter cells. Animal cells do this by the cell surface membrane pinching inwards around the middle (a cleavage furrow) until it seals off into two cells. Plant cells can't pinch, because they're surrounded by a rigid cell wall — instead, a new dividing wall (a cell plate) builds outward from the centre of the cell until it reaches the existing walls, splitting the cell in two.

Cytokinesis is a distinct step from mitosis itself — mitosis (Section 3) is specifically about the nucleus dividing and the chromosomes being sorted; cytokinesis is the cytoplasm then physically dividing to complete the process, "usually" producing two new cells.

Cell typeMechanism of cytokinesis
Animal cellThe cell surface membrane constricts inward around the equator (a cleavage furrow) until the cytoplasm is pinched into two separate cells.
Plant cellA rigid cell wall prevents the membrane from simply constricting, so a new cell plate forms across the centre of the cell and grows outward until it fuses with the existing cell wall, dividing the cell in two.

The spec's own wording says cytokinesis "usually" occurs — worth noticing, since it signals that mitosis (nuclear division) and cytokinesis (cytoplasmic division) aren't strictly the same event, and in some specialised or exceptional cases nuclear division can occur without an immediate, complete cytoplasmic split.

📌

If asked to compare mitosis in plant and animal cells, cytokinesis (not the chromosome behaviour, which is identical) is where the genuine structural difference lies — always name the cell plate for plants and the cleavage furrow/membrane constriction for animals.

Section 5 · spec content point

🚧 Where mitosis ends and meiosis begins

This spec point is really a boundary marker — a signal that everything on this page is about mitosis only, and that a genuinely different division process (meiosis) is examined separately. Knowing exactly where that line sits is worth more marks than it looks.

📖

What the specification says: "Meiosis is covered in section 3.4.3."

1
2
3

Think of mitosis as a photocopier making an exact duplicate of one page — you end up with two identical copies. A totally different process, meiosis, is more like cutting a deck of two different card sets in half and shuffling one half from each together — the four resulting hands are all different from each other and from the original decks. Mitosis and meiosis are simply not the same machine, and the vocabulary for one doesn't transfer to the other.

Mitosis produces two daughter cells that are genetically identical to the parent cell and to each other — it's used for growth, repair, and asexual reproduction. Meiosis (a separate topic) instead produces four genetically different cells with half the chromosome number, and is used specifically to make gametes for sexual reproduction. The vocabulary doesn't cross over: in mitosis, sister chromatids separate; only in meiosis do homologous chromosomes separate (and only in its first division).

Mitosis produces two daughter cells that are diploid and genetically identical to the parent cell — appropriate for growth, tissue repair, and asexual reproduction. Meiosis, covered fully elsewhere on the specification, instead produces four haploid daughter cells that are genetically different from each other and from the parent cell — appropriate specifically for producing gametes ahead of sexual reproduction. The two processes are never interchangeable, and neither is their vocabulary.

▶ 🚩 Trap — the single most common wrong phrase on this whole spec point

"Homologous chromosomes separate" is meiosis-only vocabulary. Writing it anywhere on a mitosis question — anaphase descriptions, non-disjunction questions, spindle-drug questions — is not credited, however confidently it's written. The correct term for mitosis, every time, is that sister (identical) chromatids separate.

▶ A subtler trap examiners have flagged: where mutations "arise"

A mutation present in every body cell of an organism did not "arise during meiosis" — it arose in the zygote (or in a gamete before fertilisation) and was then propagated to every cell of the body by repeated rounds of mitosis, not meiosis. This distinction — mitosis spreads a mutation through a body, meiosis is what creates new gametes — is a genuine source of lost marks precisely because both processes involve chromosomes and cell division.

Section 6 · spec content point

🎗️ Mitosis, cancer & treatment

Mitosis has to stay tightly controlled — when it doesn't, the result is a tumour. This is also where the topic connects to evaluating real experimental data, which is examined very differently from the recall-based stages content.

📖

What the specification says: "Mitosis is a controlled process. Uncontrolled cell division can lead to the formation of tumours and of cancers. Many cancer treatments are directed at controlling the rate of cell division."

1
2
3

Imagine a factory that's supposed to make exactly the number of products it's told to, then stop. Cancer is like that factory's "stop" switch breaking — it just keeps producing more and more, faster than it should, piling up into an uncontrolled heap (a tumour). A cancer treatment is like a wrench thrown into the machine specifically to jam production — it slows down all fast-running machines, but the factories that were already running fast (cancer cells) get hit hardest.

Normally, mitosis only happens when and where it's needed, under tight control. Cancer happens when that control breaks down and cells divide uncontrollably, building up into a mass of abnormal cells — a tumour. Because cancer cells divide much faster than most healthy cells, drugs that interfere with cell division affect the fast-dividing cancer cells far more than the slower-dividing healthy ones — that's the whole logic behind why these drugs work as treatments, and it's also why healthy dividing tissue (hair follicles, gut lining) still suffers side effects.

Why treatments target dividing cells

Cancer cells divide more rapidly and uncontrollably than healthy cells — put the other way round, healthy cells divide comparatively slowly. A drug that interferes with the cell cycle therefore affects cancer cells disproportionately, while healthy, slower-dividing cells are relatively spared.

How a faster cell cycle produces a tumour

A shortened interphase, or DNA replication starting earlier or proceeding faster, drives a faster overall cell cycle — resulting in a mass of abnormal, excessive cells: a tumour.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "uncontrolled growth" isn't precise enough

The mark scheme specifically wants the phrase "uncontrolled cell division," not the vaguer "uncontrolled growth." Growth and cell division aren't the same idea to an examiner — always name division specifically.

Evaluating a cancer drug from data

A balanced answer is required — weigh both sides explicitly, don't just list positives.

For the drugAgainst / limitations
Little further increase in effect above a threshold concentration; most cells stop dividing; a greater effect on rapidly dividing (cancer) cells than on slower-dividing healthy cells; may promote programmed cell death.Some cancer cells are still dividing even at the concentrations tested, so the cancer may continue to grow or spread; the best concentration may lie between those actually tested, so more trials are needed; healthy (also dividing) cells are damaged too, causing side effects; higher concentrations may be more harmful or more expensive.
▶ 🚩 Trap — say "in culture," not "not tested on humans"

When a described study was carried out on cells grown in culture, the precise criticism the mark scheme wants is that the drug was tested "in culture" — not in a whole organism — so its effect on a real patient is still unknown. Writing "not tested on humans" or "tested on animals" instead of naming "in culture" specifically will not be credited, even though it's pointing at a similar idea.

🔗

The hardest application skill on this whole spec point is applying "mitosis is controlled" consistently across three different contexts: a spindle-poison's mechanism (Section 3), evaluating a cancer drug from data (this section), and comparing mitosis with binary fission's own, plasmid-based control system (Section 7). Whenever a question links mitosis to cancer, write the full chain explicitly: named stage/mechanism affected → effect on cell division → effect on tumour growth/spread. Never leave the middle link ("cell division") implied.

Section 7 · spec content point

🦠 Binary fission in prokaryotic cells

No nucleus, no spindle, no mitosis vocabulary at all — prokaryotic cells divide by a much simpler three-step process built around a single circular DNA molecule and its plasmids.

📖

What the specification says: "Binary fission in prokaryotic cells involves: replication of the circular DNA and of plasmids; division of the cytoplasm to produce two daughter cells, each with a single copy of the circular DNA and a variable number of copies of plasmids."

1
2
3

A bacterium splitting in two is much simpler than a human cell dividing — there's no complicated packing process. It's more like photocopying a single circular instruction sheet (plus a few spare copies of a smaller note), then just splitting the room in half so each half gets one full sheet and some of the spare notes.

Prokaryotic cells don't have a nucleus or spindle fibres, so they can't divide by mitosis. Instead they use binary fission: the single loop of circular DNA (the bacterial chromosome) is copied, along with any small circular loops of extra DNA called plasmids. The cytoplasm then divides, producing two daughter cells — each gets one copy of the circular DNA, but the number of plasmid copies each daughter ends up with can vary.

  1. Replication of the (single) circular DNA molecule and of any plasmids.
  2. Division of the cytoplasm, as a new septum/cell wall grows inward across the cell.
  3. Outcome: two daughter cells, each with one copy of the circular DNA and a variable number of plasmid copies.
No spindle, no nucleus — the circular DNA and plasmids simply replicate, the cytoplasm divides, and two daughter cells result. Note the two finished daughters here have different plasmid counts (2 and 1): the spec's own wording is "a variable number of copies of plasmids," unlike the fixed chromosome count in mitosis.

Section 8 · spec content point

🧪 Viruses and why they don't divide

Viruses sit entirely outside the "cells arise from other cells" rule — the reason is built into the spec's own wording, and it's a clean, testable distinction.

📖

What the specification says: "Being non-living, viruses do not undergo cell division. Following injection of their nucleic acid, the infected host cell replicates the virus particles."

1
2
3

A virus is a bit like a USB stick with a set of building instructions on it, but no factory of its own to build anything with. On its own it can't do anything. It has to plug into someone else's factory (a living cell) and use that factory's machines and workers to run off thousands of copies of itself.

Viruses are non-living — they have no cell structure, no cytoplasm, no way to carry out life processes on their own — so they cannot divide by mitosis or binary fission, since both of those need a living cell to work. Instead, a virus injects its nucleic acid into a host cell, and it's the host cell's own ribosomes, enzymes and building blocks that get hijacked to build brand-new virus particles.

Viruses are non-living, or acellular — they have no cell structure and cannot independently carry out the processes of life, so they cannot divide by either mitosis or binary fission. Instead, a virus attaches to a specific receptor on a host cell's surface and injects its nucleic acid into that cell. The infected host cell is then hijacked: its own ribosomes, enzymes and nucleotides are used to replicate the viral nucleic acid and synthesise viral proteins, which then assemble into new virus particles and are released.

📌

The exact phrase worth reusing here is "the infected host cell replicates the virus particles" — the virus itself never divides; the host cell's own machinery does the work.

▶ 🚩 Trap — don't blur viral replication with binary fission or mitosis

Cytokinesis/telophase language ("the membrane pinching") sometimes gets muddled with viral replication or with binary fission in exam answers. Keep the three processes entirely separate: mitosis (eukaryotic, spindle-based), binary fission (prokaryotic, no spindle, self-replicating), and viral replication (no division at all — a hijacked host cell manufactures new particles).

Section 9 · spec content point · Required Practical 2

🔬 RP2 — the root-tip squash & mitotic index

This practical is tied directly to this spec point: prepare a stained squash of root-tip cells, use it to identify the stages of mitosis, and turn what you see into a number — the mitotic index.

📖

What the specification says: "Required practical 2: Preparation of stained squashes of cells from plant root tips; set-up and use of an optical microscope to identify the stages of mitosis in these stained squashes and calculation of a mitotic index."

1
2
3

Imagine squashing a tiny bit of root tip flat between two pieces of glass so you can see every cell individually under a microscope, after dyeing all the chromosomes a dark colour so they stand out. Then you just count: out of everyone you can see, how many are "mid-split" (dividing) right now? That fraction tells you how busy that patch of the plant is at making new cells.

A small piece of actively-growing root tip (the meristem) is softened in warm dilute acid to separate the cells, stained so the chromosomes show up clearly, then squashed flat under a coverslip into a single layer so nothing overlaps. Under the microscope, each cell is scored as either "in mitosis" (visible condensed chromosomes) or "in interphase" (no visible chromosomes). The mitotic index is simply the fraction of cells actively dividing.

Method: producing a stained root-tip squash

  1. Cut a small piece (1–2 mm) of root containing the meristem — the actively dividing tissue at the root tip — from a growing root (e.g. garlic or onion).
  2. Soften the tissue in warm dilute hydrochloric acid — this partially digests the middle lamella / breaks down the cell walls, separating the cells so individual ones can be distinguished.
  3. Stain with a DNA-specific stain (e.g. toluidine blue or acetic orcein) so condensed chromosomes are clearly visible.
  4. Squash the stained tip firmly under a coverslip to spread the tissue into a single cell layer — overlapping layers can't be scored reliably, and light needs to pass through for viewing.
  5. Examine under an optical microscope, low power first then high power, working systematically across multiple fields of view, identifying which stage of mitosis (or interphase) each cell is in from chromosome appearance.

Calculating the mitotic index

🧮

Mitotic index = number of cells with visible chromosomes (i.e. cells actively dividing) ÷ total number of cells counted — expressed as a decimal, percentage, or ratio, depending what the question asks for.

▶ 🚩 Trap — decimal places and showing your working

The correct value given to the wrong number of decimal places only scores partial credit — precision matters. Always show full intermediate working (cells dividing, total cells, then the division) so that even a numerically-wrong final answer can still pick up method marks. Check exactly what format the question wants: a decimal, a percentage, or a ratio.

Improving the reliability of the count

These are marked strictly in cause-and-reason pairs — a suggestion without its reason scores nothing.

  • Examine many different fields of view across the slide → so the sample is representative of the whole root tip, not one region.
  • Repeat the count and calculate a mean → so anomalous, one-off counts are smoothed out.
  • Standardise how part-cells at a field's edge are treated → so the same counting rule is applied consistently, not biased by where the boundary happens to fall.

Interpreting a higher mitotic index

A higher mitotic index means a greater proportion of cells are actively dividing in that sample. It's used to compare different regions of a root (the meristem has by far the highest index), and — connecting back to Section 6 — the same technique applied to a biopsy sample can flag rapidly dividing tumour tissue.

Section 10 · spec content point · Required Practical 2

📏 Calculating actual cell size from a microscope image

The same root-tip slide also produces a measurement question: given how big a cell looks under the microscope, and at what magnification, work out how big it actually is.

📖

What the specification says: "Students should measure the apparent size of cells in the root tip and calculate their actual size using the formula: actual size = size of image ÷ magnification."

1
2
3

If a photo of an ant has been zoomed in 10 times and the ant looks 50 mm long in the photo, the real ant is only 5 mm long — you just undo the zoom by dividing. Cell-size questions work exactly the same way: measure how big the cell looks, find out how much it's been magnified by, then divide to get the real size.

What you measure on a microscope image or drawing is the apparent (image) size — not the cell's real size, because the microscope has magnified it. To find the actual size, divide the image size by the magnification. The tricky part is almost always units: the image size and the answer often need converting into different units (commonly µm for a cell-sized structure) before or after the division.

🧮

Actual size = size of image ÷ magnification. Always keep the units of image size and magnification consistent before dividing, and convert the final answer into a sensible unit (typically µm) for a cell-sized structure.

▶ Worked example

A cell measured on a micrograph is 45 mm long. The micrograph is captioned ×3000 magnification. Find the actual length of the cell in µm.

Actual size = image size ÷ magnification = 45 mm ÷ 3000 = 0.015 mm. Converting to µm (×1000): 15 µm.

▶ 🚩 Trap — mixing units before dividing

The single most common error here is dividing before converting units, or forgetting to convert the final answer into a sensible unit for a cell (µm, not mm or m). Convert first, divide second, and always sanity-check that a final cell size in the tens-of-µm range is biologically plausible — an answer in mm or in thousands of µm for a single plant cell is almost certainly a units error.

The biology of mitosis is genuinely learnable — most of the difficulty on this spec point comes from wording precision rather than conceptual difficulty. These are the slips that come up again and again.

▶ Where the real difficulty sits at AS vs A2

The five stages and their basic descriptions are straightforward recall, and most AS-level questions (naming a stage from an image, basic spindle-disruption effects, reliability-of-counting reasoning for RP2) are short items with a clear, learnable mark scheme. The difficulty rises noticeably later: the full "describe the stages" answer is capped by a single misidentified stage name; the spindle-poison mechanism must distinguish "no metaphase" from "no anaphase" as two separately mark-paired outcomes; cancer-drug evaluation demands genuine two-sided balance with the "against" side deliberately capped; and the mitotic-index calculation requires a specific number of decimal places with full working shown. Several independent, easy-to-drop marks stack up on what looks, at a glance, like a short topic.

Section 12 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

This content works well as a supporting paragraph in a longer essay-style answer wherever the title concerns control, cycles, or the importance of a process to organisms — the trick is always closing with a sentence that ties the mechanism back to the actual title, not a generic summary.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around "control"

Mitosis is a tightly controlled process: the cell cycle is checkpoint-regulated, ensuring division only proceeds once DNA has been correctly replicated and the cell is ready. When this control breaks down, cell division becomes uncontrolled, producing a mass of abnormal cells — a tumour — which may become cancerous. Many cancer treatments exploit this same control system, targeting rapidly dividing cells disproportionately because cancer cells divide faster than most healthy tissue. This shows how a single controlled cellular process, when disrupted, can have consequences at the level of the whole organism.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around "cell division as a shared, but not identical, control system"

Cell division is fundamental to growth, repair and reproduction across all life, but the mechanism differs between kingdoms of organism. Eukaryotic cells divide by mitosis, a spindle-based process producing two genetically identical daughter cells; prokaryotic cells instead use binary fission, replicating a single circular DNA molecule and any plasmids before the cytoplasm divides, again producing two daughter cells but without any spindle apparatus at all. Both processes maintain a stable, replicated genetic content in every daughter cell, illustrating that "controlled cell division" is a universal requirement of life, even though its precise mechanism is not.

Whichever essay theme this content supports, treat it as the "mechanism" paragraph: state the process precisely — naming spindle fibres, checkpoints, or circular DNA depending on what the title is asking about — then add one sentence linking it explicitly back to that theme.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Not every cell can divide

  • Differentiated cells (e.g. mature red blood cells, neurones) permanently lose the ability to divide.
  • Cells that do retain it: meristems (plants — root/shoot tip) and stem cells (animals — bone marrow, skin/gut basal layers).
  • This is why the meristem has the highest mitotic index in a root (Section 9).

2 · The cell cycle & DNA replication

  • Interphase (G1, S, G2) is long; mitosis is short. DNA replicates in S phase only.
  • A DNA-content graph: flat-low = pre-S interphase; rising = S phase; flat-high = G2 through anaphase; sudden fall = telophase/cytokinesis only (never prophase/metaphase/anaphase alone).
  • Say "the DNA replicates," not "chromosomes replicate."
  • Cycle length = time between two successive starts (or ends) of DNA replication.

3 · The five stages of mitosis

  • IPMAT: Interphase (not part of mitosis) → Prophase (condense, envelope breaks down) → Metaphase (line up at equator, spindle attaches to centromeres) → Anaphase (centromeres divide, chromatids pulled to poles) → Telophase (envelope reforms, decondense).
  • Chromosome count check: 4 → 4 (chromatid pairs) → 8 chromatids separating → 4+4 in daughters.
  • Spindle-disrupting drug: no attachment → no metaphase; no pulling → no anaphase. Never "homologous chromosomes separate."
  • Non-disjunction = uneven split (too many/too few) — never a cell with zero chromosomes.

4 · Cytokinesis

  • Animal cells: membrane constricts inward (cleavage furrow).
  • Plant cells: new cell plate grows across the centre (rigid cell wall prevents pinching).
  • Spec says "usually" occurs — a separate step from mitosis (nuclear division) itself.

5 · Mitosis vs meiosis boundary

  • Mitosis: 2 identical diploid daughter cells. Meiosis (3.4.3): 4 genetically different haploid cells.
  • Mitosis = sister chromatids separate. Meiosis I = homologous chromosomes separate. Never swap these.
  • A mutation in every body cell arose in the zygote/gamete and spread by mitosis — not "during meiosis."

6 · Mitosis, cancer & treatment

  • Cancer cells divide faster/uncontrollably → drugs targeting division hit them harder than slow-dividing healthy cells.
  • Faster cycle (shorter interphase / earlier replication) → uncontrolled cell division → tumour. Say "cell division," not "growth."
  • Evaluate drug data both ways — and say "in culture," never "not tested on humans."

7 · Binary fission

  • 3 steps: circular DNA + plasmids replicate → cytoplasm divides → 2 daughter cells, each 1 DNA copy + variable plasmids.
  • No spindle, no nucleus, prokaryotic only. Still exactly 2 daughter cells (never 4).

8 · Viruses & cell division

  • Viruses are non-living/acellular — cannot divide by mitosis or binary fission.
  • Nucleic acid injected → host cell's own machinery replicates the virus particles.

9 · RP2 — root-tip squash & mitotic index

  • Method: cut meristem tip → soften in warm dilute HCl → stain (toluidine blue/acetic orcein) → squash → view under microscope.
  • Mitotic index = cells with visible chromosomes ÷ total cells counted. Show full working; watch decimal places.
  • Reliability improvements are pair-marked: many fields of view (representative); repeat & mean (smooths anomalies); standardise edge-cell rule (consistency).

10 · Calculating actual cell size

  • Actual size = image size ÷ magnification.
  • Convert units before/after dividing — final answer usually in µm for a cell.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Not every cell can divide

❓ Explain why a mature red blood cell and a root-tip meristem cell differ in their ability to divide.
The red blood cell has fully differentiated into a specialised cell type and has permanently lost the ability to divide; the meristem cell remains undifferentiated and retains the ability to divide, which is why actively dividing tissue is concentrated at the root tip.
❓ Suggest why the mitotic index differs between the root-tip meristem and a region of fully differentiated root tissue further up the root.
The meristem contains cells that have retained the ability to divide, so a higher proportion are actively in mitosis at any time; the differentiated region contains specialised cells that have permanently lost this ability, so far fewer (or none) are dividing.

The cell cycle & DNA replication

❓ A graph shows DNA content per cell against time. Describe what is happening to the cells during a section where DNA content is constant and low, and during a section where it suddenly falls.
A constant, low DNA content means the cells are in interphase before DNA replication (G1) — DNA content has not yet increased. A sudden fall in DNA content means the cells have completed mitosis and cytokinesis, since the replicated DNA has now been shared equally between two daughter cells; a fall is never seen during prophase, metaphase or anaphase alone, as DNA is only redistributed, not reduced, until the cell physically divides.
❓ Using a DNA-content graph, describe how you would determine the length of the cell cycle.
Measure the time between two successive starts (or two successive ends) of DNA replication on the graph, because DNA replication occurs exactly once per cell cycle, so this interval equals the length of one complete cycle.
❓ State what a DNA content twice the original value indicates about a cell, and identify the earliest cell-cycle stage at which this could be observed.
Twice the original DNA content indicates that DNA replication has occurred; the earliest stage at which this could be observed is late interphase (immediately after S phase), and it remains true through prophase, metaphase and anaphase.

The five stages of mitosis

❓ Describe the behaviour of chromosomes during each of the five stages of mitosis.
Interphase: chromosomes not yet visible; DNA has replicated so each will consist of two sister chromatids. Prophase: chromosomes condense and become visible, each as two sister chromatids joined at a centromere; the nuclear envelope breaks down. Metaphase: chromosomes line up at the equator; spindle fibres attach to centromeres. Anaphase: centromeres divide; spindle fibres pull sister chromatids (now individual chromosomes) to opposite poles. Telophase: chromosomes decondense at each pole; the nuclear envelope reforms around each set.
❓ A photomicrograph shows chromatids visibly being pulled to opposite ends of a cell. Name this stage and explain what is happening.
This is anaphase. Sister (identical) chromatids are separating and are pulled to opposite poles by the shortening of spindle fibres attached to their centromeres.
❓ A drug prevents spindle fibres from attaching to centromeres. Explain the effect this would have on a dividing cell.
Chromosomes cannot be pulled to line up at the equator, so metaphase cannot occur; since spindle fibres also cannot subsequently pull chromatids apart, anaphase cannot occur either, and mitosis cannot be completed, so no new cells are produced.

Cytokinesis

❓ Compare how cytokinesis occurs in an animal cell and in a plant cell, explaining the reason for the difference.
In an animal cell, the cell surface membrane constricts inward around the equator (a cleavage furrow) until the cytoplasm is pinched into two cells. In a plant cell, a rigid cell wall prevents the membrane from constricting, so instead a new cell plate forms across the centre of the cell and grows outward until it fuses with the existing wall, dividing the cell in two.
❓ Explain why the specification states that cytokinesis "usually" — rather than always — occurs after mitosis.
Mitosis (nuclear division) and cytokinesis (division of the cytoplasm) are distinct events; in some cases nuclear division can occur without being immediately followed by a complete division of the cytoplasm, so the two are not strictly guaranteed to occur together every time.

Mitosis vs meiosis boundary

❓ A student writes "in anaphase, homologous chromosomes separate and move to opposite poles." Identify and correct the error.
This describes meiosis I, not mitosis. In mitosis, it is sister (identical) chromatids — not homologous chromosomes — that separate and move to opposite poles.
❓ A mutation is found in every cell of an individual's body. Explain how this mutation came to be present in every cell, and identify the type of division responsible for spreading it.
The mutation arose in the zygote (or in a gamete before fertilisation) and was then propagated to every cell of the body through repeated rounds of mitosis, which produces genetically identical daughter cells; it did not arise during meiosis.

Mitosis, cancer & treatment

❓ Explain why many cancer treatments are more effective against cancer cells than against healthy cells.
Cancer cells divide more rapidly and uncontrollably than most healthy cells; a drug that interferes with the cell cycle therefore affects cancer cells disproportionately, since healthy, slower-dividing cells are relatively spared.
❓ Explain how a shortened interphase could lead to the formation of a tumour.
A shortened interphase, or DNA replication starting earlier or occurring faster, results in a faster overall cell cycle and therefore uncontrolled cell division, producing a mass of abnormal, excessive cells — a tumour.
❓ Data show that a new drug was tested on cancer cells grown in culture, with little further increase in effect above a threshold concentration. Evaluate this drug as a potential cancer treatment.
For: above the threshold concentration, most cancer cells stop dividing, and the drug has a greater effect on rapidly dividing cancer cells than on slower-dividing healthy cells. Against: the drug was tested in culture, so its effect on a whole organism is unknown; some cancer cells may still be dividing even at the concentrations tested, so the cancer could continue to grow or spread; more trials are needed, and healthy dividing cells may also be damaged, causing side effects.

Binary fission

❓ Describe the process of binary fission in a prokaryotic cell.
The circular DNA molecule and any plasmids are replicated; the cytoplasm then divides, producing two daughter cells, each with a single copy of the circular DNA and a variable number of copies of plasmids.
❓ Compare mitosis and binary fission in terms of the DNA replicated, the involvement of spindle fibres, and the number of daughter cells produced.
Mitosis replicates linear DNA (chromosomes) in a eukaryotic cell using spindle fibres, producing two daughter cells. Binary fission replicates circular DNA (and plasmids) in a prokaryotic cell without any spindle fibres, also producing two daughter cells — neither process ever produces four.

Viruses & cell division

❓ Explain why viruses cannot reproduce by cell division, and describe how new virus particles are produced.
Viruses are non-living (acellular) and cannot carry out cell division. Following injection of the virus's nucleic acid into a host cell, the infected host cell's own machinery — ribosomes, enzymes and nucleotides — is used to replicate the viral nucleic acid and synthesise viral proteins, which assemble into new virus particles.
❓ A student describes viral replication as "the virus dividing in two." Explain why this description is inaccurate.
Viruses are non-living and do not undergo cell division of any kind; a virus does not split into two, but instead hijacks a host cell's machinery after injecting its nucleic acid, and it is the host cell that manufactures many new, complete virus particles.

RP2 — root-tip squash & mitotic index

❓ Describe how you would prepare a root tip to identify the stages of mitosis using an optical microscope.
Cut a small piece of root containing the meristem; soften it in warm dilute hydrochloric acid to separate the cells; stain with a DNA-specific stain such as toluidine blue or acetic orcein; add a coverslip and squash to produce a single layer of cells; examine under an optical microscope, working across multiple fields of view.
❓ In a sample of 250 root-tip cells, 40 showed visible condensed chromosomes. Calculate the mitotic index, showing your working, and suggest one way to improve the reliability of this count.
Mitotic index = 40 ÷ 250 = 0.16 (or 16%). Reliability: examine a large number of different fields of view across the slide, so that the sample is representative of the whole root tip rather than one region.
❓ Explain why counting only whole cells at the edge of a field of view, applied consistently, improves the reliability of a mitotic index calculation.
Standardising how part-cells at a field's edge are treated ensures the same counting rule is applied every time, so the count is not biased by where the field boundary happens to fall, making repeated counts more consistent with each other.

Calculating actual cell size

❓ A student measures a cell on a micrograph as 36 mm long. The micrograph magnification is ×2000. Calculate the actual length of the cell in µm, showing your working.
Actual size = image size ÷ magnification = 36 mm ÷ 2000 = 0.018 mm = 18 µm.
❓ Explain why it is important to convert units before dividing when calculating actual cell size from a micrograph.
Image size and magnification must be expressed in consistent units before dividing, and the final answer should be converted into a sensible unit for a cell-sized structure (typically µm); mixing units, or failing to convert, produces an answer that is out by a factor of 1000 or more and is not biologically plausible.
