How the body tells self from non-self, destroys pathogens by phagocytosis, mounts the cellular and humoral responses, and how vaccines, HIV and monoclonal antibodies all trade on the same recognition logic — the second most heavily-tested spec point on the whole course.

Section 1 · spec content point

🪪 Cell-surface molecules and self-recognition

Every cell in your body carries a kind of ID badge on its outer surface. This section is really just one list worth learning cold, because it's the foundation everything else in this topic builds on.

📖

What the specification says: "Each type of cell has specific molecules on its surface that identify it. These molecules include proteins and enable the immune system to identify: pathogens; cells from other organisms of the same species; abnormal body cells; and toxins."

1
2
3

Every cell wears a kind of name badge made of protein on its outside. Your immune system's job is to read these badges and decide who's allowed in. It's on the lookout for four kinds of trouble: germs (pathogens), someone else's cells (like in a mismatched blood transfusion), your own cells gone wrong (cancerous or infected), and poison molecules (toxins).

Mini-glossary: antigen = the badge itself; self = badges your body recognises as belonging to you; non-self = badges that trigger a response.

Proteins make good identity tags because there's an almost endless variety of possible shapes they can fold into — and it's that huge range of precise 3-D shapes that lets your immune system's lymphocytes tell "self" from "non-self" at a glance. The four things the immune system is built to spot are: pathogens; cells from another organism of the same species (which is why a mismatched blood transfusion or an unmatched organ transplant gets attacked); abnormal body cells, meaning cancerous or virus-infected cells; and toxins.

Proteins are used as the body's identity tags because they combine an enormous variety of possible sequences with a highly specific tertiary structure — a huge number of distinct 3-D shapes is available, and it's exactly that variety that lets lymphocytes distinguish one cell type from another. The membrane component actually doing the recognising is usually a glycoprotein (or glycolipid), sitting on the outer surface of the plasma membrane where it is exposed and can be read by a passing lymphocyte.

📌

This is the same glycoprotein/glycolipid "tag" idea from membrane structure (3.2.3) doing double duty here as the recognition site — worth stating explicitly if a question links the two topics.

▶ 🚩 Trap — describing the "four things" too vaguely

Simply saying "the immune system recognises foreign things" is worth nothing. Learn the list by name — pathogens; cells from other organisms of the same species; abnormal body cells; toxins — because a question can ask for any one of the four on its own, and a generic answer doesn't identify which category is meant.

Section 2 · spec content point

🧬 Antigens and antigen variability

A short, word-perfect definition, plus the single most exam-relevant consequence of pathogens mutating: why last year's flu jab doesn't cover this year's flu.

📖

What the specification says: "Definition of antigen. The effect of antigen variability on disease and disease prevention."

1
2
3

An antigen is like a germ's unique fingerprint — your body reads it and builds a "wanted poster" (antibodies) to match. But some germs, like flu, keep changing their fingerprint (this is called mutating). When that happens, your body's old wanted posters don't match the new fingerprint any more, so you can catch the same illness again, and last year's vaccine stops working.

Mini-glossary: antigen = a molecule that triggers an immune response; mutation = a change in genetic material; strain = a version of a pathogen with a slightly different fingerprint.

An antigen is a molecule — usually a protein — found on a cell's surface or on a pathogen, and it's defined by what it does: it triggers your immune system to make matching antibodies. The problem is that a pathogen's genetic material can mutate, which changes the shape of its antigens — this is called antigenic variability. Since your existing antibodies and memory cells were shaped to fit the old antigen, they no longer bind properly, so you can be infected by the new strain even if you've had it before, and any vaccine built against the old antigen stops being effective.

An antigen is a molecule, usually a protein, that stimulates an immune response, resulting in the production of specific antibodies. Antigens are usually found on the cell-surface membrane of a cell, or on the surface of a pathogen. That final clause matters: describing an antigen only as "a protein on the surface" is not a complete definition — you must also state that it stimulates an immune response and antibody production.

The effect of antigen variability

  1. A mutation occurs in the pathogen's DNA, RNA, genome or genetic material.
  2. This alters the tertiary structure of the attachment protein / antigen — this change is what's meant by antigenic variability.
  3. Memory cells and antibodies produced during a previous infection are no longer complementary in shape, so they can no longer bind to the new antigen.
  4. The person can therefore be infected again, by a different strain of the same pathogen — and a vaccine built against the original antigen no longer works.
💉

Applied to vaccines: a vaccine only protects against the strain(s) whose antigens it was built from. Because circulating strains keep changing shape through mutation, manufacturers have to predict and target whichever strain is likely to be most prevalent in the coming season — this is exactly why flu vaccines are reformulated every year.

▶ 🚩 Trap — an incomplete definition of "antigen"

"A protein on the surface of a cell" scores nothing on its own. You must state the consequence too: it stimulates an immune response, leading to the production of specific antibodies. Also note: it's the virus or pathogen that mutates — never write "the disease mutated."

Section 3 · spec content point

🦠 Phagocytosis — the body's first responders

Phagocytes are non-specific — they attack anything flagged as foreign, without needing to recognise one particular pathogen. This is the six-step sequence examiners mark, plus the two pairs of similar-sounding words that trip almost everyone up.

📖

What the specification says: "Phagocytosis of pathogens. The subsequent destruction of ingested pathogens by lysozymes."

1
2
3

Think of a phagocyte like Pac-Man. It gets drawn towards a germ, gobbles it up into a little bubble, and then a bag of chemical "scissors" empties into the bubble and chops the germ into pieces. Afterwards, the phagocyte sticks some of those chopped-up pieces on the outside of itself, like putting up a "wanted poster" of the germ it just beat, so other immune cells know what to look out for.

Mini-glossary: phagocyte = the "eating" cell; phagosome = the bubble it forms; lysosome = the bag of scissors; lysozyme = the scissors themselves (an enzyme).

A phagocyte is drawn towards chemicals released by a pathogen and recognises the antigens on it as foreign. It engulfs the pathogen, trapping it inside a bubble called a vacuole (or vesicle, or phagosome — all three names are accepted). A separate structure called a lysosome then fuses with this bubble and empties enzymes called lysozymes into it, which digest the pathogen. Finally, pieces of the digested pathogen — its antigens — get displayed on the phagocyte's own surface, turning it into an antigen-presenting cell that can now recruit the rest of the immune system.

  1. The phagocyte is attracted to the pathogen by chemicals it releases, and recognises the antigens on the pathogen's surface as foreign.
  2. The phagocyte engulfs / ingests the pathogen.
  3. The pathogen is enclosed in a vacuole / vesicle / phagosome within the phagocyte's cytoplasm.
  4. A lysosome fuses with the phagosome, emptying its digestive enzymes into it.
  5. The pathogen is digested / hydrolysed by lysozymes (hydrolytic enzymes) inside the phagosome.
  6. Peptides / antigens from the digested pathogen are displayed on the phagocyte's own cell membrane — the phagocyte has now become an antigen-presenting cell.
Phagocytosis, step by step. Step 4 — antigen presentation — is the step most often left out of phagocytosis answers, and it's the bridge into Section 4.
🔤

Precise terminology, heavily policed: the lysosome is the organelle; the lysozyme is the enzyme inside it — spell them distinctly. Examiners flag persistent confusion between "vacuole" and "vesicle", and between "lysosome" and "lysozyme."

▶ Same mechanism, applied to antibody-drug conjugates

The identical machinery is reused when a cell takes in an antibody-drug conjugate: the cell ingests/engulfs the antibody (or the cell membrane surrounds it — endocytosis), lysosomes fuse with the resulting vesicle/phagosome, and lysozymes break down/digest the antibody to release the drug it was carrying. Recognising this as "the phagocytosis machinery, reused" is the fastest way to answer an unfamiliar-context question like this.

▶ 🚩 Trap — mixing up what fuses with what

Never write "the pathogen fuses with the lysosome" — it is the phagosome (containing the pathogen) that fuses with the lysosome, not the pathogen itself. Also, "break down" or "destroy the bacteria" on their own are treated as too vague if that exact phrase already appears in the question stem — say specifically that lysozymes hydrolyse the pathogen.

Section 4 · spec content point

🛡️ The cellular response — T lymphocytes

The cellular response is T cells responding to a foreign antigen. Only helper T cells and cytotoxic T cells are required — the spec explicitly excludes the role of other T cell types.

📖

What the specification says: "The response of T lymphocytes to a foreign antigen (the cellular response). The role of antigen-presenting cells in the cellular response. The role of helper T cells (TH cells) in stimulating cytotoxic T cells (TC cells), B cells and phagocytes. The role of other T cells is not required."

1
2
3

A phagocyte holds up a "wanted poster" (the antigen) on its own outside. A helper T cell with a matching key checks the poster against its own shape — if it fits, the helper T cell gets excited and clones itself into a whole army. That army then sends orders three ways: to killer cells (cytotoxic T cells, who poke holes in infected cells), to B cells (who start making antibody factories), and to more phagocytes (telling them to eat more germs). Some of the clone army become "memory guards" who remember this exact germ forever.

Mini-glossary: antigen-presenting cell = the cell showing the wanted poster; helper T cell = the cell that checks the match and gives the orders; cytotoxic T cell = the killer cell; clone = an army of identical copies.

Once a phagocyte has become an antigen-presenting cell, a helper T cell carrying a receptor protein that's complementary in shape to the displayed antigen binds to it. This activates the helper T cell, which divides by mitosis to produce a clone of identical helper T cells. These cloned helper T cells then act as the stimulating link to three separate branches of the immune system at once: they stimulate a specific B cell, they stimulate cytotoxic T cells, and they stimulate phagocytes to carry out more phagocytosis. Some of the clone also develop into memory cells. Cytotoxic T cells kill abnormal or virus-infected body cells by producing a protein called perforin, which punches holes in the target cell's membrane so it becomes freely permeable and the cell dies.

  1. A pathogen is engulfed by a phagocyte, which displays the antigen on its own surface — becoming an antigen-presenting cell.
  2. A helper T cell (TH) with a complementary receptor protein binds to the antigen displayed on the antigen-presenting cell.
  3. This activates the helper T cell, which divides by mitosis to form a clone.
  4. The cloned helper T cells then: stimulate a specific B cell; stimulate cytotoxic T cells; stimulate phagocytes to carry out more phagocytosis; and develop into memory cells.
📌

"Helper" only needs to appear once across the whole answer. A named antigen-presenting cell (macrophage, phagocyte, B cell) is accepted, and "T cell stimulates B cell to undergo clonal selection" is dense enough to be worth two separate marking points on its own.

☠️

Cytotoxic T cells (TC): these kill abnormal and virus-infected body cells by producing perforin, a protein that makes holes in the target cell-surface membrane so it becomes freely permeable — water and ions flood in and out uncontrollably, and the cell dies.

▶ Applying this to an autoimmune or "foreign protein" scenario

The same chain applies when the trigger is a faulty self-protein rather than a pathogen: the faulty protein is recognised as an antigen (a "foreign" protein); T cells bind to it; sensitised T cells stimulate clonal selection of B cells; this results in antibodies being released against the faulty protein. A prolonged or excessive response follows a similar pattern — persistent antigen presence causes more/faster T cell production, directed against both the pathogen and normal tissue, the responding cells live longer than usual, and more cytokines (chemicals causing swelling) are produced.

▶ 🚩 Trap — starting the answer in the wrong place

Even if a question only asks about "the humoral response" or "B cells," the mark scheme's opening mark point is still the presentation of antigen on an antigen-presenting cell — not the B cell itself. Always begin a cellular or humoral response answer with the antigen-presentation step.

Section 5 · spec content point

🧪 The humoral response — B cells, clonal selection & antibody structure

This dense spec bullet covers B lymphocytes, clonal selection, antibody definition and antibody structure here — the antigen-antibody complex, its destructive mechanism, and the roles of plasma and memory cells continue in the next section, because the same spec statement covers both.

📖

What the specification says: "The response of B lymphocytes to a foreign antigen, clonal selection and the release of monoclonal antibodies (the humoral response). Definition of antibody. Antibody structure. The formation of an antigen-antibody complex, leading to the destruction of the antigen, limited to agglutination and phagocytosis of bacterial cells. The roles of plasma cells and of memory cells in producing primary and secondary immune responses."

1
2
3

A B cell is like a locksmith carrying one specific key shape on its surface. If that key happens to fit a germ's antigen "lock," the B cell grabs it and — with a nod from a helper T cell — starts photocopying itself into a factory of identical B cells, called plasma cells. Each plasma cell then pumps out copies of that same key (an antibody) by the thousand. The antibody itself is shaped like the letter Y, with two identical key-ends that fit only one type of lock.

Mini-glossary: antibody = the Y-shaped key protein; plasma cell = the antibody factory; binding site = the key-end shape (never call it an "active site").

A B cell with a complementary antibody already on its own surface binds to a displayed antigen, forming an antigen-antibody complex. A specific helper T cell then stimulates that specific B cell, which divides by mitosis to form a clone — this whole process is clonal selection. Most of the clone becomes plasma cells, which secrete antibodies, while some become memory cells instead. An antibody is a protein made by plasma cells, built from four polypeptide chains — two long "heavy" chains and two shorter "light" chains — held together in a Y shape by disulfide bridges. The tips of the Y (the variable regions) are unique to each antibody and form the binding site; the base (the constant region) is the same across a whole class of antibodies.

The humoral response sequence

  1. The antigen is displayed on antigen-presenting cells / phagocytes / B cells.
  2. A B cell with a complementary antibody on its surface binds to the antigen, forming an antigen-antibody complex.
  3. A specific helper T cell stimulates the specific B cell.
  4. The B cell divides by mitosis — forms a clone — this is clonal selection.
  5. Plasma cells produce / release / secrete antibodies (up to around 2000 per second per cell).
  6. Some B cells develop into memory cells instead of plasma cells.
📌

Which organelles make antibodies? The rough endoplasmic reticulum / ribosomes, and the Golgi apparatus / vesicles — antibodies are proteins, synthesised and then processed and packaged for secretion. Ignore the nucleus. Always write the organelle's name out in full — "ER" on its own is not accepted.

Definition and structure of an antibody

Definition: an antibody is a protein produced by plasma cells (derived from B lymphocytes) in response to a specific antigen, with a binding site complementary to that antigen.

Structure: four polypeptide chains — two identical heavy chains and two identical light chains — held together in a Y shape by disulfide bridges. There is a constant region, the same in all antibodies of a given class, which allows phagocytes to recognise it generically; and two variable regions, whose tertiary structure differs between antibodies and forms the two binding sites, each complementary to one specific antigen. A hinge region gives the molecule flexibility.

Two heavy chains run the full length (stem + one arm each); two light chains cover only the upper arm segments. All four are held by disulfide bonds — never hydrogen or peptide bonds.
▶ 🚩 Trap — "active site"

Reject "active site" every time — an antibody has a binding site or a variable region; "active site" belongs to enzymes only, and mark schemes explicitly penalise it (usually once across the whole answer) if it recurs.

Section 6 · spec content point

🤝 Antigen-antibody complexes, agglutination & primary/secondary responses

Same spec statement as Section 5 — this half covers how an antigen-antibody complex actually leads to destruction of the pathogen, and how plasma and memory cells produce the primary and secondary responses.

📖

What the specification says: "The response of B lymphocytes to a foreign antigen, clonal selection and the release of monoclonal antibodies (the humoral response). Definition of antibody. Antibody structure. The formation of an antigen-antibody complex, leading to the destruction of the antigen, limited to agglutination and phagocytosis of bacterial cells. The roles of plasma cells and of memory cells in producing primary and secondary immune responses."

1
2
3

Antibodies never kill anything themselves — think of them as having two sticky hands, so one antibody can grab onto two different germs at once, gluing lots of germs together into one big clump, like a snowball. That clump is now too big and slow to sneak into your cells, and it's easy for a phagocyte to spot and gobble up. The first time you meet a germ, it takes your body days to build up antibodies — that's the primary response, and that's why you feel ill. The second time the same germ turns up, "memory guards" left over from last time recognise it instantly and pump out antibodies so fast you often don't even notice you were exposed — that's the secondary response.

Mini-glossary: agglutination = germs clumped together; memory cell = a cell that "remembers" a germ for years; primary/secondary response = first time vs repeat exposure.

Each antibody has two binding sites, so a single antibody can bind two pathogens simultaneously, clumping them together — this clumping is called agglutination. The resulting clump is too large to enter host cells and is easily located and engulfed by phagocytes — so the antibody's role is to bind and mark, while phagocytes carry out the actual destruction. An antibody only binds one type of antigen because its variable regions have a specific tertiary structure, complementary to that antigen alone. During a first infection (the primary response), antibody levels rise slowly and only reach a low concentration, because clonal selection of B cells takes time — this is why symptoms appear. On a second exposure to the same antigen (the secondary response), memory cells recognise it immediately and divide rapidly into plasma cells, so antibody concentration rises faster and higher, and the pathogen is usually destroyed before symptoms even develop.

How an antigen-antibody complex leads to destruction

Antibodies bind to antigens and act as markers (opsonins), causing clumping — agglutination — of the pathogens carrying that antigen. Because each antibody has two binding sites, one antibody molecule can bind two separate pathogens at once, clumping them together into a large aggregate. This clump is too large to enter host cells, and is easily located and engulfed by phagocytosis. The destruction mechanism the spec limits this to is precisely agglutination and phagocytosis of bacterial cells — the antibody itself never destroys anything.

An antibody is specific to one antigen because it has a specific tertiary structure, giving it a binding site / variable region that is complementary only to that one antigen; since that antigen is only found on one particular type of cell, the antibody only binds to and forms an antigen-antibody complex with those cells.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "clotting" and "killing"

The word is agglutination, never "clotting." And antibodies never "kill" a pathogen or toxin — they bind and cause agglutination; phagocytes carry out the actual destruction. Four phrases worth being able to reproduce almost word for word: "the antigen is displayed on an antigen-presenting cell"; "a specific helper T cell with a complementary receptor binds and stimulates a specific B cell"; "the B cell divides by mitosis — clonal selection — forming plasma cells and memory cells"; "plasma cells secrete antibodies; the antibody binds to the antigen, forming an antigen-antibody complex, causing agglutination and phagocytosis of bacterial cells."

Section 7 · spec content point

💉 Vaccination and herd immunity

A vaccine is just a controlled way of triggering the same humoral response chain from Sections 5–6 — the point of it is to get memory cells in place before you ever meet the real pathogen.

📖

What the specification says: "The use of vaccines to provide protection for individuals and populations against disease. The concept of herd immunity."

1
2
3

A vaccine is a safe preview of a germ's "wanted poster" (antigen), given to your body before you ever meet the real thing — so your body builds memory guards in advance, without you getting ill. Herd immunity is like a firebreak: if enough people around you are vaccinated, the germ can't spread far enough to reach the few people who can't be vaccinated (like newborn babies or people who are very ill), so they're protected too, indirectly.

Mini-glossary: vaccine = a safe dose of antigen; herd immunity = protection of the unvaccinated via reduced spread; transmissible = spreads person-to-person.

A vaccine contains an antigen from a pathogen (or a safe version of it), which gets displayed on antigen-presenting cells just like a real infection would. This triggers the normal chain: a helper T cell binds and stimulates a specific B cell, which clones into plasma cells (releasing antibodies) and memory cells. If the real pathogen turns up later, those memory cells trigger a fast, large secondary response that clears it before symptoms appear. Herd immunity is a side effect of vaccinating enough of a population: once transmission is difficult enough, even unvaccinated people — the very young, the ill, the immunosuppressed — are protected, because the disease simply can't find enough new hosts to spread through the population.

How a vaccine provides protection

  1. The vaccine contains an antigen from the pathogen.
  2. This antigen is displayed on antigen-presenting cells.
  3. A specific helper T cell binds to the antigen and stimulates a specific B cell.
  4. The B cell divides by mitosis to form a clone of plasma cells.
  5. Plasma cells secrete large amounts of antibody; memory cells are also produced.
  6. On re-infection, memory cells trigger a faster, larger secondary response, destroying the pathogen before symptoms appear.

Herd immunity

Herd immunity is the protection of unvaccinated individuals that occurs when a sufficiently large proportion of a population is vaccinated, so the pathogen cannot spread easily — protecting those who cannot be vaccinated themselves, such as the very young, the ill, or the immunosuppressed.

⚖️

The contrast AQA sets: vaccinating against a transmissible disease (e.g. measles) achieves herd immunity, reducing spread through the population. Vaccinating against a non-transmissible disease (e.g. tetanus, contracted from soil through a wound, not person-to-person) gives no herd immunity — it only protects the vaccinated individual.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "the disease mutated"

A disease can reappear in a vaccinated population because vaccination uptake has fallen (more unvaccinated individuals in the population), or because the virus / pathogen has mutated (antigenic variability) — never write that "the disease mutated"; it is the pathogen that mutates.

▶ Ethical issues with vaccines — quick reference

Vaccines are usually tested on animals and then on human volunteers who may be put at some risk; there are side effects, so a person may be harmed by a vaccine for a disease they might never have caught; supply may be limited, raising who-goes-first questions; making vaccination compulsory balances individual freedom against herd immunity; some vaccines use fetal tissue or animal products in production; and cost raises questions of whether wealthier countries or people get priority. A full "discuss" answer pairs each concern with the corresponding public-health benefit — see Section 13 for the complete ethics treatment alongside monoclonal antibodies.

⚖️ Active and passive immunity

Every "contrast" answer here needs both sides paired on the same point — a one-sided answer scores less than the paired version, even if both sides are individually correct somewhere in the answer.

📖

What the specification says: "The differences between active and passive immunity."

1
2
3

Active immunity is building your own army from scratch — it takes a while to train the soldiers (days), but once trained they stick around for years (memory cells). Passive immunity is borrowing someone else's already-trained soldiers for a while — protection is instant, but the borrowed soldiers get used up and leave, so it doesn't last.

Mini-glossary: active immunity = your own antibodies, from infection or vaccination; passive immunity = antibodies given to you from outside, e.g. antivenom or through the placenta.

Active immunity is produced by your own plasma cells in response to meeting the actual antigen — either by catching the disease or by vaccination. Because memory cells are formed, it takes time to develop (days, while clonal selection happens) but lasts a long time. Passive immunity involves antibodies that are introduced from an outside source — an injection of antibodies (like antivenom), or antibodies crossing the placenta or in breast milk from mother to baby. Because no memory cells are made and the antibody itself is eventually broken down and not replaced, protection is fast-acting but only short term.

A frequent national error assumes maternal antibodies crossing the placenta "kill all pathogens." Section 9 · spec content point

🧫 HIV structure and replication

HIV is not a cell — no nucleus, no DNA of its own, no cell wall or membrane — and the mark-scheme reject list for this section is worth learning as carefully as the structure itself.

📖

What the specification says: "Structure of the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and its replication in helper T cells."

1
2
3

HIV is like a tiny burglar carrying a fake key (its attachment protein) that happens to fit the lock on a helper T cell. It breaks in, but instead of stealing anything, it leaves behind a blueprint (its genetic code, converted into DNA) inside the cell's own factory. The factory then follows that blueprint and starts building copies of the burglar, which leave to go break into more cells.

Mini-glossary: RNA = HIV's genetic material; reverse transcriptase = the enzyme that converts RNA into DNA; capsid = the protein coat; attachment protein = the "key" on the surface.

HIV carries its genetic material as RNA rather than DNA, protected inside a protein coat called a capsid, itself wrapped in a lipid envelope derived from a previous host cell's membrane. Studded on that envelope are attachment proteins, which are what let HIV recognise and bind to receptors on a helper T cell. HIV also carries its own enzyme, reverse transcriptase, which it needs because host cells can't normally read RNA the way HIV needs them to. To replicate: the attachment proteins bind to receptors on a helper T cell, the RNA enters the cell, reverse transcriptase converts that RNA into DNA (which gets inserted into the host's own DNA), the cell's machinery is hijacked to produce new viral proteins, and new virus particles are assembled and released to infect further cells.

Structure of HIV

  • RNA — the genetic material
  • Reverse transcriptase — an enzyme, carried inside the virus
  • Capsid / capsomeres — the protein coat surrounding the RNA
  • (Phospho)lipid viral envelope — made of membrane, derived from a previous host cell
  • Attachment proteins — studded on the envelope, bind to receptors on the host cell
Do not label: nucleus · DNA (it's RNA) · plasmids · "capsule" (correct term is capsid) · cell wall / cell membrane — HIV is not a cell.

HIV replication

  1. Attachment proteins attach / bind to receptors on the helper T cell / lymphocyte.
  2. Nucleic acid / RNA enters the cell.
  3. Reverse transcriptase converts RNA to DNA, which is inserted into the host cell's DNA.
  4. The cell produces viral protein / capsid / enzymes, using its own machinery.
  5. Virus particles are assembled and released from the cell (by budding, lysis, or emerging).
▶ 🚩 Trap — the explicit HIV-structure reject list

Reject nucleus, DNA, or plasmids for HIV's structure — it has RNA, not DNA. Reject "capsule" — the correct term is capsid. Reject any suggestion that HIV has a cell membrane or a cell wall, since HIV is not a cell. And note the terminology split: the virus has attachment proteins; only the host cell has receptors — don't swap the two.

Section 10 · spec content point

😷 How HIV causes AIDS, and why antibiotics fail against viruses

AIDS symptoms are never caused by HIV directly — they're caused by opportunistic infections and cancers the body can no longer fight off, once its helper T cell "coordinator" has been knocked out.

📖

What the specification says: "How HIV causes the symptoms of AIDS. Why antibiotics are ineffective against viruses."

1
2
3

Helper T cells are like the team coach — they don't fight directly, but they call the plays for everyone else (killer cells, antibody factories, phagocytes). HIV specifically targets and destroys the coach. With no coach, no plays get called, so the team can't fight off infections it would normally shrug off easily — and it's those extra infections, not HIV itself, that make someone with AIDS seriously ill. Separately, antibiotics work by damaging things only bacteria have (like their cell wall) — but viruses don't have any of those things, so there's nothing for an antibiotic to attack.

Mini-glossary: opportunistic infection = an infection a healthy immune system would normally fight off easily; antibiotic resistance = the correct term for a bacterium surviving a drug (not "immunity").

HIV destroys (or reduces the number of) helper T cells. Since helper T cells are the stimulating link to B cells, cytotoxic T cells and phagocytes, fewer helper T cells means fewer B cells are activated or stimulated to divide into plasma cells — so less or no antibody is produced. Cytotoxic T cells and phagocytes also aren't stimulated properly. The overall effect is that the person can no longer fight off infections that a healthy immune system would easily clear — these opportunistic infections, and some cancers, are what actually cause AIDS symptoms, not HIV itself. Antibiotics don't work on viruses because they're built to target features specific to bacteria — cell wall-building enzymes, or bacterial ribosomes and metabolic pathways — and viruses have none of these; they simply hijack the host cell's own machinery, leaving nothing bacteria-specific for an antibiotic to attack.

How HIV causes the symptoms of AIDS

  1. Less / no antibody is produced.
  2. Because HIV destroys (or reduces the number of) helper T cells.
  3. So few / no B cells are activated / stimulated, or few/no B cells undergo mitosis / differentiate / form plasma cells.

The consequence chain extends further: without functioning helper T cells, cytotoxic T cells and phagocytes are also not properly stimulated, so the person cannot effectively fight secondary, opportunistic infections. AIDS symptoms are caused by those opportunistic infections and by certain cancers — not by HIV directly.

Why antibiotics are ineffective against viruses

Antibiotics work by inhibiting the enzymes bacteria use to build their cell wall (murein / peptidoglycan), or by disrupting bacterial ribosomes and metabolic pathways. Viruses have no cell wall, no murein, no ribosomes of their own, and no independent metabolic pathways — they hijack the host cell's own machinery to replicate instead. There is therefore no bacteria-specific target for an antibiotic to act on, and any drug that damaged the host's own cellular machinery would damage the patient's own cells too.

▶ 🚩 Trap — vague "weakened immune system" language, and "antibiotic resistance" vs "immunity"

"The body can't fight it" is too vague — always name what's actually reduced: fewer/no functional T- and B-lymphocytes, and therefore less/no antibody production and clearance. Separately, this topic is widely confused with vaccines, and the correct term for a bacterium surviving an antibiotic is antibiotic resistance — not "immunity," which is reserved for the host's own immune response.

Section 11 · spec content point

🎯 Monoclonal antibodies — targeted therapy & diagnosis

Every monoclonal antibody in a clone has the same tertiary structure, so it binds one specific target reliably — that reliability is the whole reason it's useful for delivering drugs or dyes precisely.

📖

What the specification says: "The use of monoclonal antibodies in: targeting medication to specific cell types by attaching a therapeutic drug to an antibody; medical diagnosis."

1
2
3

A monoclonal antibody is like an army of identical clone soldiers, all carrying the exact same "wanted poster" shape. Because they're all identical, you can strap a package (a drug, or a dye) onto every single one, send them out, and know they'll only deliver it to houses (cells) that match that one exact shape — leaving other houses alone.

Mini-glossary: monoclonal = made from one single clone of cells; targeted therapy = delivering a drug only to matching cells; diagnosis = using antibodies to detect something, not treat it.

A monoclonal antibody is a batch of antibodies that all share the same tertiary structure, because they're produced from a single clone of identical plasma cells or B cells — not from a mixture of different B cell lines. That uniformity is what makes them useful: in direct therapy, the antibody binds to an antigen on a target cell (e.g. a cancer cell) and physically blocks the receptor that growth-signalling chemicals would normally bind to, stopping the cell dividing. In indirect (antibody-drug conjugate) therapy, a cytotoxic drug is chemically attached to the antibody, so it only gets delivered to cells carrying the matching antigen — meaning a lower dose can be used and side effects are reduced. The same specificity is used in diagnosis: linking a dye or fluorescent marker to the antibody lets a specific cell type be identified under a microscope or in a lab test.

Definition: a monoclonal antibody has the same tertiary structure throughout the batch, because it is produced from identical / cloned plasma cells or B cells — it is best described in the context of a single plasma cell / B lymphocyte being cloned.

Uses

  • Direct therapy: the antibody binds to antigens on target cells (e.g. cancer cells) and blocks the receptors that growth-stimulating chemicals would normally bind to, so the cells stop dividing.
  • Indirect therapy (antibody-drug conjugate): a cytotoxic drug is attached to the antibody, which delivers it only to cells carrying the complementary antigen — so a lower dose can be used and side effects are reduced.
  • Medical diagnosis: the target protein is located in the cell-surface membrane; a complementary antibody binds to / attaches to it, forming an antigen-antibody complex; cells are then identified by a dye, stain or fluorescent marker linked to the antibody.
📌

Explaining how a monoclonal antibody blocks damage: the antibody binds to the cell's antigen (or forms an antigen-antibody complex with it), so less or no harmful antibody / signal can bind there — and when the monoclonal antibody itself binds, it does not trigger the same damaging effect. For diagnosis-style questions, "colour production" or "marker" on their own are too vague — always state exactly what is attached to the antibody (a dye, stain or fluorescent marker).

▶ Why can targeted therapy still cause side effects?

Even a highly specific antibody-drug conjugate will sometimes bind to non-tumour, healthy cells that happen to carry a small amount of the same or a similar antigen, causing damage to those healthy cells or to other organs/systems — targeting reduces but never fully eliminates side effects.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "active site" and "genetically identical antibody"

Reject "active site" here too. Also reject "genetically identical antibody" — antibodies are proteins, not genetic material, so they don't have genes; it is the plasma cells that are cloned (genetically identical to each other), not the antibody molecule itself. A monoclonal antibody is not "a clone of an antibody."

🚫 Scope note — monoclonal antibody production isn't examined

The spec explicitly rules something out here, and that boundary is worth understanding, not just skipping past — knowing what you don't need to know saves real revision time.

📖

What the specification says: "Details of the commercial or scientific production of monoclonal antibodies are not required."

1
2
3

It's like being told you don't need to know how a car factory actually builds a car — you just need to know what the car can do, and why some people are cautious about how the factory operates. This clause exists to draw a clear line: you are examined on what monoclonal antibodies are, how they're defined, and how they're used therapeutically and diagnostically — but not on the laboratory or industrial steps used to actually manufacture them (such as fusing cells to create hybridomas). This is a boundary statement, not a content statement — it tells you where revision effort should stop. You are not required to know the commercial or scientific steps used to manufacture monoclonal antibodies (for example, the biotechnology behind fusing cells to produce a stable clone). What you are required to know sits either side of this boundary: the definition of a monoclonal antibody (Section 11 — same tertiary structure, produced from a clone of identical plasma/B cells) and its therapeutic and diagnostic uses (Section 11); and the ethical issues associated with how monoclonal antibodies are produced and used (Section 13), which legitimately references the use of animals in production even though the production method itself is off the syllabus.

A common source of confusion: students sometimes assume that because "production is not required," any mention of animals or mice in an ethics answer must be wrong. ⚖️ Ethical issues with vaccines and monoclonal antibodies

"Discuss" always means both sides — a list of concerns with no counterbalancing benefit is capped, no matter how many valid concerns it contains.

📖

What the specification says: "Ethical issues associated with the use of vaccines and monoclonal antibodies."

1
2
3

Picture a set of weighing scales. On one side you put the concerns — animal testing, side effects, cost, who gets treatment first. On the other side you put the benefits — lives saved, diseases prevented, treatments that work when nothing else does. A good answer always puts something on both sides of the scale, never just one.

Vaccines raise concerns around animal and human trial risk, side effects for people who might never have caught the disease anyway, who gets priority when supply is limited, whether vaccination should ever be made compulsory, the use of fetal tissue or animal products in production, and cost/access across richer and poorer countries. Monoclonal antibodies raise similar concerns: animals (commonly mice) are used in production and testing, sometimes dying during trials; human trials have caused deaths and serious side effects; treatment is expensive, limiting who can access it; and patients who are very ill may struggle to give fully informed consent. On the other side, monoclonal antibody treatments are highly specific, so healthy cells are largely spared and side effects tend to be milder than conventional chemotherapy — and they've proven effective in conditions with few other treatment options.

Ethical issues with vaccines

  • Whether vaccination should be compulsory, balancing individual freedom against herd immunity.
  • The use of fetal tissue or animal products in production.

▶ 🚩 Trap — one-sided "discuss" or "evaluate" answers

An answer that argues only "yes, ethical" or only "no, unethical" is capped, typically at around half the available marks. Section 14 · spec content point

🔬 The ELISA test

No named required practical is tied directly to this spec point, but the ELISA test is examined as a genuine laboratory method — describe it as a real technique, not just a labelled diagram.

📖

What the specification says: "The use of antibodies in the ELISA test."

1
2
3

Think of ELISA like fishing with a very specific hook, plus a dye that only shows up if you've actually caught something. A target molecule is stuck to the bottom of a well; a matching "hook" (antibody) with a dye-triggering tag attached is poured in; anything that doesn't get caught is washed away; then a colour-changing chemical is added — if the colour changes, you know your hook caught something.

Mini-glossary: antigen = the target molecule; substrate = the chemical that changes colour; wash step = rinsing away anything that didn't bind.

ELISA uses the specificity of antibody-antigen binding to detect and measure a target molecule. In the direct version (detecting an antigen), the antigen sample is fixed to a well, a specific antibody with an enzyme already attached is added and binds if the antigen is present, unbound antibody is washed away, then a substrate is added — if the enzyme is present, it reacts and produces a colour change. In the indirect version (commonly used to detect a patient's own antibodies, e.g. an HIV test), the antigen is fixed to the well and the patient's blood plasma is added; if the patient has made antibodies to that antigen, they bind; after washing, a second antibody — one that's complementary to the patient's antibody, with an enzyme attached — is added, binds, and is washed again; a substrate is added last, and a colour change confirms the patient's antibody, and therefore the enzyme, was present.

Direct ELISA (detecting an antigen)

  1. The antigen sample is fixed to the surface of a well.
  2. A specific antibody, with an enzyme already attached, is added and binds if the antigen is present.
  3. The well is washed to remove any unbound antibody.
  4. A substrate solution is added; if the enzyme is present, it reacts with the substrate and produces a colour change.

Indirect ELISA (detecting the patient's own antibodies)

  1. The antigen is fixed to the well; a sample of the patient's blood plasma is added — if the person has made antibodies to that antigen, they bind, forming an antigen-antibody complex.
  2. The well is washed to remove unbound antibody.
  3. A second antibody, with an enzyme attached, is added — this second antibody is complementary to the patient's own (first) antibody, not to the original antigen.
  4. The well is washed again to remove unbound second antibody.
  5. A substrate is added; a colour change indicates the enzyme is present, confirming the patient's antibody was present.
Indirect ELISA (used to test for a patient's own antibodies, e.g. an HIV test) — the second antibody is complementary to the patient's antibody, not to the original antigen. Washing between every step removes unbound antibody; skip it and the test can give a false positive.
📌

The four generic mark points, in any ELISA answer: (1) the (first) antibody binds / attaches / is complementary in shape to the antigen fixed in the well; (2) a second antibody with an enzyme attached is added; (3) the second antibody attaches to the antigen — or, in the indirect version, to the first antibody; (4) a substrate is added and the colour changes. Point 4 is only awarded if the enzyme is specifically mentioned. Annotated diagrams are accepted, but never mix the direct and indirect versions together in one answer.

▶ Why wash between steps, and how to read a negative result

Why wash? To show that only the enzyme is causing the colour change, and that washing is effective / all unbound antibody has been removed. Without this step, unbound antibody-enzyme could cause a colour change even where no target antigen or antibody is actually present — a false positive.

Interpreting a negative result: the antibody / antigen being tested for is not present, so the second antibody / enzyme cannot bind and is not present either — meaning there is no colour change.

▶ 🚩 Trap — the enzyme doesn't bind on its own

Reject "the enzyme attaches/binds to the anti-human antibody" as a description of step 3 — it is the second antibody, with the enzyme already attached to it before it is added, that binds. The enzyme itself never binds independently.

Misconception frequency — high: "active site," antibodies "killing" pathogens, HIV mistaken for a bacterium, monoclonal antibodies mistaken for "a clone of an antibody." Terminology precision risk — high: vacuole/vesicle/phagosome/phagolysosome, lysosome/lysozyme, agglutination not clotting, capsid not capsule.

At AS, the cellular and humoral response sequences each require several named cell types stated in the correct order, with the correct causal links between them — a partially correct sequence loses the connecting marks even when every individual cell type named is technically correct. At A2, HIV/immune-response comprehension is a persistent national weak spot, and the ethical-evaluation demands require genuinely two-sided answers.

▶ Example model paragraph

A pathogen engulfed by a phagocyte has its antigen displayed on the phagocyte's own cell-surface membrane, turning it into an antigen-presenting cell. A helper T cell with a complementary receptor binds to this antigen, is activated, and divides by mitosis to form a clone. The cloned helper T cells then stimulate cytotoxic T cells, which kill infected body cells, and stimulate B cells to undergo clonal selection, dividing into plasma cells that secrete antibodies and memory cells that provide long-term active immunity. Antibodies bind to antigens, forming antigen-antibody complexes that cause agglutination and are cleared by phagocytosis — never by the antibody itself, and never via an "active site."

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Cell-surface molecules

  • Proteins make good ID tags: enormous variety + highly specific tertiary structure.
  • Learn the four things identified: pathogens; cells from other organisms of the same species; abnormal body cells; toxins.
  • Glycoprotein/glycolipid on the outer plasma membrane is the actual recognition site.

2 · Antigens & variability

  • Antigen = molecule (usually protein) that stimulates an immune response, leading to antibody production.
  • Mutation → antigen's tertiary structure changes → antigenic variability → old memory cells/antibodies no longer bind → reinfection possible, vaccine outdated.

3 · Phagocytosis

  • Attracted by chemicals → engulfs → vacuole/vesicle/phagosome → lysosome fuses, releases lysozymes → digested → antigens presented (APC).
  • Lysosome = organelle; lysozyme = enzyme. Don't mix them up.

4 · Cellular response (T cells)

  • APC displays antigen → helper T cell (complementary receptor) binds, activates, clones by mitosis.
  • Cloned helper T cells stimulate: cytotoxic T cells, B cells, phagocytes; some become memory cells.
  • Cytotoxic T cells kill via perforin — freely permeable membrane, cell dies.

5 · Humoral response & antibody structure

  • B cell (complementary antibody) binds antigen → helper T stimulates it → clonal selection (mitosis) → plasma cells + memory cells.
  • Antibody = protein, 4 chains (2 heavy + 2 light), Y-shape, disulfide bridges, constant + 2 variable regions, hinge.
  • Never "active site" — binding site / variable region only.

6 · Antigen-antibody complex

  • 2 binding sites per antibody → clumps pathogens together → agglutination → phagocytosed. Antibodies never "kill."
  • Primary response: slow, low [Ab]. Secondary: memory cells → fast, high [Ab], often before symptoms.

7 · Vaccination & herd immunity

  • Vaccine antigen → same chain as infection → memory cells form without illness.
  • Herd immunity only works for transmissible diseases (measles, not tetanus).
  • Disease reappears: falling uptake OR pathogen mutation — never "the disease mutated."

8 · Active & passive immunity

  • Active: own plasma cells, memory cells, slow, long-lasting.
  • Passive: antibody from outside, no memory cells, fast, short-lived (e.g. antivenom, placenta/breast milk).
  • Always give both sides of the contrast — never a one-sided answer.

9 · HIV structure & replication

  • RNA, reverse transcriptase, capsid, lipid envelope, attachment proteins. Reject nucleus/DNA/plasmids/capsule/cell wall.
  • Replication: attach → RNA enters → reverse transcriptase makes DNA → inserted into host DNA → viral proteins made → particles assembled/released.

10 · HIV, AIDS & antibiotics

  • HIV destroys helper T cells → fewer B cells activated → less/no antibody → opportunistic infections/cancers cause AIDS symptoms, not HIV itself.
  • Antibiotics target bacterial cell wall/ribosomes — viruses have neither, so no target.

11 · Monoclonal antibodies

  • Same tertiary structure, from a clone of identical plasma/B cells (not "genetically identical antibody").
  • Direct therapy: blocks growth receptors on target cells. Indirect: antibody-drug conjugate, targeted delivery.
  • Diagnosis: antibody + dye/fluorescent marker binds specific cell-surface protein.

12 · Production — not required

  • You don't need the lab/commercial production method — only the definition and uses.
  • Ethics questions can still reference animal use in production, even though the method itself isn't examined.

13 · Ethical issues

  • Vaccines: trial risk, side effects, supply priority, compulsory vs freedom, fetal tissue/animal products, cost.
  • Monoclonal antibodies: animal production/deaths, human trial risk, cost, informed consent — vs high specificity, fewer side effects than chemo.
  • Always balance both sides — a one-sided "discuss" answer is capped.

14 · The ELISA test

  • Direct: antigen fixed → enzyme-linked antibody binds → wash → substrate → colour change.
  • Indirect: antigen fixed → patient's antibody binds → wash → 2nd enzyme-linked antibody binds 1st antibody → wash → substrate → colour.
  • Wash removes unbound antibody — prevents false positives.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Cell-surface molecules

❓ State the four categories of molecule that cell-surface molecules allow the immune system to identify.
Pathogens; cells from other organisms of the same species; abnormal body cells (cancerous or virus-infected); toxins.
❓ A membrane protein is described as "the recognition site" a lymphocyte reads. What type of molecule is it, and why is its location significant?
A glycoprotein (or glycolipid); it sits on the outer surface of the plasma membrane, so it is exposed and can be recognised by lymphocytes as self or non-self.
❓ Explain why proteins, rather than lipids or carbohydrates alone, are so effective as cell-identity tags.
Proteins have an enormous variety of possible sequences and a highly specific tertiary structure, giving an almost unlimited range of distinct 3-D shapes — it is that variety of precise shape that allows lymphocytes to distinguish self from non-self.

Antigens & antigen variability

❓ Define an antigen.
A molecule, usually a protein, that stimulates an immune response, resulting in the production of specific antibodies; antigens are usually found on the cell-surface membrane of a cell or on the surface of a pathogen.
❓ A virus that caused infection last year has mutated. Explain why a person who caught it last year could catch it again this year.
A mutation in the viral genetic material alters the tertiary structure of the attachment protein/antigen — antigenic variability; memory cells and antibodies from the previous infection are no longer complementary and cannot bind; so the person can be infected again by the new strain.
❓ Suggest why flu vaccines have to be reformulated and given again every year, unlike some other vaccines.
The influenza virus undergoes frequent antigenic variability, so the antigens present differ from strain to strain; a vaccine built against last year's antigen no longer matches this year's circulating strain, so manufacturers must predict and target whichever strain is likely to be most prevalent.

Phagocytosis

❓ Describe the process of phagocytosis, from a phagocyte first encountering a bacterium to that bacterium being destroyed.
The phagocyte is attracted to the pathogen by chemicals it releases and recognises its antigens as foreign; it engulfs the pathogen, enclosing it in a vacuole/vesicle/phagosome; a lysosome fuses with the phagosome, emptying its enzymes into it; the pathogen is digested/hydrolysed by lysozymes; peptides/antigens from the pathogen are then displayed on the phagocyte's own cell membrane, making it an antigen-presenting cell.
❓ A student writes: "the virus fuses with the lysosome and is destroyed." Identify and correct the error.
It is the phagosome (containing the pathogen) that fuses with the lysosome, not the pathogen itself; the lysosome then releases lysozymes into the phagosome to digest the pathogen.
❓ Explain how the mechanism of phagocytosis is reused when a cell takes up an antibody-drug conjugate.
The cell ingests/engulfs the antibody (or the cell membrane surrounds it — endocytosis); lysosomes fuse with the resulting vesicle/phagosome; lysozymes break down/digest the antibody, releasing the drug it was carrying.

Cellular response (T cells)

❓ Describe the cellular response to a foreign antigen, starting from an antigen-presenting cell.
A pathogen is engulfed by a phagocyte, which displays the antigen on its surface, becoming an antigen-presenting cell; a helper T cell with a complementary receptor protein binds to the antigen; this activates the helper T cell, which divides by mitosis to form a clone; the cloned helper T cells stimulate a specific B cell, stimulate cytotoxic T cells, stimulate phagocytes to carry out more phagocytosis, and develop into memory cells.
❓ Explain how a cytotoxic T cell destroys a virus-infected body cell.
The cytotoxic T cell produces perforin, which makes holes in the cell-surface membrane of the infected cell; the membrane becomes freely permeable; the cell dies.
❓ A question asks only about "the humoral response." A candidate begins their answer with "a B cell binds to an antigen." Explain why this loses the opening mark.
The opening mark point is consistently the presentation of antigen on an antigen-presenting cell (a phagocyte or infected body cell), not the B cell itself — even a question about the humoral response must begin with this antigen-presentation step.

Humoral response & antibody structure

❓ Describe the humoral response, from antigen display to antibody secretion.
The antigen is displayed on antigen-presenting cells/phagocytes/B cells; a B cell with a complementary antibody on its surface binds to the antigen, forming an antigen-antibody complex; a specific helper T cell stimulates the specific B cell; the B cell divides by mitosis — clonal selection; plasma cells secrete antibodies; some B cells develop into memory cells.
❓ Define an antibody and describe its structure.
A protein produced by plasma cells (derived from B lymphocytes) in response to a specific antigen, with a binding site complementary to that antigen; structurally, four polypeptide chains — two heavy and two light — held together in a Y shape by disulfide bridges, with a constant region, two variable regions forming the binding sites, and a hinge region giving flexibility.
❓ Name the organelles most directly responsible for producing and secreting antibodies.
The rough endoplasmic reticulum / ribosomes, and the Golgi apparatus / vesicles; the nucleus is not credited, and the abbreviation "ER" is not accepted — it must be written in full.

Antigen-antibody complex

❓ Explain how antibodies lead to the destruction of a bacterial pathogen.
Antibodies bind to the antigen and act as markers/opsonins; because each antibody has two binding sites, it can bind two pathogens at once, causing them to clump together — agglutination; the clump is too large to enter cells and is easily located and engulfed by phagocytes.
❓ Explain why a given antibody only binds to one specific antigen.
The antibody has a specific tertiary structure; its binding site/variable region is complementary in shape to only one antigen; that antigen is only found on certain cells, so the antibody only binds to and forms an antigen-antibody complex with those cells.
❓ A patient is exposed to the same pathogen twice, six months apart. Explain why they show no symptoms the second time.
Memory cells produced during the primary response recognise the antigen immediately and divide rapidly into plasma cells; this secondary response produces a higher concentration of antibody, more rapidly, than the primary response; the pathogen is destroyed before symptoms have time to develop.

Vaccination & herd immunity

❓ Explain how a vaccine provides protection against future infection.
The vaccine contains an antigen from the pathogen, displayed on antigen-presenting cells; a specific helper T cell binds to the antigen and stimulates a specific B cell; the B cell divides by mitosis to form a clone of plasma cells, which secrete antibody; memory cells are also produced; on re-infection, memory cells give a faster, larger secondary response, destroying the pathogen before symptoms appear.
❓ Explain why vaccinating a population against measles can achieve herd immunity, but vaccinating against tetanus cannot.
Measles is transmissible between people, so a high enough vaccination rate stops it spreading and protects even unvaccinated individuals — herd immunity. Tetanus is contracted from soil through a wound, not from person to person, so vaccinating others provides no protective effect for the unvaccinated — only the vaccinated individual is protected.
❓ Suggest one reason a disease could reappear in a population that was previously well vaccinated against it.
Vaccination uptake has fallen, allowing more unvaccinated individuals into the population, or the pathogen has mutated (antigenic variability), meaning the existing vaccine no longer matches the circulating antigen.

Active & passive immunity

❓ Distinguish between active and passive immunity, in terms of memory cells and duration.
Active immunity involves memory cells and is long-lasting, because antibody is produced by the individual's own plasma cells in response to the antigen; passive immunity does not involve memory cells and is short-lived, because the antibody is introduced from an outside source and is broken down without being replaced.
❓ A newborn baby is protected against certain diseases for a few months due to antibodies received in breast milk. Explain the type of immunity this represents, and why it doesn't last.
This is passive immunity: the antibodies are introduced from an external source (breast milk) rather than produced by the baby's own plasma cells, so no memory cells are formed; the given antibody is gradually broken down and not replaced, so protection is only temporary.
❓ Explain why antivenom provides fast-acting but short-term protection against snake venom toxins.
Antivenom contains ready-made antibodies introduced directly into the body, so no time is needed for clonal selection — protection is immediate; however, no memory cells are formed and the antibody itself is eventually broken down and not replaced, so protection does not persist.

HIV structure & replication

❓ Describe the structure of HIV.
RNA as its genetic material; reverse transcriptase, an enzyme; a protein capsid made of capsomeres; a (phospho)lipid viral envelope derived from a host cell membrane; attachment proteins studded on the envelope.
❓ A student labels a diagram of HIV with "nucleus" and "cell wall." Explain why both labels are incorrect.
HIV is not a cell, so it has neither a nucleus nor a cell wall; its genetic material (RNA) is enclosed in a capsid, and its outer layer is a lipid envelope, not a cell wall or cell membrane.
❓ Describe how HIV replicates once it has entered a helper T cell.
Attachment proteins bind to receptors on the helper T cell, allowing viral RNA to enter the cell; reverse transcriptase converts the RNA into DNA, which is inserted into the host cell's own DNA; the cell then produces new viral protein/capsid/enzymes using its own machinery; new virus particles are assembled and released to infect further cells.

HIV, AIDS & antibiotics

❓ Explain how HIV infection eventually leads to the symptoms associated with AIDS.
HIV destroys (or reduces the number of) helper T cells; fewer/no B cells are activated/stimulated to divide into plasma cells, so less/no antibody is produced; cytotoxic T cells and phagocytes are also not properly stimulated; the person can no longer fight off opportunistic infections and certain cancers, and these — not HIV directly — cause the symptoms of AIDS.
❓ Explain why a course of antibiotics would not be an effective treatment for a viral infection such as HIV.
Antibiotics work by inhibiting enzymes involved in building the bacterial cell wall, or by disrupting bacterial ribosomes and metabolic pathways; viruses have no cell wall, no ribosomes and no independent metabolic pathways of their own, using the host cell's machinery instead, so there is no bacteria-specific target for an antibiotic to act on.
❓ A student writes: "HIV makes the person immune to fighting infections, because it is a very strong bacterium." Identify two errors.
HIV is a virus, not a bacterium; and the correct description is that HIV weakens the immune system by destroying helper T cells, reducing the person's ability to mount an immune response — it does not make them "immune" to anything.

Monoclonal antibodies

❓ Define a monoclonal antibody.
Antibodies with the same tertiary structure, produced from identical/cloned plasma cells or B cells — best understood in the context of a single plasma cell/B lymphocyte being cloned.
❓ Explain how an antibody-drug conjugate reduces side effects compared with a conventional drug given directly.
A cytotoxic drug is attached to a monoclonal antibody that is complementary to an antigen found specifically on the target cells (e.g. cancer cells); the drug is delivered only to cells carrying that antigen, so a lower dose can be used and healthy cells are largely unaffected, reducing side effects.
❓ Explain how a fluorescently-labelled monoclonal antibody can be used to identify a specific cell type for diagnosis.
The target protein is located in the cell-surface membrane; the antibody is complementary to it and binds, forming an antigen-antibody complex; cells carrying that protein are then identified by the dye/stain/fluorescent marker linked to the antibody.

Production — not required

❓ A student asks whether they need to memorise the hybridoma technique for producing monoclonal antibodies. What should they revise instead?
The commercial or scientific production method is explicitly not required by the specification; they should instead focus on the definition of a monoclonal antibody, its therapeutic and diagnostic uses, and the ethical issues associated with its production and use.
❓ Explain why an ethics question can still legitimately mention "mice were used to produce the antibodies," even though production isn't examinable.
The production method itself (the technical steps) is not required content, but the ethical consequences of that production — including the use of animals — are a separate, examinable part of the specification's ethical-issues statement.

Ethical issues

❓ Discuss ethical issues associated with the use of vaccines.
Vaccines are tested on animals and then on human volunteers who may be put at risk; there are side effects, so a person may be harmed by a vaccine for a disease they might never have caught; supply may be limited, raising questions of who is vaccinated first; making vaccination compulsory raises questions of individual freedom versus herd immunity; some vaccines use fetal tissue or animal products; cost raises questions of equitable access — balanced against the public-health benefit of herd immunity and disease prevention at population scale.
❓ Discuss ethical issues associated with the use of monoclonal antibodies in cancer therapy.
Production involves animals (e.g. mice), which may die during testing; human trials have caused deaths and serious side effects in some cases; treatment is expensive, limiting access; informed consent is difficult to obtain from very ill patients — balanced against the treatment's high specificity, meaning fewer side effects than conventional chemotherapy, and its effectiveness in conditions with few alternatives.

The ELISA test

❓ Describe how an indirect ELISA test is used to detect antibodies against HIV in a patient's blood sample.
The HIV antigen is fixed to the surface of a well; a sample of the patient's blood plasma is added, and if the patient has made antibodies against the antigen, they bind, forming an antigen-antibody complex; the well is washed to remove unbound antibody; a second antibody, with an enzyme attached, complementary to the patient's antibody, is added and binds; the well is washed again; a substrate is added, and a colour change indicates the enzyme, and therefore the patient's antibody, was present.
❓ Explain why a well must be washed between each stage of an ELISA test.
Washing removes any unbound antibody, showing that only enzyme still present (bound in a complex) is causing the colour change; without this step, unbound antibody-enzyme could cause a colour change even where the target antigen or antibody is not actually present, giving a false positive.
❓ A direct ELISA test produces no colour change. Explain what this result means.
The target antigen is not present in the sample; so the enzyme-linked antibody has nothing to bind to and is washed away, meaning the enzyme is not present when the substrate is added, and no colour change occurs.
