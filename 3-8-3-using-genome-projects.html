Using Genome Projects

How the genome and proteome are precisely defined, how sequencing a simple organism's genome lets you derive its proteome (and find vaccine antigens), why complex organisms are much harder to decode, and how comparing genomes — between species, within a species, and against an organism's own proteome — drives evolutionary, medical and ethical questions.

⚠️

The single habit that protects the most marks on this spec point: genome and proteome are two separate, tightly worded definitions, and almost this whole topic is examined through them. Genome = the complete set of genes, or all the DNA, in a cell/organism — never "within a species or population," because a genome belongs to one cell or organism, not a group. Proteome = the range/number of different proteins a cell can produce — bare "number of proteins" is never credited. The second most costly slip: describing a vaccine as containing antibodies rather than the correct antigens.

Section 1 · spec content point

🧬 Sequencing projects & defining the genome

This spec point opens with a simple factual statement, but almost every mark that comes from it actually tests one thing hiding underneath: whether you can define the genome with word-perfect precision.

📖

What the specification says: "Sequencing projects have read the genomes of a wide range of organisms, including humans."

1
2
3

Imagine every living thing has its own instruction book, written using a four-letter alphabet, that tells its cells how to build and run it. A "genome" is one complete copy of that book, belonging to just one living thing (or even just one of its cells) — never to a whole group of living things sharing a book between them. Scientists have now "read" — worked out, letter by letter — the entire instruction book of loads of different living things, including humans.

  • Genome = one complete copy of the instruction book (all the DNA)
  • Sequencing = reading the book, letter by letter
  • Belongs to one cell or one organism — never a whole species

A genome sequencing project works out the exact order of DNA bases that make up an organism's entire genome. This has now been done for a huge range of organisms, including humans, through projects like the Human Genome Project. The key thing to get exactly right is the definition of "genome" itself: it's the complete set of genes, or all of the DNA, that belongs to one cell or one organism. It's tempting to describe a genome as belonging to a species or a population, but that's wrong — a genome is something one individual cell or organism has, not something a whole group shares.

PhraseCredited?
"The complete set of genes in a cell/organism"Yes
"All the DNA in a cell/organism"Yes
"All the DNA/genes within a species/population"No — the genome belongs to an individual, not a group

Define the genome, precisely

The complete set of genes, or all the DNA, in a cell / organism. This is also acceptable phrased as "all the genes / alleles / genetic material / genetic code in a cell / organism / person," or as "the total number of DNA bases in a cell / organism."

Accepted phrasingRejected phrasing
"...in a cell / organism / person""...within a species"
All the genes / alleles / genetic material / genetic code"...within a population"
The total number of DNA bases in a cell / organismAnything that describes a genome as shared across a group

What a sequencing project is, in context: it has read (determined the complete DNA base sequence of) the genomes of a wide range of organisms — bacteria, plants, animals and humans among them. Sequencing this many genomes at all is only possible because of how sequencing technology has changed — covered fully as its own spec point in Section 4.

📌

Why this matters later: every later application in this spec point — deriving a proteome, comparing genomes between or within species, screening or diagnosis — starts from the same premise: that a genome can be read accurately, and that many genomes, not just one, can now be read at scale.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "within a species" or "within a population"

This is the single most common way this one-mark recall item is lost. A genome belongs to one cell or one organism — never to a species or a population as a whole. If you find yourself writing "the genome of a species," stop and rewrite it as "the genome of an organism/cell" instead.

Section 2 · spec content point

💉 From genome to proteome in simple organisms: the vaccine chain

This section links three ideas in one causal chain: the second word-perfect definition on this spec point (the proteome), why that translation is comparatively easy in a simple organism, and the real-world payoff — identifying vaccine antigens.

📖

What the specification says: "Determining the genome of simpler organisms allows the sequences of the proteins that derive from the genetic code (the proteome) of the organism to be determined. This may have many applications, including the identification of potential antigens for use in vaccine production."

1
2
3

Once scientists have read a simple germ's entire instruction book (its genome), they can work out the full list of ingredients it can make (its proteome) — because in a simple organism, almost every instruction directly makes one ingredient, with hardly any blank pages in between. Some of those ingredients sit on the outside of the germ, like a uniform. Scientists pick out that uniform pattern (an antigen) and use it to make a vaccine — basically a "wanted poster" that trains your body to recognise the real germ instantly if it ever turns up. The vaccine itself isn't the weapon (antibodies) — it's the poster that teaches your body to build its own weapons.

  • Proteome = the full list of different ingredients (proteins) a cell can make
  • Antigen = the "uniform" pattern on the outside of a germ
  • Vaccine = built from antigens, not antibodies

Once a simple organism's genome has been sequenced, its proteome — the full range of different proteins it can make — can be worked out from the genetic code, because in a simple organism there's very little in the genome that doesn't code for a protein. This matters medically: some of those proteins sit on the surface of a pathogen and can act as antigens, molecules the immune system can learn to recognise. Identifying those surface proteins is the basis of vaccine production — the vaccine itself contains (or triggers production of) antigens, and it's the body's own immune system that then makes antibodies and memory cells in response, not the vaccine.

  1. Sequence the pathogen's genome
  2. Use the genetic code to derive its proteome
  3. Identify surface proteins as potential antigens
  4. Use those antigens to build a vaccine
  5. The body's own immune system responds — making antibodies and memory cells

Define the proteome, precisely

The range / number of different proteins that a cell is able to produce (at a given time), or the range of proteins the genome/DNA is able to code for. A bare "number of proteins" is not credited — the word different, or "range of," is doing the real work in this definition. As with the genome, a proteome belongs to a cell/organism, not to a species or population.

The vaccine-production chain

Sequence the pathogen's genome → use the genetic code to derive its proteome (the amino acid sequences of its proteins) → identify which proteins sit on the pathogen's surface, since these can act as antigens → use those antigens to build a vaccine that stimulates an immune response and the production of memory cells.

Sequence the pathogen's genome, derive its proteome, identify surface antigens, then build a vaccine from those antigens. The body's own immune system — not the vaccine — makes the antibodies and memory cells.

Why simpler organisms are tractable

Prokaryotes and viruses have little or no non-coding DNA, few regulatory genes, and (in prokaryotes) no introns — so their genome can be translated fairly directly into their proteome using the genetic code. That's exactly what makes the vaccine chain above possible for a simple pathogen, and it sets up the sharp contrast with complex organisms covered in Section 3.

▶ 🚩 Trap — a vaccine is not antibodies

Never write that a vaccine contains antibodies. A vaccine contains (or is built from, or codes for) antigens; the body's own immune system is what then produces the antibodies and memory cells in response. This is the second most costly slip on this spec point after the genome/species error in Section 1.

Section 3 · spec content point

🧩 Why complex organisms are harder to decode

The direct genome-to-proteome translation from Section 2 breaks down in complex organisms — and the reason has a name: two specific features the specification wants named, plus two further mechanisms that explain why a proteome ends up bigger than its genome.

📖

What the specification says: "In more complex organisms, the presence of non-coding DNA and of regulatory genes means that knowledge of the genome cannot easily be translated into the proteome."

1
2
3

A simple organism's instruction book is nearly all useful instructions. A complex organism's book has loads of blank or scribbled-out pages mixed in (non-coding DNA), and a set of "editor" pages that decide which instructions actually get followed and when (regulatory genes) — so just reading the book doesn't tell you the finished list of ingredients any more. On top of that, one single instruction can be read in more than one way (alternative splicing) and the ingredient can get tweaked after it's made (post-translational modification) — so one instruction can end up making more than one final ingredient. That's why a complex organism's final ingredient list (its proteome) is bigger than its instruction count (its genome).

In a complex organism, much of the DNA doesn't code for protein at all — non-coding DNA — and regulatory genes control which of the remaining genes are actually switched on, in which cell, and when. Both of these mean you can't just "read off" the proteome from the genome the way you can in a simple organism. There's a second reason the proteome ends up even bigger than the genome: the same gene's transcript can be spliced in different ways (alternative splicing) to make more than one mature mRNA, and proteins can be chemically altered after they're made (post-translational modification) — so a single gene can end up producing several different final proteins.

BarrierEffect
Non-coding DNADoesn't code for protein; hard to tell which sequences do
Regulatory genesControl which genes are expressed, and when — not all genes are "on" at once
Alternative splicingOne gene → more than one mature mRNA → more than one protein
Post-translational modificationA protein is altered after translation, adding further variety

The two named barriers

The specification names both explicitly, and a full answer needs both: non-coding DNA (introns within genes, and repeats between genes) and regulatory genes / transcription factors, which control which genes are actually expressed, in which cell, and when. Together, these mean the presence of a gene in the genome does not guarantee its protein appears in the proteome of any given cell at any given time.

Why the proteome ends up bigger than the genome

Alternative splicing — the same pre-mRNA transcript can have different combinations of exons joined together, producing different mature mRNAs, and therefore different protein products from a single gene. Post-translational modification — a protein is chemically altered after translation (for example in the Golgi apparatus), adding further variety. Together, a single gene can give rise to more than one protein, so an organism's proteome (its total number of different proteins) is typically greater than the number of genes in its genome.

The same pre-mRNA is spliced two different ways: mRNA 1 skips exon 3, mRNA 2 skips exon 2. Different mature mRNAs give different amino acid sequences, so a single gene yields more than one protein — plus post-translational modification adds further variety after translation. Result: proteome (protein count) > genome (gene count). Schematic, not exact data.

Apply it: why can't a prokaryote make a human protein correctly?

A prokaryote cannot splice pre-mRNA (so it cannot remove introns from a eukaryotic gene), lacks a Golgi apparatus (so it cannot carry out the same post-translational modification), and lacks the required eukaryotic transcription factors.

🔗

Synoptic link — why gene technology uses cDNA, not genomic DNA: this is exactly the limitation that motivates using recombinant cDNA (made from mature mRNA, so already free of introns) rather than genomic DNA in gene technology (3.8.4.1) — a prokaryote host cell can express cDNA correctly precisely because the intron problem has already been removed before the gene ever reaches it.

▶ Precise wording worth reusing

When explaining why the proteome is hard to determine in a complex organism, state all of: (1) much of the DNA is non-coding — introns within genes and repeats between genes; (2) regulatory genes and transcription factors control which genes are expressed, and when; (3) pre-mRNA is spliced, and the same gene can be spliced differently to give different polypeptides; (4) proteins are modified after translation (e.g. in the Golgi apparatus). The two words the specification names explicitly are "non-coding DNA" and "regulatory genes" — make sure both appear in any answer to this spec point.

Section 4 · spec content point

⚙️ Sequencing methods: continuously updated and automated

A short spec statement, but it's the enabling fact behind everything else in this topic — none of the large-scale comparison or screening work in Section 5 would be practical without it.

📖

What the specification says: "Sequencing methods are continuously updated and have become automated."

1
2
3

Reading a whole instruction book used to be slow, expensive work done almost entirely by hand. Now machines do most of the reading, and those machines keep getting better — so today, reading a whole genome is fast and cheap enough that scientists can do it again and again, for lots of different people or organisms, instead of just once ever.

Sequencing technology hasn't stayed still — the methods used to read a genome are continuously updated, and much of the process has become automated, meaning machines rather than manual lab work now carry out most of the reading. The practical effect is that sequencing a whole genome is now fast and relatively cheap. That combination of speed, low cost and automation is what turns genome sequencing from a one-off scientific milestone into a routine tool that can be applied at scale — to many organisms, or to many individuals within one species.

Why sequencing has become faster and cheaper

Sequencing methods are continuously updated and have become automated. That automation, cost and speed context is the whole reason later applications become possible: personalised medicine and large-scale comparative genomics both need sequencing many genomes — not just one — to be practical, fast and affordable enough to use routinely.

📌

Why this matters later: automation is the enabling fact behind every application in Section 5 — comparing genomes between species, comparing genomes within a species, and screening/diagnosis all depend on sequencing being cheap enough to do at scale, not just once.

▶ How this gets examined

This point is usually tested as a short "explain why sequencing is now fast/cheap" recall item, or folded into a longer question about why a particular large-scale application (screening a population, comparing many species) is now realistic. The expected answer is short: methods are continuously updated, and have become automated — don't overcomplicate it with unnamed specific technologies, since none are required by the specification.

Section 5 · applying the content

🔍 Comparing genomes & named applications

This is the single hardest routing decision on this spec point: the same underlying skill — comparing DNA base sequences — answers two completely different question types, depending only on who is being compared. Getting the scale right matters more than any individual fact here.

Name techniques used to compare DNA between organisms

Accepted answers include: PCR (the polymerase chain reaction), genetic / DNA fingerprinting, gel electrophoresis, or DNA / genome sequencing — a low-tariff item that tests breadth of recall across the whole gene-technologies toolkit (3.8.4), not depth on any one method.

Comparing genomes BETWEEN species — relatedness and gene function

Comparing DNA base sequence similarity between two different species' genomes (or specific homologous genes) shows how closely related they are: a more similar sequence means a more recent common ancestor — the same molecular-evidence logic used in 3.4.5. The same comparison can identify a gene's likely function in a newly-sequenced, less-studied organism — a close match to a gene of known function in a well-studied model organism (for example, a human) suggests a similar function.

Comparing genomes WITHIN a species — personalised medicine

Comparing genomes between individuals of the same species identifies genetic differences at specific loci — for example, alleles or mutations linked to disease risk, or that affect how an individual responds to a particular drug (pharmacogenomics). This underpins personalised medicine: treatment (drug choice or dosage) tailored to an individual's own genome, rather than a single dose or drug given to everyone.

Point of comparisonBetween different speciesWithin one species, between individuals
What similarity/difference showsMore similar sequence = more recent common ancestor = more closely relatedA differing allele at one locus, against an otherwise near-identical genome
What it's used forEstablishing relatedness; inferring gene function from a well-studied model organismPharmacogenomics — predicting drug response; personalised medicine

▶ 🚩 Trap — blurring the two comparison scales

Keep this distinct: comparing between species establishes relatedness and gene function; comparing within a species, between individuals, underpins personalised medicine. Blurring the two scales loses the reasoning mark even when the vocabulary used is otherwise correct.

Named real-world uses of genome sequencing

  • Identifying potential antigens → vaccine development
  • Monitoring mutation / antigenic variability → pathogen tracking
  • Tailoring drug choice/dose to an individual → personalised medicine
  • Finding disease-associated alleles → screening / diagnosis
  • Comparing sequences across species → classification / evolutionary comparison
  • Finding a protein a drug can act on → drug-target identification
▶ 🚩 Trap — a vague "new technology" answer

A vague "new technology" or "genome sequencing" answer, without naming an actual technique or use from the list above, is not credited — a recurring, examiner-flagged cause of lost marks. Always name a specific, concrete application.

Evaluate the ethics of genome sequencing/screening

This is an Evaluate command word — a one-sided answer is capped, so a full answer states points on both sides.

Section 6 · exam technique

🎯 Common mistakes & precise wording

This spec point is short in content but examined at high precision — nearly every mark rides on exact terminology. These are the slips that come up again and again.

▶ Where this point sits in difficulty terms

Memorisation load is low — four short spec sentences and two tightly worded definitions — but application depth is medium-to-high: distinguishing between-species and within-species comparison, tracing the vaccine chain accurately, and applying the prokaryote-limitation reasoning all require genuine understanding, not just recall. Terminology precision risk is high: the genome/proteome definitions are short but exactingly worded, and examiner reports show genome sequencing and molecular sequencing methods are commonly conflated in "how would you investigate relatedness" questions, with many students vaguely citing "new technology" instead of an actual technique.

Section 7 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

3.8.3 supplies the "read the genome, derive the proteome" strand of any DNA-technology essay — the trick is closing each paragraph with a sentence that ties the mechanism back to whatever the title is actually about, rather than repeating a generic summary.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around vaccine development

Once a pathogen's genome has been sequenced, its proteome can be derived using the genetic code, since simpler organisms have little non-coding DNA and few regulatory genes, so their genome translates fairly directly into their proteome. Identifying which of those proteins sit on the pathogen's surface allows scientists to select potential antigens for vaccine production; the vaccine itself introduces these antigens, and it is the body's own immune system that then produces antibodies and memory cells. This shows how a purely genetic technique — sequencing — can be converted directly into a medical application.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around evolutionary relationships

Automated, continuously updated sequencing methods have made it practical to sequence and compare genomes across a wide range of species. Comparing the base sequence similarity of homologous genes between two species provides molecular evidence for how closely related they are — a more similar sequence indicates a more recent common ancestor. This is a modern, molecular extension of the same evolutionary reasoning traditionally based on anatomical or fossil evidence.

3.8.3 links naturally to 3.4.5 (genome sequencing and immunology as modern evidence for evolutionary relationships), 3.4.7 (comparing DNA/protein sequences to investigate genetic diversity), and 3.8.4.1 / 3.8.4.2 (recombinant DNA technology and DNA probes/personalised medicine) — use it as the "read the genome" opening move of a DNA-technology essay, then hand off explicitly to those spec points for how that sequence information is written, transferred, screened or used to treat an individual. Naming the vaccine-antigen chain or the splicing/post-translational-modification reasoning is more convincing than asserting "genome sequencing is important" without mechanism.

🧠

Memory hook. Genome: one cell/organism, never "a species." Proteome: "range/number of different proteins," never bare "number of proteins." Between species → relatedness and gene function. Within species, between individuals → personalised medicine (pharmacogenomics). Genome vs proteome → proteome is BIGGER, because of alternative splicing (different exon combinations, one gene → several mRNAs → several proteins) plus post-translational modification. And always: vaccine = antigens, never antibodies.

Self-check before moving on

  • I can define the genome as belonging to a cell/organism, never "a species or population".
  • I can define the proteome as the range/number of different proteins a cell can produce, not bare "number of proteins".
  • I can trace the vaccine-production chain: genome → proteome → surface antigens → vaccine (not antibodies).
  • I can explain why simple organisms' genomes translate more directly into their proteome (little non-coding DNA, few regulatory genes, no introns in prokaryotes) than complex organisms' do.
  • I can explain, using alternative splicing and post-translational modification, why a proteome typically contains more proteins than its genome has genes.

5-Minute Cram

1 · Sequencing projects & the genome

  • Sequencing projects have read the genomes of a wide range of organisms, including humans.
  • Genome = complete set of genes / all the DNA in a cell/organism. Never "within a species/population".
  • Accept: all the genes/alleles/genetic material/genetic code; or total number of DNA bases in a cell/organism.

2 · Genome → proteome: the vaccine chain

  • Proteome = range/number of different proteins a cell can produce. Bare "number of proteins" is not credited.
  • Chain: sequence genome → derive proteome → identify surface antigens → build vaccine → body makes antibodies + memory cells.
  • Vaccine ≠ antibodies — it's built from antigens.
  • Simple organisms are tractable: little/no non-coding DNA, few regulatory genes, no introns in prokaryotes.

3 · Why complex organisms are harder

  • Two named barriers: non-coding DNA and regulatory genes.
  • Proteome > genome because of alternative splicing (one gene → several mRNAs) and post-translational modification.
  • Prokaryote can't make a human protein: no splicing (can't remove introns), no Golgi (no PTM), no eukaryotic transcription factors.
  • This is why cDNA, not genomic DNA, is used in gene technology (3.8.4.1).

4 · Automated, updated sequencing

  • Sequencing methods are continuously updated and have become automated → fast and cheap.
  • This is the enabling fact behind large-scale comparative genomics and personalised medicine.

5 · Comparing genomes & applications

  • Techniques to compare DNA: PCR, DNA/genetic fingerprinting, gel electrophoresis, DNA/genome sequencing.
  • Between species → relatedness / gene function (more similar = more recent common ancestor).
  • Within species, between individuals → personalised medicine / pharmacogenomics. Never blur the two scales.
  • Named uses: vaccine development, pathogen tracking, personalised medicine, screening/diagnosis, classification/evolution, drug-target ID.
  • Ethics = Evaluate: balance benefits (earlier diagnosis, targeted treatment) against concerns (confidentiality, discrimination, consent).

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Sequencing projects & the genome

❓ Define the genome.
The complete set of genes, or all the DNA, in a cell/organism. Accept: all the genes/alleles/genetic material/genetic code in a cell/organism/person; accept the total number of DNA bases in a cell/organism. Reject any answer describing the genome as belonging to a species or a population.
❓ A student defines the genome as "all the DNA within a species." Explain what is wrong with this definition.
A genome belongs to one cell or one organism, not to a species or a population as a whole — "within a species" is explicitly rejected by the mark scheme and is the single most common way this recall item is lost.
❓ State what sequencing projects have achieved so far.
They have read (determined the complete DNA base sequence of) the genomes of a wide range of organisms, including humans.

Genome → proteome: the vaccine chain

❓ Define the proteome.
The range/number of different proteins that a cell is able to produce (at a given time), or the range of proteins the genome/DNA is able to code for. Bare "number of proteins" is not credited — it must be qualified as the range/number of different proteins.
❓ Explain how sequencing a pathogen's genome could lead to the production of a vaccine against it.
The pathogen's genome is sequenced and used, via the genetic code, to determine its proteome; proteins found on the surface of the pathogen can be identified as potential antigens; these antigens are then used in a vaccine, which stimulates an immune response and the production of memory cells. The vaccine itself contains/is built from antigens, not antibodies.
❓ A student writes "the vaccine contains antibodies which protect the patient." Correct this statement.
The vaccine contains, or is built from, antigens identified from the pathogen's proteome; it is the patient's own immune system that responds to these antigens by producing antibodies and memory cells.

Why complex organisms are harder

❓ Explain why the proteome of a complex organism cannot easily be determined from its genome.
Much of the DNA is non-coding (introns within genes and repeats between genes), so it does not code for polypeptides and it is difficult to tell which sequences are coding; regulatory genes and transcription factors control which genes are expressed, and when, so not all genes in the genome are expressed in a given cell at a given time.
❓ Explain why a proteome typically contains more proteins than its genome has genes.
Pre-mRNA can be spliced in different ways (alternative splicing), so the same gene can produce more than one mature mRNA and therefore more than one polypeptide; proteins can also be modified after translation (post-translational modification, e.g. in the Golgi apparatus), producing further variation in the final protein.
❓ Suggest why a prokaryote cannot always correctly produce a human protein from a human gene.
A prokaryote cannot splice pre-mRNA, so it cannot remove introns from a eukaryotic gene; it does not have a Golgi apparatus, so it cannot carry out the post-translational modification the protein needs; and it lacks the eukaryotic transcription factors required to transcribe the gene correctly.

Automated, updated sequencing

❓ Explain why genome sequencing is now fast and relatively inexpensive.
Sequencing methods are continuously updated, and have become automated — machines now carry out most of the process rather than manual laboratory work, allowing many genomes to be sequenced quickly and cheaply.
❓ Suggest why automation of sequencing methods was necessary before personalised medicine based on genome comparison became realistic.
Personalised medicine requires comparing the genomes of many individuals, not sequencing a single genome once; automation makes sequencing fast and affordable enough to carry out at the scale this requires.

Comparing genomes & applications

❓ Name two techniques that could be used to compare the DNA of two different organisms.
Any two of: the polymerase chain reaction (PCR); genetic/DNA fingerprinting; (gel) electrophoresis; DNA/genome sequencing.
❓ Explain how comparing the genomes of two different species could provide evidence of how closely related they are.
The base sequences of homologous genes (or genomes) from the two species are compared; a greater similarity in base sequence indicates a more recent common ancestor and therefore a closer evolutionary relationship.
❓ A pharmaceutical company compares the genomes of many patients with the same condition to guide drug dosage. Name this application and explain its purpose.
This is personalised medicine (pharmacogenomics): comparing genomes between individuals of the same species identifies genetic differences at specific loci that affect how a person responds to a drug, allowing treatment (drug choice or dose) to be tailored to an individual's own genome rather than using a single dose for everyone.
