Species and Taxonomy

What actually makes two organisms the same species, why courtship behaviour matters, the eight-taxon hierarchy that classifies every living thing, how binomial names work, and how modern molecular evidence lets biologists test relatedness directly instead of guessing from appearance.

⚠️

The single habit that protects the most marks on this spec point: a species is defined by fertile offspring, never "viable" offspring — a mule is viable but sterile, so horse and donkey stay separate species. The second most costly slip is the taxonomic hierarchy: domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species — eight taxa, in that exact order, and if a diagram uses a letter like "f", your written answer still needs the actual word "family", not the letter.

Section 1 · spec content point

💑 Defining a species & the role of courtship behaviour

One precise definition, and one behaviour whose job is to protect that definition — this section is short, but almost every mark on it depends on getting one exact word right.

📖

What the specification says: "Two organisms belong to the same species if they are able to produce fertile offspring. Courtship behaviour as a necessary precursor to successful mating. The role of courtship in species recognition."

1
2
3

Same species really just means "can make babies who can grow up and have babies of their own." A horse and a donkey can have a baby together (a mule), but that mule can't have babies itself — so horses and donkeys still count as different species. Before animals mate, they often do a little routine called courtship, a bit like a secret handshake, to check they've picked a partner of the right species who is ready to breed. If the handshake goes wrong, the animals just don't mate.

  • Fertile = able to have babies of your own (not just able to survive)
  • Courtship = the behaviour "routine" before mating
  • Species recognition = using courtship to check it's the right partner

Two organisms belong to the same species if they can interbreed to produce offspring that are themselves fertile — able to reproduce. "Viable" offspring (simply alive and healthy) is not enough, because a mule is viable but cannot itself reproduce. Courtship behaviour happens before mating and does several jobs at once: it lets an individual check its partner is the same species, check it's the opposite sex, check it's actually ready to breed, and get both partners' fertility timed together. It works step by step — each partner's action triggers the next partner's response — and it simply stops if one partner responds the wrong way.

Defining a species precisely

A species is a group of organisms able to interbreed to produce fertile offspring. The word "fertile" is not optional — it is the single word that decides whether the definition is fully correct. Offspring that survive and grow (are viable) but cannot themselves reproduce do not satisfy the definition: the classic example is a mule, produced from a horse and a donkey, which is healthy and viable but sterile. Because the mule cannot itself produce offspring, horses and donkeys remain two separate species.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "fertile" vs "viable"

Never substitute "viable" for "fertile" when defining a species. Viable only means the offspring survived; fertile specifically means the offspring can go on to reproduce. This single substitution is one of the most consistently penalised errors on this spec point — always write "fertile offspring", not just "offspring", and not "viable offspring".

Why courtship behaviour matters

Courtship behaviour is a necessary precursor to successful mating, and it does several linked jobs that all protect the species definition above:

  • Species recognition — so an individual doesn't waste reproductive effort mating with the wrong species.
  • Sex recognition — confirms a potential mate is of the opposite sex.
  • Confirms breeding readiness — that the mate is sexually mature and fertile.
  • Synchronises fertility — timing gamete release between the two partners so fertilisation is most likely to succeed.
  • Forms pair bonds — which can support later parental care and successful rearing of offspring.

Courtship works as a stimulus–response chain: each partner's behaviour acts as the stimulus that triggers the next response in the sequence. If a mismatched signal is given — for instance by a member of a different species — the sequence simply halts, because the partner does not respond correctly to it.

▶ 🚩 Trap — don't anthropomorphise courtship

Never write that an animal "knows" or "recognises" the wrong species in a human sense — this scores nothing. Describe courtship mechanistically: a signal stimulates, triggers or leads to the next step, or a mismatched signal simply fails to trigger a response, so courtship stops. Keep the language behavioural and causal, not conscious.

Interpreting courtship-sequence data

A common task presents a numbered courtship sequence for two related species side by side and asks you to reason about it:

Type of taskHow to answer it
Spot a step that is extra or missing when comparing the two sequencesRead both sequences line by line and identify exactly where they diverge — most of the sequence is usually shared, with one differing element.
Explain why courtship stops between the two speciesThe receiving partner does not respond correctly to the mismatched signal, so the stimulus–response chain is broken and courtship (and therefore mating) does not continue.
A specific chemical signal (a pheromone) is involvedName the actual chemical described in the question — a general answer such as "a courtship chemical" or "a hormone" on its own is not precise enough.
🧪

Designing a control for a courtship investigation: keep every variable constant except the one being tested — for example, the presence or absence of a specific visual or chemical stimulus — and compare the response rate against a condition where that one stimulus is withheld or replaced with a uniform/neutral version. A higher response rate specifically when the stimulus is present is what demonstrates its effect.

Section 2 · spec content point

🗂️ Phylogenetic classification: arranging species by ancestry

Classification here isn't arbitrary filing — it's built entirely around one idea: how recently two organisms shared a common ancestor. Three terms need to be defined precisely, not just used loosely.

📖

What the specification says: "A phylogenetic classification system attempts to arrange species into groups based on their evolutionary origins and relationships. It uses a hierarchy in which smaller groups are placed within larger groups, with no overlap between groups. Each group is called a taxon (plural taxa)."

1
2
3

Imagine putting every living thing into a set of boxes-inside-boxes, like Russian dolls or folders on a computer. The biggest box holds huge groups, and inside it are smaller and smaller boxes, right down to one very small box for each individual species. No box is ever shared between two different big boxes — everything has exactly one place it belongs. Each box, whatever size, is called a "taxon". The whole point of arranging things this way is that things placed closer together (in smaller, shared boxes) are actually more closely related by ancestry, not just similar-looking.

  • Taxon = one box/group in the system (plural: taxa)
  • Hierarchy = the boxes-inside-boxes structure itself
  • Phylogenetic = based on evolutionary history/ancestry, not just looks

A phylogenetic classification system sorts species into groups based on how they actually evolved and how they're related to each other — not just on what they look like. It's built as a hierarchy: small groups sit inside bigger groups, and a group never overlaps with another group at the same level — every organism has exactly one place at every level. Each group, at any size, is called a taxon (plural: taxa).

Three definitions worth learning precisely

TermPrecise definition
Phylogenetic classificationA system that arranges species into groups based on their evolutionary origins and relationships — i.e. how recently they shared a common ancestor.
HierarchySmaller groups are placed within larger groups, with no overlap between groups. That "no overlap" phrase is a separately markable part of the definition, not an optional flourish — every organism belongs to exactly one group at each level.
Taxon (plural taxa)Each individual group within the hierarchy, at whatever level.

"No overlap" specifically means that if an organism belongs to one genus, it cannot simultaneously belong to a different genus — the groups at any given rank never share members between them. This is what allows the hierarchy to be drawn as a strict nested structure rather than a loose, overlapping set of categories.

▶ Precise phrasing worth reusing

Two phrases are worth being able to reproduce almost word-for-word: "arranges species into groups based on their evolutionary origins and relationships", and "smaller groups are placed within larger groups, with no overlap between groups." A description of the hierarchy that misses the "no overlap" clause is treated as incomplete.

Section 3 · spec content point

🧬 The eight-taxon hierarchy, in order

This is the one list on this spec point you have to be able to recite cold, top to bottom, without skipping a level or swapping two adjacent ones.

📖

What the specification says: "One hierarchy comprises the taxa: domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus and species."

1
2
3

Think of a postal address written the other way round from usual: continent, then country, then city, then street, then house number — each one narrower than the last, and each one sitting inside the one before it. Biology's version has eight "narrowing" levels instead of five, going from a huge group right down to a single species. Learn the order using the sentence "Dear King Philip Came Over For Good Soup" — the first letter of each word gives you the first letter of each level, in the right order.

  • Domain → Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species

The eight taxa, from broadest to narrowest, are: domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species. Every organism sits inside exactly one group at each of these eight levels, each one nested inside the level above it. The classic memory aid is "Dear King Philip Came Over For Good Soup" — the first letters D, K, P, C, O, F, G, S give the order.

Each rank nests fully inside the one above it. Mnemonic: "Dear King Philip Came Over For Good Soup" — D·K·P·C·O·F·G·S, broadest to narrowest.

▶ 🚩 Trap — three ways this hierarchy loses marks

1. Omitting Domain — some students start the list at Kingdom, skipping the broadest rank entirely. 2. Swapping Order and Family — Order always comes before (is broader than) Family; check the direction, not just the two words. 3. Naming the letter, not the rank — if a labelled diagram uses a letter such as "f" for a rank, a written answer must give the actual word "family", not the letter "f". A labelling exercise and a naming exercise are marked differently, and the letter alone earns nothing in a naming answer.

Section 4 · spec content point

🏷️ Binomial nomenclature

One universal naming system, two words, and a fixed set of formatting rules — short, but tested precisely.

📖

What the specification says: "Each species is universally identified by a binomial consisting of the name of its genus and species, eg, Homo sapiens."

1
2
3

Every species on Earth gets its own two-word "official" name, a bit like a first name and surname, except back to front: the first word is the bigger group it belongs to (its genus), and the second word is its own specific name (its species). Humans are called Homo sapiens — Homo is capitalised, sapiens is lower case, and both words are always written in italics.

  • Genus = first word, capital letter, italic
  • Species = second word, lower case, italic
  • Binomial = the two words together

Every species is identified worldwide by one two-part name — its binomial — made of its genus name followed by its species name. The genus name always starts with a capital letter; the species name is always lower case; both are always written in italics (or underlined if handwritten). For example, Littorina littorea: "Littorina" is the genus, "littorea" is the species.

Every species is universally identified by a two-part name made of its genus and species — binomial nomenclature. Given a binomial such as Littorina littorea: the first word (capitalised) is the genus, Littorina; the second word (lower case) is the species, littorea. Both words together, italicised, form the full binomial.

Formatting ruleDetail
Word orderGenus name first, species name second
CapitalisationGenus: capital first letter. Species: all lower case
StyleBoth words italicised in print (or underlined individually if handwritten)
Worked exampleHomo sapiens — genus Homo, species sapiens

This is typically tested as a short, low-tariff recall item: given an unfamiliar binomial, state which word is the genus and which is the species, confirming you understand the genus-then-species order and the capitalisation/italicisation convention — this is exactly the same logic as the "genus" row of the eight-taxon table in Section 3, since genus and species are the two narrowest taxa.

📌

Why bother with a universal binomial? It gives every species a single, unambiguous name recognised worldwide — avoiding the confusion of different common names being used for the same organism in different regions or languages.

Section 5 · spec content point

🚫 What this spec point does not require

Knowing exactly where the boundary of what's examinable sits is worth marks too — it stops you wasting revision time, and stops you writing content that can't be credited on this spec point.

📖

What the specification says: "Recall of different taxonomic systems, such as the three domain or five kingdom systems, will not be required."

1
2
3

You don't need to memorise what's actually inside each big group — like which living things count as one of "the three domains" or "the five kingdoms". You only need the shelf order (the eight-taxon hierarchy from Section 3) and how to write a two-word name (the binomial from Section 4). It's like knowing a library's shelf-numbering system without having to memorise every single book on every shelf.

  • Three-domain system = one way scientists sometimes group all life into three domains — not examinable in detail here
  • Five-kingdom system = an older way of grouping life into five kingdoms — also not examinable in detail here

You are not expected to recall the detail of specific named taxonomic systems, such as which organisms belong in each of the three domains, or which organisms belong in each of the five kingdoms. What you do need is the eight-taxon hierarchy itself (domain through to species, in order) and the binomial naming convention — those are the two things this spec point actually examines.

The specification explicitly excludes recall of the contents of specific named taxonomic classification systems — for example, which organisms are grouped into which of the three domains, or which organisms are grouped into which of the five kingdoms. This spec point's examinable content is limited to two things: the eight-taxon hierarchy of rank names, in the correct order (Section 3), and the binomial naming convention (Section 4).

📌

What this means for revision time: don't spend time memorising which organisms sit inside "Eukarya" versus other domains, or the defining features of each of the five kingdoms — that recall is not required here. Prioritise being able to recite the eight taxon names in the correct order and apply the binomial rules confidently instead.

▶ Why might a question still mention "three domains" or "five kingdoms"?

These systems may still appear as background or context in a question stem — for instance, naming "Eukarya" as an example of a domain, as in the worked example throughout this page. But any marks available in such a question would be for correctly applying the eight-taxon hierarchy or the binomial rules, not for recalling which organisms belong to each domain or kingdom.

Section 6 · extra depth & exam technique

🔬 Reading relatedness: cladograms & modern molecular evidence

Taxonomic rank and physical appearance are both useful, but they can mislead. This section covers how to read relatedness correctly from a phylogenetic tree, and how DNA and protein comparisons let biologists test relatedness directly.

Deducing relatedness from shared taxonomic rank

Two organisms placed in the same genus are more closely related to each other than either is to an organism in a different genus, because members of the same genus share a more recent common ancestor. This reasoning generalises to any rank: sharing a lower (narrower) taxon always means a more recent common ancestor than sharing only a higher (broader) one.

▶ 🚩 Trap — naming the shared taxon isn't enough on its own

Simply stating "they are in the same family" without the causal link to a more recent common ancestor misses the reasoning mark. A comparative-relatedness answer must be explicit and comparative: "these two organisms are more closely related than either is to the third, because they share a more recent common ancestor" — the shared taxon is the evidence, the common ancestor is the reasoning.

Reading a phylogenetic tree (cladogram)

A rooted cladogram reads left (the oldest common ancestor) to right (the present day). Every branch point represents a common ancestor shared by everything to its right on that branch. The more recent — the further right — the shared branch point between two tips, the more closely related they are, regardless of how similar or different they look.

Reptile and Bird share the most recent common ancestor on this tree (the right-most branch point) — so they are the most closely related pair shown, even though a bird looks very different from a reptile at first glance.

Why molecular evidence is trusted more than appearance alone

Advances in immunology and genome sequencing help clarify evolutionary relationships far more directly than classifying purely on observable physical characteristics can. This matters because unrelated organisms can independently evolve similar features under similar selection pressures — convergent evolution — which can make appearance misleading. Shared molecular sequence similarity, by contrast, is direct evidence of shared ancestry: more similar DNA, mRNA or amino acid sequences indicate a more recent common ancestor and therefore a closer relationship.

Route 1 — comparing DNA (DNA hybridisation)

Base or nucleotide sequences can be compared directly, or by DNA hybridisation:

  1. Heat DNA samples from the two species to break the hydrogen bonds between strands, separating each sample into single strands.
  2. Mix single strands from the two species together, allowing complementary sequences to anneal and form hybrid double strands.
  3. Reheat the hybrid strands, and record the temperature needed to separate them again.
  4. More complementary base pairing — a closer relationship — requires a higher separation temperature.

Route 2 — comparing proteins (the immunological/precipitin method)

Amino acid sequences (primary structure) can be compared directly, or by the immunological precipitin method:

  1. Inject protein or serum from species A into a test animal, which produces antibodies against it.
  2. Extract the antibodies produced (forming an antiserum).
  3. Add serum or protein from species B to this antiserum.
  4. The amount of precipitate formed indicates how similar the two proteins are, and therefore how closely related the two species are.
▶ 🚩 Trap — naming a technique is not the same as describing it

A question asking you to describe how biological molecules are compared to test relatedness wants a working procedure, not just the name of a technique — simply writing "immunological evidence" or "DNA hybridisation" earns no credit on its own. Pick one route (DNA or protein) and describe every mechanistic step in order; going deep on one method scores far better than naming several techniques shallowly.

Which comparison methods are actually valid?

✅ Genuinely valid methods❌ Plausible but invalid
Comparison of observable/measurable characteristics; amino acid sequence; DNA or mRNA base sequence; antibody-binding (immunological) assaysCourtship/behaviour; mutation rate; chromosome number; allele frequency; species richness; index of diversity — none of these directly measures relatedness

The valid methods all share one feature: they involve direct comparison — differences or similarities — of a heritable molecular or measurable characteristic. The invalid options may correlate loosely with evolutionary change, but none of them is itself a direct measure of how closely related two species are.

▶ Where this point sits in difficulty terms

The content itself is short — a definition, a behaviour, a list of eight taxon names, and a naming convention — but three independent wording traps ("fertile" vs "viable"; the correct taxon order; converting a diagram letter into a rank name) are each tested repeatedly and each independently cost marks nationally. Section 8 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

This content works well as the opening, foundational paragraph of a longer essay-style answer on classification or relatedness — it defines the vocabulary the rest of the essay depends on.

▶ Example paragraph — opening a classification-themed essay

Two organisms belong to the same species if they are able to produce fertile offspring, and courtship behaviour acts as a necessary precursor to mating by allowing species recognition. Phylogenetic classification arranges species into a hierarchy of taxa — domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus and species — with smaller groups nested inside larger ones and no overlap between groups; each species is identified by a binomial of its genus and species names, e.g. Homo sapiens. Advances in immunology and genome sequencing increasingly clarify these evolutionary relationships directly, since organisms with more similar DNA, mRNA or amino acid sequences share a more recent common ancestor.

▶ Essay technique

Open with the species definition and the eight-taxon hierarchy as the essay's foundation — they define the vocabulary everything else depends on. Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Species & courtship behaviour

❓ Define what is meant by the term "species".
A group of organisms able to produce fertile offspring. Offspring that are merely viable — alive but incapable of reproducing themselves, as in a mule produced from a horse and a donkey — does not satisfy this definition; the offspring must themselves be fertile.
❓ A student compares the courtship sequences of two closely related bird species and notices species B performs an extra head-bobbing step not seen in species A. Explain how this difference could prevent interbreeding between the two species.
Courtship acts as a stimulus–response chain in which each partner's behaviour is the stimulus that triggers the next response; if a female of species A does not respond correctly to the unfamiliar extra head-bobbing step performed by a male of species B, the courtship sequence stops before mating can occur — preventing gene flow between the two species.
❓ Suggest a suitable control for an investigation into whether a specific chemical secreted by female moths increases the response rate of males.
Expose a separate, comparable group of males to a control stimulus lacking the chemical (for example, a solvent-only or unscented equivalent), while keeping all other variables — such as temperature, light and time of exposure — constant, then compare the response rate between the two groups; a higher response rate specifically when the chemical is present demonstrates its effect.

Phylogenetic classification

❓ Explain what is meant by a "phylogenetic classification system".
A system that arranges species into groups (taxa) based on their evolutionary origins and relationships — i.e. how recently they shared a common ancestor; it is organised as a hierarchy in which smaller groups are nested within larger groups, with no overlap between groups.
❓ Explain why the taxonomic hierarchy is described as having "no overlap between groups".
Each organism belongs to exactly one group at every level of the hierarchy — for example, an organism placed in the genus Homo cannot simultaneously belong to a different, unrelated genus — so groups at any given rank never share members.
❓ What is meant by the term "taxon"?
A taxon is a single group within the classification hierarchy (plural: taxa); each taxon may itself contain one or more smaller taxa nested within it.

The eight-taxon hierarchy

❓ State, in order from broadest to narrowest, the eight taxa that make up the hierarchy used in this classification system.
Domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species.
❓ A student is shown an unlabelled classification diagram in which the taxa are represented only by the letters D, K, P, C, O, F, G and S. State the full name of the rank represented by the letter "F".
Family — the letter alone is not sufficient in a naming question; the full rank name must be given.
❓ Identify the error in the sequence: "domain, kingdom, phylum, class, family, order, genus, species".
Family and order have been swapped — order is the broader taxon and must come before (be listed above) family in the hierarchy.

Binomial nomenclature

❓ The species Littorina littorea is referred to using a binomial. Identify which word is the genus and which is the species.
Littorina is the genus (first word, capitalised); littorea is the species (second word, lower case); both words together, printed in italics, form the full binomial.
❓ Explain why a binomial system of naming is useful for identifying organisms.
It gives every species a single, universal two-part name — its genus and species — so the same organism can be identified unambiguously anywhere in the world, avoiding the confusion of different common names being used in different regions or languages.
❓ Write the binomial name for modern humans, following the correct convention.
Homo sapiens — Homo (the genus) capitalised, sapiens (the species) in lower case, both italicised.

What's excluded here

❓ A question asks a student to name the five kingdoms of a five-kingdom classification system. Explain why this is not something this specification point requires.
The specification explicitly excludes recall of named taxonomic systems, such as the three-domain or five-kingdom systems; only the eight-taxon hierarchy (domain to species) and the binomial naming system are examinable here.
❓ Suggest why a question might still mention a system like "the five kingdoms", even though recall of its contents isn't required.
Such a system might appear only as background or context within a question stem; any marks available would be for correctly applying the eight-taxon hierarchy or binomial rules, not for recalling which organisms belong to each kingdom.

Relatedness & molecular evidence

❓ Two mammal species are found to be in the same genus, while a third mammal species is in a different genus entirely. Explain what this tells you about their relatedness.
The two species placed in the same genus are more closely related to each other than either is to the third species, because members of the same genus share a more recent common ancestor than organisms sharing only a broader taxon such as family or order.
❓ Describe, as a full procedure, how DNA hybridisation can be used to compare the relatedness of two species.
DNA samples from the two species are heated to break the hydrogen bonds between strands, separating each into single strands; single strands from the two species are then mixed, allowing complementary base sequences to anneal into hybrid double strands; the hybrid strands are reheated and the temperature needed to separate them again is recorded; a higher separation temperature indicates more complementary base pairing and therefore a closer evolutionary relationship.
❓ A precipitate forms when antiserum raised against species A's protein is mixed with serum from species B. Explain what the amount of precipitate indicates.
The antibodies in the antiserum bind to the protein from species B where the amino acid sequences are similar to species A's protein, forming a precipitate; the greater the amount of precipitate formed, the more similar the two proteins are, and therefore the more closely related the two species are.
