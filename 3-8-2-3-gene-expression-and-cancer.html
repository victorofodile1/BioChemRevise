Gene Expression and Cancer

How faults in two opposite gene types — and in the methylation that controls them — remove the normal checks on cell division, how the resulting tumours are told apart, and how understanding the two-gene logic feeds into prevention, diagnosis and treatment.

⚠️

The one habit that protects the most marks on this spec point: a mutated tumour suppressor gene stops inhibiting cell division — a loss-of-function fault, brakes failing. A mutated proto-oncogene (an oncogene) causes increased cell division — a gain-of-function fault, accelerator stuck down. These are opposite mechanisms that both end in more cell division — swapping them is the single most common error on this topic. The same swap-risk applies to methylation direction: match hyper-/hypo- to the correct gene type every single time.

Section 1 · spec content point

🔬 Benign and malignant tumours

Whatever the exact wording of the question, this always reduces to the same paired comparisons — and it is paired comparison that earns marks, not two disconnected facts about growth or spread.

📖

What the specification says: "The main characteristics of benign and malignant tumours."

1
2
3

Think of a benign tumour as a weed growing in a plant pot — it stays inside the pot (a capsule), it doesn't send roots into the rest of the garden, and if you lift the pot out, it's gone. A malignant tumour is a weed with roots that push straight through the pot and into the surrounding soil, and bits of root can break off and start new weed patches (secondary tumours) somewhere else in the garden entirely.

  • Tumour = a lump made from a mass of cells that keep dividing when they shouldn't
  • Benign = stays put, usually boxed in, less dangerous
  • Malignant = invades and spreads, this is what "cancer" means
  • Capsule = a membrane "box" around a tumour
  • Metastasis = cells breaking off and starting new tumours elsewhere

A benign tumour tends to grow slowly, stays surrounded by a capsule, and its cells still look and behave fairly normally — so it stays in one place and rarely causes serious damage on its own, though it can still press on nearby organs. A malignant tumour grows quickly, has no capsule holding it in, and its cells look abnormal and poorly specialised. Critically, malignant cells can invade the tissue around them and break away to travel in the blood or lymph, seeding new "secondary" tumours elsewhere in the body — this spreading process is called metastasis, and it's the single biggest reason malignant tumours are more dangerous and more likely to come back after treatment.

CharacteristicBenign tumourMalignant tumour
Growth rateGrows slowlyGrows rapidly
Cell nuclei & differentiationNuclei look normal; cells stay well-differentiatedNuclei are larger and darker (abnormal DNA content); cells become poorly differentiated / dedifferentiated
CapsuleUsually encapsulated — surrounded by a membrane, stays compactUnencapsulated — can grow projections into surrounding tissue

📌

The three most reliably credited points: growth rate, presence of a capsule / spread by metastasis, and degree of differentiation. If a question only wants a short answer, lead with these three.

Metastasis is the single defining feature of malignancy. It is the property that most sharply separates the two tumour types: the ability to invade surrounding tissue and spread elsewhere in the body, via the blood or lymphatic system, to form secondary tumours. A benign tumour, even a large one, stays where it is and does not seed new tumours elsewhere — which is exactly why malignant tumours are considered more dangerous.

▶ 🚩 Trap — stating both sides separately doesn't count as a comparison

Writing "benign tumours grow slowly" on its own, with the malignant side never stated anywhere in the answer, is not credited as a comparison. Both sides of the same characteristic need to be stated together, ideally in one paired sentence — for example "benign tumours grow slowly, whereas malignant tumours grow rapidly," not two separate, disconnected sentences about each tumour type in isolation.

Section 2 · spec content point

🚀 Proto-oncogenes and oncogenes

A proto-oncogene is the cell's accelerator — pressed only when division is genuinely needed. Mutation can jam that accelerator down.

📖

What the specification says: "The role of the following in the development of tumours: tumour suppressor genes and oncogenes." (This section covers the oncogene half of that content point; Section 3 covers the tumour suppressor gene half, using the same specification wording.)

1
2
3

A proto-oncogene is like an accelerator pedal in a car — pressing it makes the cell divide, and normally it's only pressed when the cell actually needs to divide. If the pedal gets jammed down permanently, the car just keeps accelerating whether it needs to or not. That's what happens when a proto-oncogene mutates into an oncogene: the "divide" signal gets stuck on, so the cell keeps dividing over and over, far more than it should.

  • Proto-oncogene = the normal gene, the accelerator pedal
  • Oncogene = the mutated, permanently-pressed version
  • Gain of function = the gene now does its normal job too much, not a brand-new job

A proto-oncogene's normal job is to stimulate cell division at a controlled rate, often by coding for a growth-factor protein or a growth-factor-receptor protein — division only happens when the right growth-factor signal is present. A mutation can permanently switch this gene on, turning it into an oncogene: an over-active version that makes a hyperactive protein continuously, even without the growth-factor signal being there. Because the gene is now doing more of its own normal job rather than a new one, this is called a gain-of-function fault, and the result is excessive, uncontrolled cell division.

Normal proto-oncogeneMutated → oncogene
What it codes forA growth-factor protein, or a growth-factor-receptor proteinA hyperactive, over-expressed version of the same protein
When it's activeOnly when the correct growth-factor signal is presentContinually — active regardless of whether the growth-factor signal is present
Effect on cell divisionStimulates division at a controlled, appropriate rateOver-stimulates division — excessive, uncontrolled cell division
Type of fault—Gain of function — the accelerator is stuck on
The gene isn't doing a new job — it's doing its normal "stimulate division" job too much, too often.
▶ The nuance worth stating precisely

An oncogene does not gain a brand-new function — it does too much of its normal job. A proto-oncogene's job was always to stimulate division; an oncogene just does that job constantly and inappropriately. Describing it as a gene that has "started doing something new" is imprecise, and won't be credited over the correct gain-of-function description.

Section 3 · spec content point

🛑 Tumour suppressor genes

The opposite mechanism to Section 2: a tumour suppressor gene is the cell's brake and safety check — and cancer here comes from that brake failing, not from it being pressed harder.

📖

What the specification says: "The role of the following in the development of tumours: tumour suppressor genes and oncogenes." (Same specification content point as Section 2 — this half covers tumour suppressor genes specifically.)

1
2
3

A tumour suppressor gene is like the brakes on that same car — its whole job is to stop the car (the cell) when something's wrong, or even to write the car off entirely if it's too damaged to be safe (that's apoptosis — programmed cell death). If a mutation breaks the brakes, nothing stops the car anymore, and damaged cells just keep being copied instead of being stopped or destroyed. Most cells carry two copies of this gene — like having a spare brake — so usually both copies need to fail before real trouble starts.

  • Tumour suppressor gene = the brakes / safety check
  • Apoptosis = programmed cell death, a deliberate "delete this cell" signal
  • Loss of function = the brake stops working — the opposite of gain of function

A tumour suppressor gene's normal job is to slow or halt the cell cycle, or to trigger apoptosis of cells that are damaged or abnormal, before they can divide further. Mutation can inactivate the gene — switching it off, or breaking the protein it makes — so it can no longer slow the cycle or trigger apoptosis. This is a loss-of-function fault: the opposite logic to an oncogene, because a normally-restraining function is being removed rather than gained. Because most human cells carry two copies of every gene (they're diploid), a single faulty copy is often masked by the other, still-working copy — which is why cancer usually needs more than one "hit" before control over the cell cycle is fully lost.

Normal tumour suppressor geneMutated → inactivated
What it doesCodes for a protein that slows/halts the cell cycle, or triggers apoptosis of damaged/abnormal cellsGene switched off, or its protein no longer functions
Effect on the cycleRestrains division — a brake or safety check on faulty cellsNo restraint — the cycle is not slowed, damaged cells are not eliminated
Type of fault—Loss of function — brakes failing
📌

Both faults converge on the same outcome. An oncogene (gain of function, excess stimulation) and an inactivated tumour suppressor gene (loss of function, lost restraint) are opposite mechanisms — but both remove control over the cell cycle, and either can lead, alone or together, to uncontrolled/rapid cell division and a tumour, classified as benign or malignant by the Section 1 characteristics.

The "two functional copies" nuance

Most human cells are diploid — heterozygous — so a single faulty copy of a tumour suppressor gene can often be masked by the remaining functional allele, which still works normally. This is why cancer usually needs more than one "hit" before control is fully lost: either both copies become faulty, or one faulty copy is inherited or acquired and the other is then lost or mutated later.

▶ 🚩 Trap — never swap the direction of these two faults

Never state that a tumour suppressor gene is "switched on" to cause cancer, or that an oncogene is "switched off" — that reverses the direction of each fault. A tumour suppressor gene fault is always loss of function (inactivated); an oncogene fault is always gain of function (over-activated). This exact swap is the single most common error examiners report on this topic — commit to one gene type and one direction before you start writing, and don't change direction mid-answer.

Section 4 · spec content point

🧬 Abnormal methylation of tumour suppressor genes and oncogenes

A second route to the same two faults — switching a gene off or on without changing a single base of DNA. This route is very commonly left out of "explain how cancer develops" answers, which costs marks.

📖

What the specification says: "The role of the following in the development of tumours: … abnormal methylation of tumour suppressor genes and oncogenes …"

1
2
3

Imagine each gene has a light switch at the start of it, and that switch can get covered in little stickers (methyl groups). Sticking lots of stickers over the brake gene's switch (the tumour suppressor gene) dims it right off — the brake stops working. Peeling stickers off the accelerator gene's switch (the proto-oncogene) turns it fully on — the accelerator gets pressed. No stickers ever change the gene itself, just whether its switch is covered up or not — and because stickers can be peeled back off again, this kind of fault can, in theory, be undone.

  • Methylation = adding methyl group "stickers" to a gene's switch (promoter)
  • Hypermethylation = more stickers → switch dimmed off
  • Hypomethylation = fewer stickers → switch turned on
  • Reversible = unlike a mutation, this can be undone

Methyl groups attached to a gene's promoter control how much that gene gets transcribed — more methyl groups generally means less transcription. Increased methylation (hypermethylation) of a tumour suppressor gene's promoter switches that gene off, silencing the "brake." Decreased methylation (hypomethylation) of a proto-oncogene's promoter switches that gene on, activating the "accelerator." Neither of these changes the DNA base sequence at all — which is exactly why, unlike a mutation, this kind of fault is reversible, and it's why drugs that reset methylation patterns are a genuinely promising treatment idea.

Epigenetic changeActing on…Effect
Hypermethylation (increased methylation)The promoter of a tumour suppressor geneSwitches it off — silences the "brake" gene, consistent with "more methylation = less transcription"
Hypomethylation (decreased methylation)The promoter/regulatory region of a proto-oncogeneSwitches it on / increases its expression — activates the "accelerator" gene
More methyl groups ("Me") on a tumour suppressor gene's promoter block its transcription; fewer methyl groups on a proto-oncogene's promoter allow/boost its transcription. Neither changes the DNA base sequence.

Because these are epigenetic changes rather than mutations, they alter gene function without changing the base sequence of DNA — and critically, unlike a mutation, they are reversible. This is exactly why epigenetic-based drugs are a promising treatment direction (see Section 6).

▶ 🚩 Trap — leaving methylation out of an "explain" answer

Never give only mutation as the cause of cancer in an "explain" answer — methylation of tumour suppressor genes is frequently the route weaker answers omit entirely. A complete answer needs both the mutation route and this epigenetic route.

▶ 🚩 Trap — never swap the methylation pairing

It is not "hypomethylation silences a tumour suppressor gene," and it is not "hypermethylation activates an oncogene." The fixed pairing is: HYPERmethylation + tumour suppressor gene → OFF, and HYPOmethylation + proto-oncogene → ON.

Section 5 · spec content point

🎗️ Increased oestrogen and breast cancer

The one named real-world route on this spec point — and it only earns marks if it's explicitly tied back to gene expression, not left as a generic "oestrogen raises risk" statement.

📖

What the specification says: "The role of the following in the development of tumours: … increased oestrogen concentrations in the development of some breast cancers."

1
2
3

Oestrogen acts like a key that can unlock certain genes, including some accelerator genes (proto-oncogenes) that tell breast cells to divide. The more of that key is floating around, the more of those doors get unlocked, and the more division genes get switched on — so more cells divide. A treatment that jams the lock (blocks the oestrogen receptor) stops the key from working at all, which slows this down.

  • Oestrogen = a hormone that can act as a "key" for certain genes
  • Transcription factor = a protein/hormone that switches genes on by helping start transcription
  • Oestrogen-receptor-blocking drug = jams the lock so the key can't work

Oestrogen is a steroid hormone that acts as — or activates — a transcription factor: it moves into the nucleus and switches on transcription of particular target genes. If oestrogen concentration rises, this can drive increased activation of proto-oncogenes, meaning more of the "divide" signal gets switched on, and more cell division follows. This has to be explicitly linked to gene expression and transcription-factor activity, not left as a vague "oestrogen increases cancer risk" statement, to earn credit. A named treatment that follows logically from this mechanism is a drug that blocks the oestrogen receptor, so the hormone can no longer switch these genes on.

Oestrogen is a steroid hormone that acts as (or activates) a transcription factor — it moves into the nucleus and stimulates transcription of target genes. Increased oestrogen concentration can drive increased activation of proto-oncogenes, contributing to increased cell division; this mechanism must be linked explicitly to gene expression / transcription-factor activity, not left as a generic risk-factor statement.

StepWhat happens
1Oestrogen concentration in breast tissue rises (locally produced fat-cell oestrogen contributes to this after the menopause)
2Oestrogen acts as/activates a transcription factor, binding to the promoter region of genes that stimulate cell division, including proto-oncogenes
3Transcription of these genes increases
4Increased cell division follows — a tumour can develop
💊

Named treatment consistent with this mechanism: an oestrogen-receptor-blocking drug. It binds the oestrogen receptor, blocking transcription-factor activation and so reducing the stimulation of division that this pathway would otherwise cause.

▶ 🚩 Trap — a generic risk-factor answer isn't enough

An answer like "oestrogen increases cancer risk" scores nothing on its own. The answer must link back to gene expression / transcription-factor activity specifically — exactly as oestrogen's mechanism is described elsewhere on the specification. Naming the process (transcription factor, promoter, proto-oncogene) is what turns a generic statement into a credited one.

Section 6 · spec content point

📊 Evaluating evidence, prevention, diagnosis and treatment

The applied half of this spec point: weighing up correlational evidence properly, and applying the two-gene logic to how cancer is actually screened for, diagnosed and treated.

📖

What the specification says: "Students should be able to evaluate evidence showing correlations between genetic and environmental factors and various forms of cancer; and interpret information relating to the way in which an understanding of the roles of oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes could be used in the prevention, treatment and cure of cancer."

1
2
3

Just because two things happen together doesn't automatically mean one causes the other — like noticing people who carry umbrellas tend to arrive drier, but the umbrella isn't magically stopping rain elsewhere; you'd want to check what else might explain the pattern. That's why any data linking a factor to cancer needs to be checked carefully: was the group studied big enough, was it just one type of person, and could the difference just be down to chance? Doctors also use knowledge of these faulty genes in three practical ways: checking (screening) people for the faulty gene, working out (diagnosing) exactly what kind of tumour someone has, and choosing (treating) with drugs that target the specific fault.

When evaluating a correlation between a factor and cancer, describe the pattern using the actual data given, then state clearly that correlation does not prove causation, then give specific limitations — things like other variables not being controlled, a small sample size, only one population or age group studied, how long the study ran, and whether a proper statistical test showed the difference was actually significant. For prevention, diagnosis and treatment: DNA probes can screen at-risk people for a faulty tumour suppressor gene allele; methylation patterns or gene-expression profiling can diagnose the type of tumour; and treatment can use oestrogen-receptor-blocking drugs or drugs that change methylation patterns in either direction. Methylation-based drugs are promising specifically because epigenetic changes are reversible, unlike changes to the DNA sequence itself.

Correlation is not causation — the evaluation structure to use every time

  1. Describe the correlation using the actual figures given in the data (state whether it's positive or negative, and quote the numbers).
  2. State explicitly that correlation does not establish causation.
  3. Give specific limitations of the study — uncontrolled variables, sample size, a single population or age group, study length, or whether the difference was shown to be statistically significant.
📌

The exact phrasing worth learning: "There is less than a 5% (0.05) probability that the difference is due to chance, so the difference is significant." A vaguer "the results are significant," on its own, is not enough — it is specifically the difference that is significant.

Prevention, diagnosis and treatment — applying the two-gene logic

ApplicationHow it uses the two-gene logic
Prevention / screeningDNA probes can detect mutated tumour-suppressor-gene alleles in at-risk individuals (e.g. those with a family history), allowing monitoring or preventive action
DiagnosisMethylation profiling or gene-expression profiling identifies the type of tumour, so treatment can be targeted
TreatmentOestrogen-receptor-blocking drugs; drugs that alter methylation, in either direction; drugs that inhibit growth-factor receptors

Methylation-altering drugs can work in either direction: reducing methylation of a tumour suppressor gene to re-enable its expression, or increasing methylation of an oncogene to silence it.

💊

Why epigenetic drugs are a promising treatment direction: epigenetic changes, unlike mutations, are reversible — so a drug that resets an abnormal methylation pattern can restore normal gene function, without needing to repair the DNA sequence itself.

▶ 🚩 Trap — a bare yes/no risk-benefit answer scores nothing

Questions asking you to evaluate a treatment with a risk attached require balanced, biologically reasoned points on both sides — for example, weighing quality of life without treatment against the risk of recurrence, or against a treatable side effect. A one-sided evaluation, or a bare "yes" or "no," earns no marks; the marks are specifically for the supporting biological reasoning on each side.

Section 7 · exam technique

🎯 Common mistakes & precise wording

This spec point is testable at high difficulty not because the biology is conceptually hard, but because so much of the mark comes from keeping two opposite mechanisms — and their methylation directions — correctly separated under pressure.

Vague or wrong versionWhat to say instead
"The tumour suppressor gene switches on to cause cancer"It switches off / is inactivated — a loss-of-function fault
"The oncogene switches off"An oncogene is the over-activated, switched-on state — a gain-of-function fault
"Hypomethylation silences a tumour suppressor gene"Hypermethylation silences a tumour suppressor gene; hypomethylation activates a proto-oncogene
"The oncogene gains a new function"It does its normal "stimulate division" job too much, not something new
Only mentioning mutation as a cause of cancerAlso state the abnormal-methylation route — it is very commonly the marks left off
"Uncontrolled growth"Mark schemes want "cell division" stated explicitly, not "growth"
"Oestrogen increases cancer risk" (generic)Link explicitly to gene expression / transcription-factor activity and proto-oncogene activation
"The results are significant""There is less than a 5% (0.05) probability that the difference is due to chance, so the difference is significant"
🧩

The skeleton every question on this spec point reduces to: decide (1) which gene type — tumour suppressor gene or proto-oncogene; (2) which route — mutation, abnormal methylation, or (for breast cancer specifically) increased oestrogen concentration; (3) which direction — gain of function for an oncogene, loss of function for a tumour suppressor gene. Get all three right, in one linked sentence, and the consequence sentence — uncontrolled/rapid cell division, forming a tumour — is the same one every time.

▶ Where this point actually bites, and how to fix it

The underlying two-gene logic is short to state, but keeping its two halves — and their methylation directions — correctly opposite under exam pressure is where marks are actually lost. It bites hardest when confusing tumour suppressor genes with proto-oncogenes/oncogenes in terms of normal function versus mutated state, and when omitting the methylation route entirely in favour of mutation alone. The fix: before writing, commit to one gene type and one direction, and never change direction mid-answer.

Section 8 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

This content works well as a supporting paragraph inside longer essay-style answers, particularly on themes about control mechanisms or the causes of disease — the trick is closing with a sentence that ties the mechanism back to the essay title, not just repeating the two-gene logic again.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around "control in living organisms"

Proto-oncogenes normally stimulate cell division only when the correct growth-factor signal is present, while tumour suppressor genes normally restrain division by slowing the cell cycle or triggering apoptosis of damaged cells. Mutation, or abnormal methylation, of either — permanently activating a proto-oncogene into an oncogene, or inactivating a tumour suppressor gene — removes one of these control points, so cell division is no longer properly regulated and a tumour can form. This shows that control of the cell cycle depends on maintaining a balance between opposing "on" and "off" signals at the level of gene expression, and losing either signal is sufficient to disrupt that balance.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around "mutation as a cause of disease"

Cancer illustrates how a mutation does not need to introduce anything new to cause disease — it can simply push an existing gene too far in its normal direction, as with a proto-oncogene mutating into a permanently active oncogene, or too far away from it, as with an inactivated tumour suppressor gene. Because these are opposite mechanisms converging on the same outcome, uncontrolled cell division, this demonstrates that a single kind of genetic change (mutation) can disrupt very different types of gene, provided the outcome for the cell — loss of a working control point — is the same.

Whichever essay theme this content is used for, treat it as the "mechanism" paragraph: state the process precisely — naming which gene type, which route (mutation or methylation), and which direction (gain or loss of function) — then add one sentence linking it explicitly back to the theme the title is asking about.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Benign & malignant tumours

  • Benign: grows slowly, encapsulated, doesn't invade, doesn't spread, well-differentiated, rarely recurs.
  • Malignant: grows rapidly, unencapsulated, invades, spreads via blood/lymph (metastasis), poorly differentiated, frequently recurs.
  • Metastasis is the single defining feature of malignancy.
  • Always state both sides of the same characteristic together — never as separate, disconnected facts.

2 · Proto-oncogenes & oncogenes

  • Normal role: stimulates division (codes for growth-factor / growth-factor-receptor protein), only when the signal is present.
  • Mutation → permanently ON → oncogene → hyperactive protein made continuously.
  • Gain of function — "accelerator stuck down." Not a new function, just too much of the normal one.

3 · Tumour suppressor genes

  • Normal role: slows/halts the cycle, or triggers apoptosis of damaged cells.
  • Mutation → inactivated → no restraint on the cycle, damaged cells not eliminated.
  • Loss of function — "brakes failing." Opposite mechanism to an oncogene.
  • Diploid cells often mask one faulty copy — usually needs more than one "hit."

4 · Abnormal methylation

  • Hypermethylation of a tumour suppressor gene's promoter → switches it OFF.
  • Hypomethylation of a proto-oncogene's promoter → switches it ON.
  • No change to the DNA base sequence — and because of that, it's reversible, unlike mutation.
  • Never leave methylation out of an "explain how cancer develops" answer — mutation alone is incomplete.

5 · Oestrogen & breast cancer

  • Oestrogen acts as/activates a transcription factor → increases transcription of target genes, including proto-oncogenes.
  • Must be linked explicitly to gene expression / transcription-factor activity, not left generic.
  • Named treatment: an oestrogen-receptor-blocking drug.

6 · Evidence, prevention & treatment

  • Correlation ≠ causation — describe the data, state this explicitly, then give named limitations (sample size, controls, population, study length, statistical significance).
  • Exact phrase: "less than a 5% (0.05) probability the difference is due to chance, so the difference is significant."
  • Prevention: DNA probes screen for mutated alleles. Diagnosis: methylation / gene-expression profiling. Treatment: oestrogen-receptor blockers, methylation-altering drugs (either direction).
  • Risk-benefit evaluations need balanced points on both sides — never a bare yes/no.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Benign & malignant tumours

❓ Give three differences between a benign and a malignant tumour.
Benign tumours grow slowly, whereas malignant tumours grow rapidly; benign tumours are usually encapsulated and do not invade surrounding tissue, whereas malignant tumours are unencapsulated and invade surrounding tissue; benign tumour cells remain well-differentiated, whereas malignant tumour cells are often poorly differentiated / abnormal in appearance. Each point must state both sides together, not as two separate facts.
❓ Explain why metastasis makes a malignant tumour more dangerous than a benign one of the same size.
Malignant tumour cells can invade surrounding tissue and break off to travel via the blood or lymphatic system, forming secondary tumours elsewhere in the body; a benign tumour, even if large, remains localised and does not seed new tumours, so its effects generally stay confined to one site.
❓ A patient's tumour is described as encapsulated, slow-growing and well-differentiated. State, with a reason, whether it is more likely to be benign or malignant.
More likely benign, because these are the defining characteristics of a benign tumour: encapsulation prevents invasion of surrounding tissue, slow growth reflects controlled division, and well-differentiated cells retain a relatively normal appearance and function.

Proto-oncogenes & oncogenes

❓ Explain how mutation of a proto-oncogene can lead to a tumour.
A proto-oncogene normally codes for a growth-factor or growth-factor-receptor protein and stimulates cell division only when the correct signal is present; mutation can permanently activate the gene into an oncogene, producing a hyperactive protein continually, even without the normal signal; this is a gain-of-function fault, resulting in excessive, uncontrolled cell division and the formation of a tumour.
❓ A student describes an oncogene as "a gene that has gained a completely new function." Evaluate this description.
This description is imprecise and would not be credited: an oncogene does not gain a new function, it performs its normal function — stimulating cell division — too much and too often. The correct description is that the gene is permanently over-activated (gain of function), not that it has acquired a new role.
❓ Why is a mutated proto-oncogene classed as a "gain-of-function" fault rather than a "loss-of-function" fault?
Because the mutation increases the activity of the gene's normal product (a growth-stimulating protein) rather than removing it — the gene continues to do its usual job, but constantly and inappropriately, so a function is gained (excess stimulation), not lost.

Tumour suppressor genes

❓ Explain how mutation of a tumour suppressor gene can lead to a tumour.
A tumour suppressor gene normally slows or halts the cell cycle, or triggers apoptosis of damaged or abnormal cells; mutation can inactivate the gene, or produce a non-functional protein, so it can no longer slow the cycle or trigger apoptosis; this is a loss-of-function fault, so damaged cells are no longer eliminated and can divide repeatedly, forming a tumour.
❓ A person inherits one faulty copy of a tumour suppressor gene but shows no signs of cancer for many years. Explain why.
Most human cells are diploid, carrying two copies of each gene; the remaining functional copy continues to work normally and can mask the effect of the single faulty copy; cancer only tends to develop once this second, still-functional copy is also lost or mutated, removing all restraint on the cell cycle.
❓ Explain why describing a mutated tumour suppressor gene as "switched on" would be incorrect.
A mutated tumour suppressor gene is inactivated, not activated — it is switched off, or its protein product no longer functions, so it stops restraining the cell cycle; this is the opposite direction to an oncogene, which is switched permanently on. Describing a tumour suppressor gene fault as "switching on" reverses the correct direction of the fault.

Abnormal methylation

❓ Explain how abnormal methylation could lead to the development of a tumour, referring to both a tumour suppressor gene and an oncogene.
Increased methylation (hypermethylation) of a tumour suppressor gene's promoter inhibits its transcription, silencing the gene so it can no longer slow the cell cycle or trigger apoptosis; decreased methylation (hypomethylation) of a proto-oncogene's promoter increases its transcription, activating the gene so it stimulates cell division continually; neither change alters the DNA base sequence.
❓ Suggest why a drug that reduces methylation of a tumour suppressor gene's promoter could be used as a cancer treatment.
Reducing methylation of the promoter would increase transcription of the tumour suppressor gene, restoring production of its protein; this would restore the gene's ability to slow the cell cycle or trigger apoptosis of abnormal cells, without needing to correct any change to the DNA base sequence, since methylation is reversible.
❓ A student writes: "hypomethylation of a tumour suppressor gene silences it." Correct this statement.
This reverses the correct pairing. Hypermethylation, not hypomethylation, of a tumour suppressor gene's promoter silences it; hypomethylation instead applies to a proto-oncogene, where it switches the gene on by increasing its transcription.

Oestrogen & breast cancer

❓ Explain how increased oestrogen concentration could contribute to the development of some breast cancers.
Oestrogen acts as, or activates, a transcription factor, which binds to the promoter region of genes that stimulate cell division, including proto-oncogenes; increased oestrogen concentration increases transcription of these genes; this leads to increased cell division, which can result in tumour formation.
❓ A treatment binds to the oestrogen receptor without activating it. Explain the effect this would have on gene expression in breast tissue.
With the receptor occupied but not activated, oestrogen can no longer bind and activate transcription factor activity at the promoters of genes that stimulate division, including proto-oncogenes; transcription of these genes decreases, reducing the stimulation of cell division that raised oestrogen levels would otherwise cause.
❓ "Oestrogen increases the risk of breast cancer." Explain why this statement alone would not gain full marks, and rewrite it to gain credit.
The statement alone is too generic — it does not explain the mechanism. A creditable version links it to gene expression: oestrogen acts as a transcription factor, increasing transcription of proto-oncogenes and other genes that stimulate cell division, so a higher oestrogen concentration increases the rate of cell division in breast tissue.

Evidence, prevention & treatment

❓ A study finds a positive correlation between a dietary factor and the incidence of a particular cancer. Evaluate this evidence.
The data show a positive correlation between the dietary factor and cancer incidence, but a correlation alone does not establish a causal link; other variables may not have been controlled, the sample size or study population may have been limited to one group, the study may not have run for long enough, and it should be checked whether a statistical test showed the difference to be significant — specifically, whether there is less than a 5% (0.05) probability that the difference is due to chance.
❓ Explain how an understanding of oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes could be used in cancer prevention and diagnosis.
Prevention: DNA probes can be used to screen at-risk individuals, such as those with a family history, for mutated tumour-suppressor-gene alleles, allowing monitoring or preventive action to be taken. Diagnosis: methylation profiling or gene-expression profiling can identify the specific type of tumour present, so that treatment can be targeted appropriately.
❓ A new cancer treatment carries a risk of serious side effects. Evaluate whether a patient should undergo this treatment.
For: without treatment, quality of life may be poor or survival unlikely, and the side effects, while serious, may themselves be treatable or affect only a minority of patients. Against: the risk of the side effect occurring may be high, and any cancer that develops as a result may recur or fail to respond to further treatment; if the evidence comes from a short study, further cases may not yet have appeared. A balanced answer must give reasoned points on both sides — a bare yes or no earns no credit.
