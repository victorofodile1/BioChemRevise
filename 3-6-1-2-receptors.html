Receptors

How a receptor turns a stimulus into a nervous impulse — the Pacinian corpuscle's stretch-mediated channels and generator potential, then how the wiring of rods and cones in the retina trades sensitivity against acuity.

⚠️

The single habit that protects the most marks on this spec point: both set-piece mechanisms here — the Pacinian corpuscle's causal chain, and the rods/cones convergence argument — are marked as a complete, ordered sequence. Skipping or merging one link loses that mark even when every other fact you wrote is correct. Section 1 · spec content point

🎯 Receptors, stimuli & the specificity principle

Every receptor on the specification, whatever its shape, follows exactly the same two rules. Get these precisely right first, because the rest of this page is really just filling in the mechanism underneath them.

📖

What the specification says: "The Pacinian corpuscle should be used as an example of a receptor to illustrate that: receptors respond only to specific stimuli; stimulation of a receptor leads to the establishment of a generator potential."

1
2
3

Think of a receptor like a receptionist who will only accept one specific kind of parcel — say, only parcels marked "pressure." A parcel marked "light" or "sound" gets turned away completely, even if it's delivered to the exact same desk. When the right parcel does arrive, the receptionist doesn't shout down the corridor straight away — first they fill in a form that gets bigger the harder the parcel was pressed against the desk. Only once that form is completely full do they send the shout (the nervous impulse) down the corridor — and that shout is always exactly the same volume, however full the form was when it tipped over.

  • Stimulus = any change around the receptor (the parcel)
  • Receptor = the cell that only accepts one type of change
  • Transducer = the job of turning that change into an electrical signal
  • Generator potential = the "form" that fills up more the stronger the change is
  • Threshold = the form being completely full
  • Action potential = the shout — always the same, once it happens at all

A stimulus is any change in the internal or external environment. A receptor is built to detect just one kind of stimulus — a Pacinian corpuscle only ever responds to mechanical pressure, never to light, sound or temperature, because its membrane protein is shaped to respond to that one type of energy and no other. Where a receptor sits at the end of a sensory neurone, it also acts as a transducer, converting the stimulus's energy into an electrical change in the membrane. That electrical change is called a generator potential, and its size varies with how strong the stimulus is — it's graded, not fixed. Only if the generator potential is big enough to cross a threshold does it go on to trigger an action potential, which — unlike the generator potential — is always exactly the same size no matter how far above threshold the stimulus pushed it.

TermPrecise meaning

Stating the specificity principle, using the Pacinian corpuscle as the example

  • A Pacinian corpuscle responds only to mechanical pressure/deformation — never to light, sound or temperature.
  • Each type of receptor is built around a different-shaped membrane protein, so it can only ever detect the one stimulus energy that protein is built for.
  • This is the general rule every receptor on the specification follows: a rod's pigment absorbs light, a chemoreceptor's protein binds CO₂/H⁺ — and neither will ever respond to the other's stimulus.
📌

Rule of thumb: any question anywhere in Topic 3.6 that asks how a receptor "detects" or "responds to" a stimulus is, underneath, still testing this specificity-plus-generator-potential template — state both halves even if the question only seems to be asking about one.

Generator potential versus action potential — the distinction examiners reuse everywhere

A generator potential is graded — it varies in size with how strong the stimulus is. An action potential is all-or-nothing — once triggered, it is always the same size, however strong the stimulus that caused it. Stimulation of a receptor always produces a generator potential first; only if this reaches threshold does it go on to trigger an action potential in the sensory neurone. This distinction is confused constantly and penalised heavily whenever it is — it gets reused as a discriminator throughout this whole section of the specification.

▶ 🚩 Trap — never write "signal" or "message" for an impulse

Wherever nervous transmission is being described, "signal" and "message" are treated as too vague and are not accepted in place of "impulse" or "action potential." Get into the habit of naming the actual electrical event every time, even in a quick sentence.

Section 2 · spec content point

🏗️ The basic structure of a Pacinian corpuscle

Only three named structures, but examiner reports consistently show that most students can only reliably name one or two of them on a labelled diagram — this short section is worth drilling until it's automatic.

📖

What the specification says: "The basic structure of a Pacinian corpuscle."

1
2
3

Picture an onion, sliced in half — layer after layer of tissue wrapped around a middle. In the very centre of a Pacinian corpuscle sits the end of a nerve cell, and wrapped around it are lots of onion-like rings called lamellae, with a squishy gel sitting between each ring. The whole thing sits buried deep in your skin (and in your joints too), waiting for something to press on it.

  • Lamellae = the onion-like rings of tissue
  • Sensory neurone ending = the nerve cell tip sitting in the very middle

A Pacinian corpuscle is built around a single sensory neurone ending, wrapped in concentric rings of connective tissue called lamellae, with a layer of gel separating each ring from the next. The whole structure sits deep in the skin, and also in joints, ligaments and tendons, where it can pick up changes in pressure. Because the lamellae spring back into shape once pressure stops changing, a corpuscle only fires while pressure is actively changing — steady, unchanging pressure eventually stops producing any impulses at all.

  • A single sensory neurone ending, wrapped in concentric rings of connective tissue called lamellae, separated from one another by a layer of gel.
  • Pacinian corpuscles sit deep in the skin, and also in joints, ligaments and tendons.
  • Because the lamellae spring back into shape once the pressure stops changing, the corpuscle responds to changing pressure rather than to constant, steady pressure — continuous unchanging pressure eventually stops producing further impulses.
The three named structures — lamellae, sensory neurone ending, and the stretch-mediated Na⁺ channel it carries — with the channel shown closed at rest and open once stretched. Section 3 turns the right-hand panel into the causal chain.
▶ 🚩 Trap — vague or incomplete structure naming

Examiner reports consistently flag Pacinian corpuscle labelling as weak: most students can only reliably name one or two of the three required structures on a labelled diagram, and the channel is very often given an incomplete name such as just "sodium channel" — dropping the required "stretch-mediated" (or "mechanically-gated") qualifier costs the mark even though "sodium channel" on its own isn't wrong, just incomplete.

Section 3 · spec content point

🔗 Stretch-mediated Na⁺ channels & the generator potential

This is one unbroken causal chain, not a set of loose facts — pressure at one end, an action potential (maybe) at the other, with seven separate, separately-credited links in between. Learn it in order, then practise reproducing every link without skipping any.

📖

What the specification says: "Deformation of stretch-mediated sodium ion channels in a Pacinian corpuscle leads to the establishment of a generator potential."

1
2
3

Imagine a stretchy doorframe with a narrow door in it. If nobody's leaning on the frame, the door stays shut. But push on the frame, and it stretches — which pulls the door wider open, letting people (sodium ions) rush through into the room. The more people who get in, the more crowded (depolarised) the room becomes. If it gets crowded enough, an alarm goes off (an action potential) — and that alarm is always exactly the same volume, it either goes off or it doesn't.

Pressure deforms the corpuscle, which deforms the lamellae and stretches the sensory neurone's membrane. That stretching physically widens the stretch-mediated sodium channels sitting in the membrane, so sodium ions diffuse in down their concentration gradient. This depolarises the membrane, producing a generator potential — and the bigger the pressure, the more channels open, so the bigger the generator potential. If the generator potential is big enough to reach threshold, it triggers an action potential, which then travels off along the sensory neurone.

Click a numbered dot, or use Next / Back above, to step through the causal chain — the caption and the highlighted dot always stay in sync.
▶ 🚩 The single costliest omission

Chains that jump straight from "stretching" to "action potential," with no mention of the sodium channels or of depolarisation, lose every linking mark in between — even though the start and end points given are both correct. Every "channel opens" statement needs the ion movement and its consequence stated in full, every time: not "channels open," but "channels open, so sodium ions diffuse in, depolarising the membrane."

Why a stronger stimulus produces a bigger response

  • Generator potential size is proportional to stimulus intensity — this is how the nervous system codes stimulus strength, since action potentials themselves can never vary in size.
  • A stronger pressure deforms the membrane more, so more stretch-mediated channels open, more Na⁺ diffuses in, and a larger generator potential results.
  • A larger generator potential reaches threshold sooner and produces action potentials at a higher frequency — the action potentials themselves stay all-or-nothing (identical size); only their frequency, and how quickly they begin, changes with stimulus strength.
🔗

Synoptic link — reaction time. The time taken for the stretch-mediated channels to open is itself a genuine, credited component of why a measured reaction time is always longer than nerve-conduction time alone would predict — alongside synaptic delay and the time needed for muscle contraction, covered later in this topic.

▶ ⭐ A full-mark answer, written out as a single sequence

"Explain how deformation of a Pacinian corpuscle leads to the establishment of a generator potential."

"Pressure deforms the corpuscle, which deforms the lamellae and stretches the sensory neurone's membrane. This stretching widens the stretch-mediated sodium ion channels in the membrane, so sodium ions diffuse into the neurone down their electrochemical gradient. This depolarises the membrane, establishing a generator potential. If the generator potential reaches threshold, voltage-gated sodium channels open and an action potential is triggered, which then propagates along the neurone."

Every underlined idea here is a separate mark: deformation of the lamellae/membrane → stretch-mediated channels open (named, specific type) → Na⁺ diffuses in (ion named, direction given) → depolarisation (the electrical event) → generator potential (the named product) → the threshold condition → action potential (named, and correctly conditional on threshold being reached). Missing any one link, or giving them out of order, is the single most common way this answer loses marks.

Section 4 · spec content point

👁️ The retina: rods and cones

Rods and cones don't differ in sensitivity and acuity for two separate reasons — one structural difference, convergence, explains both. Always state that structural cause before either consequence.

📖

What the specification says: "The human retina in sufficient detail to show how differences in sensitivity to light, sensitivity to colour and visual acuity are explained by differences in the optical pigments of rods and cones and the connections rods and cones make in the optic nerve."

1
2
3

Imagine two ways of passing on a message. Rods work like a big group chat: lots of rod cells all dump their small messages into the same group thread, so even faint whispers combine into something loud enough to notice — but when the brain gets the ping, it has no idea which person in the group actually spoke, so the picture is blurry. Cones work like private one-to-one messages: each cone has its very own line straight to the brain, so the brain always knows exactly who sent what — sharp and precise — but each cone needs a decent amount of light on its own to bother sending anything at all.

  • Rhodopsin = the light-catching chemical inside rods
  • Iodopsin = the light-catching chemical inside cones (three versions, for colour)
  • Convergence = many cells sharing one line to the brain

Rods contain one pigment, rhodopsin, and detect light intensity only, with no colour information. Cones contain three types of a different pigment, iodopsin, giving trichromatic colour vision. The bigger difference, though, is wiring: many rods share a single route to the brain — convergence — so their small signals add together (summation), making rods very sensitive in dim light, but meaning the brain can't tell exactly which rod fired, giving low acuity. Cones show little or no convergence, especially at the fovea, so each cone sends its own separate signal — giving high acuity, but meaning each cone needs brighter light to reach threshold on its own.

Convergence is the single structural cause of both effects: many rods sharing one pathway sum their small signals (sensitive, but the brain can't tell which rod fired); cones keep close to their own line to the brain (need more light, but precisely resolved).

Explaining each direction of the trade-off

  • Why rods give high sensitivity but low acuity: several rod cells converge onto a single (bipolar, then ganglion) neurone. Each individual rod's generator potential may be too small to reach threshold alone, but the generator potentials from many rods sum together on the shared neurone (spatial summation) — making threshold more likely to be reached even in dim light, so rods are highly sensitive. That same shared wiring is exactly why acuity is low: because many rods feed into one neurone, the brain receives a single combined impulse and cannot tell which specific rod(s) within the group were actually stimulated, so two points of light falling on different rods in the same group can't be resolved as separate.
  • Why cones give high acuity but need brighter light: cones show little or no convergence — particularly at the fovea, where each cone connects to its own bipolar and ganglion cell. Each cone sends its own separate impulse to the brain, so two close points of light falling on two different cones produce two distinguishable impulses — high visual acuity. The trade-off is lower sensitivity: with no neighbouring cell to sum with, a single cone often can't reach threshold alone in dim light, so cones need much brighter light before they respond at all.

Colour vision and colour blindness

Colour is detected only by cones, because there are three distinct types of cone, each containing a different light-sensitive pigment (broadly red-, green- and blue-sensitive). Any given colour is perceived from the particular combination and relative strength of stimulation across the three cone types — which is also why colour blindness typically still allows some colours to be seen: if one pigment type is non-functional, the remaining two functional types can still combine to produce other colours, just not the full range.

▶ 🚩 Trap — never write "green cones"

The precise, credited wording is "cones containing green-sensitive pigment" (and equivalently for red and blue) — a bare colour-name label applied directly to the cone itself, such as "green cone," is not accepted on its own.

📌

One structural difference drives both effects. Rods and cones do not differ in sensitivity and acuity for two separate, unconnected reasons — convergence, or its absence, is the single cause of both. Many-to-one wiring (rods) trades individual identity for combined signal strength: more sensitive, less precise. Near one-to-one wiring (cones) keeps each cell's identity intact: less sensitive, but precisely resolved. Always state the structural cause before either consequence.

Section 5 · applying it

🧪 Practical skills for this point

There is no numbered required practical tied directly to 3.6.1.2 — instead, the specification lists three skin-receptor investigations that all draw directly on the vocabulary and mechanisms built up on this page.

  • Sensitivity investigation (e.g. the smallest temperature change a patch of skin can distinguish) tests the same threshold/generator-potential principle established for the Pacinian corpuscle: a stimulus has to be strong enough to push a generator potential over threshold before it's detected at all.
  • Habituation investigation (touch receptors responding less over repeated, sustained stimulation) reflects the same idea seen with the Pacinian corpuscle's lamellae springing back under constant pressure — receptors are generally built to respond to change, not to an unchanging stimulus.
  • Resolution ("two-point discrimination") investigation tests the same acuity concept established for rods and cones — here applied to touch receptors in the skin instead of to light receptors in the retina, but the underlying idea (can the brain tell two nearby stimulated points apart, or does it perceive just one?) is identical.

Note: the specification's one numbered required practical on receptor/response behaviour (using a choice chamber or maze to test an environmental variable's effect on animal movement) belongs to the adjacent spec point on survival and response, not to 3.6.1.2 directly — but it still rests on the same underlying idea that a receptor detects a stimulus and a response follows.

Section 6 · exam technique

🎯 Common mistakes & precise wording

This spec point is short, but genuinely high-risk — the biology itself isn't conceptually hard, but a very large share of the available marks rides on exact terminology and on never dropping a link out of a causal chain.

▶ Why this point is riskier than its length suggests

This is exclusively second-year (A2) content, tested at a compact but demanding level: every named structure — lamellae, the stretch-mediated Na⁺ channel, rhodopsin, iodopsin — is separately credited, and the convergence argument needs genuine causal reasoning rather than simple recall. Two failure patterns dominate: incomplete causal chains that skip the sodium-channel or depolarisation steps, and confusion over the direction of convergence (mixing up which cell type — rods or cones — shares a neurone with others). Both are entirely avoidable with deliberate, repeated practice of the full sequences rather than just the isolated facts.

Section 7 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

This spec point contributes to synoptic and essay-style answers more often than it produces its own extended-response question — it works well as a worked example inside a broader argument, provided each paragraph closes by tying the mechanism back to the essay's actual theme.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around "the importance of receptors in living organisms"

A receptor is specific to a single type of stimulus: a Pacinian corpuscle responds only to mechanical pressure, never to light, sound or temperature. Stimulation produces a generator potential — pressure deforms the corpuscle's stretch-mediated sodium ion channels, they widen, sodium ions diffuse in, and the membrane depolarises; if the generator potential reaches threshold, an action potential is triggered. This shows how a receptor acts as a transducer, converting one specific form of physical stimulus energy into the single common electrical currency — the nervous impulse — that the rest of the nervous system can then act on.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around structure explaining function

Rods (rhodopsin, high convergence onto shared neurones) give high sensitivity in dim light but low acuity; cones (iodopsin, little or no convergence) give high acuity and colour vision but need brighter light. Both differences trace back to a single structural feature — the degree of convergence in the optic nerve — showing how the wiring of a sensory system, and not only the pigment or protein it uses, can itself determine what that system is good at detecting.

⚠️

One wording trap that matters for essays specifically: never call a receptor's binding site an "active site" — that term is reserved for enzymes. Call it a specific or complementary binding/receptor site instead, however synoptic the answer gets.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Receptors, stimuli & specificity

  • A receptor is specific to one stimulus type — a Pacinian corpuscle only ever responds to mechanical pressure.
  • Where it's part of a sensory neurone, a receptor is a transducer: it converts stimulus energy into a change in membrane potential.
  • Generator potential = graded (varies with stimulus strength). Action potential = all-or-nothing (always the same size).
  • Never write "signal" or "message" for an impulse.

2 · Structure of the Pacinian corpuscle

  • Three named structures: sensory neurone ending, wrapped in lamellae (rings of connective tissue, gel between each).
  • Sits deep in the skin, joints, ligaments and tendons.
  • Responds to changing pressure only — lamellae spring back, so steady pressure stops producing impulses.

3 · Stretch-mediated channels & the generator potential

  • Chain: pressure → lamellae/membrane deform → stretch-mediated Na⁺ channels widen → Na⁺ diffuses in → depolarisation → generator potential → (if threshold) → action potential.
  • Bigger stimulus → more channels open → bigger generator potential → threshold reached sooner → higher frequency of action potentials (never bigger action potentials).
  • Never skip the channel/depolarisation link — jumping straight from "stretching" to "action potential" loses marks.

4 · The retina: rods and cones

  • Rods: rhodopsin (1 pigment), high convergence (many:1), high sensitivity, low acuity, peripheral retina.
  • Cones: iodopsin (3 pigments), little/no convergence (~1:1), colour vision, high acuity, need more light, dense at the fovea.
  • Convergence is the single structural cause of the sensitivity/acuity trade-off — always say this before either consequence.
  • Never write "green cone" — say "cone containing green-sensitive pigment."

Receptors, stimuli & specificity

❓ State two general principles that the Pacinian corpuscle is used to illustrate about receptors.
A receptor is specific to a single type of stimulus — a Pacinian corpuscle responds only to mechanical pressure/deformation, not to light, sound or temperature; and stimulation of a receptor leads to the establishment of a generator potential, which — if it reaches threshold — triggers an action potential in the sensory neurone.
❓ Distinguish between a generator potential and an action potential.
A generator potential is graded — its size varies with (is proportional to) the strength of the stimulus. An action potential is all-or-nothing — once triggered it is always the same size, regardless of stimulus strength.
❓ Explain why a receptor is described as a transducer.
A receptor converts the energy of a stimulus (e.g. mechanical pressure) into an electrical change in membrane potential (a generator potential) — converting one form of energy into another is what makes it a transducer.

Structure of a Pacinian corpuscle

❓ Describe the basic structure of a Pacinian corpuscle.
A single sensory neurone ending, surrounded by layers (lamellae) of connective tissue, separated by gel. It is found deep in the skin, and also in joints, ligaments and tendons.
❓ A Pacinian corpuscle is placed under constant, unchanging pressure. Explain why it eventually stops producing impulses.
The lamellae spring back into their original shape even while the pressure is maintained, so the sensory neurone's membrane is no longer being stretched; without ongoing deformation, the stretch-mediated sodium channels close again, so no further generator potential is produced.

Stretch-mediated channels & the generator potential

❓ Explain, step by step, how deformation of a Pacinian corpuscle leads to the establishment of a generator potential.
Pressure deforms the lamellae, which deform the membrane of the sensory neurone ending; this stretches/deforms the stretch-mediated sodium ion channels, so they widen; sodium ions diffuse into the neurone down their electrochemical gradient; the membrane depolarises, producing a generator potential; if the generator potential is large enough to reach threshold, an action potential is produced and passes along the sensory neurone.
❓ Explain why a stronger pressure stimulus produces action potentials at a higher frequency, rather than producing larger action potentials.
A stronger pressure deforms the membrane more, opening more stretch-mediated sodium channels, so more sodium ions diffuse in and a larger generator potential is produced; this reaches threshold sooner and more often, increasing the frequency of action potentials — but each action potential remains all-or-nothing and so stays the same size regardless of stimulus strength.
❓ A student writes: "the membrane is stretched, so an action potential is produced." Identify what is missing from this answer.
The answer omits the stretch-mediated sodium channels opening, sodium ions diffusing in, the resulting depolarisation, the generator potential being established, and the condition that threshold must be reached before an action potential is produced — each of these is a separately credited step.

The retina: rods and cones

❓ Compare rods and cones in terms of their pigments, connections in the optic nerve, sensitivity and acuity.
Rods contain rhodopsin, one pigment type, and show convergence — many rods connect to a shared bipolar and ganglion cell — giving high sensitivity (generator potentials from several rods sum to reach threshold in dim light) but low acuity (the brain cannot tell which rod fired). Cones contain iodopsin, three pigment types giving colour vision, and show little/no convergence — each cone has close to its own bipolar and ganglion cell — giving high acuity (separate impulses reach the brain) but lower sensitivity (needing brighter light to reach threshold alone).
❓ Explain why cones give high visual acuity but rods do not.
Each cone is connected to its own bipolar and ganglion cell (little/no convergence), so each cone sends a separate impulse to the brain, allowing two close points of light on different cones to be resolved as separate; several rods share a single neurone, so the brain receives one combined impulse and cannot tell which rod(s) among the group were stimulated.
❓ A person's green-sensitive cone pigment is non-functional. Explain why they can still see some colours.
There are three types of cone, each containing a different pigment (red-, green- and blue-sensitive); colours are perceived from the combined stimulation of more than one cone type, so the two remaining functional pigment types can still combine to produce some colours, even without a working green-sensitive pigment.
