Respiration

How a cell actually releases the energy locked in glucose and packages it as ATP — from glycolysis in the cytoplasm, through the link reaction and Krebs cycle in the mitochondrial matrix, to oxidative phosphorylation on the cristae, plus what happens to the pathway when oxygen runs out and how fat and protein feed into the same system. Entirely A-level-only content.

⚠️

The one habit that protects the most marks across this whole spec point: never write that respiration, or any single stage of it, "produces energy." Energy is released, and what a stage actually produces is ATP, reduced NAD, reduced FAD or carbon dioxide — say exactly which. The other repeat offender: the enzyme embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane that actually makes ATP is called ATP synthase, never "stalked particles."

Section 1 · spec content point

🔋 Respiration & the four-stage overview

Before the detail, fix the shape of the whole pathway: respiration is not one reaction, but four linked stages, each running in a specific part of the cell, gradually releasing the energy stored in glucose so it can be captured as ATP rather than lost as heat. Almost every mark later in this topic depends on placing a fact in the right stage and the right location.

📖

What the specification says: "Respiration produces ATP."

1
2
3

Think of a mitochondrion as a little power station with four workshops in a row. Glucose (the fuel) arrives, and each workshop does one job on it, passing the leftovers to the next workshop, gradually charging up lots of tiny rechargeable batteries (ATP) instead of burning the fuel all at once. Only the very last workshop actually needs to breathe in oxygen to keep running.

  • ATP = the cell's rechargeable battery — the actual useful energy currency
  • Mitochondrion = the power-station organelle where most of the workshops sit
  • Glucose = the fuel that gets broken down, workshop by workshop

Respiration is the controlled, step-by-step breakdown of glucose (and other fuels) to release energy that's used to make ATP. It happens in four linked stages, each in its own part of the cell: glycolysis in the cytoplasm, then the link reaction and Krebs cycle in the matrix (the fluid-filled centre of a mitochondrion), and finally oxidative phosphorylation on the cristae — folds of the mitochondrion's inner membrane that give it a huge surface area. Doing this gradually, in small controlled steps, means the energy can be captured as ATP rather than released all at once as heat.

AQA does not expect a precise ATP-per-coenzyme conversion factor from memory, only the stage sequence, the locations, and the per-stage figures shown above.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "produces energy"

Never write that respiration, or any one of its four stages, "produces energy" — that phrase is not credited on its own. Say instead that ATP is produced, or that energy is released (for example, "released as electrons pass along the electron transport chain," or "released when ATP is hydrolysed"). It's a small wording change that protects marks across the entire topic, not just one section.

Section 2 · spec content point

🧫 Glycolysis: location & role

Before the mechanism, fix two facts that get tested constantly on their own: where glycolysis happens, and that it needs no oxygen at all — regardless of whether the cell goes on to respire aerobically or anaerobically.

📖

What the specification says: "Glycolysis is the first stage of anaerobic and aerobic respiration. It occurs in the cytoplasm and is an anaerobic process."

1
2
3

Glycolysis is like chopping the vegetables before you even decide whether you're going to cook them in the oven or not. Every cell does this first step in the same place — floating around in the cell's main fluid, not inside the power-station part — and it doesn't need any oxygen to do it, whichever way the recipe goes afterwards.

"Glycolysis" literally means glucose-splitting. It's the very first stage of respiration, shared by both the aerobic and anaerobic routes — every cell does it, no matter what happens to the pyruvate afterwards. Crucially, it happens in the cytoplasm, not inside the mitochondrion, and it is itself an anaerobic process: it doesn't use oxygen, even in a cell that is otherwise respiring aerobically. What happens next — whether pyruvate goes on into the mitochondrion or gets converted to lactate or ethanol instead — is decided later, not during glycolysis itself.

  • Glycolysis is the first stage of both aerobic and anaerobic respiration — every respiring cell carries it out, regardless of which pathway follows.
  • It takes place in the cytoplasm, entirely outside the mitochondrion.
  • It is itself an anaerobic process — it requires no oxygen at any point, which is why it can continue even when a cell has none available.
▶ 🚩 Trap — placing glycolysis inside the mitochondrion

Glycolysis happens in the cytoplasm. Never draw it, or describe it, as occurring inside the mitochondrion — that confusion also tends to muddle the location of the ATP and reduced NAD it produces, which examiners will read as a location error, not just a labelling slip.

Substrate-level vs oxidative phosphorylation

Glycolysis makes its ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation — the direct transfer of a phosphate group from an intermediate substrate molecule to ADP, forming ATP. This is a useful term to define precisely now, because it recurs in the Krebs cycle (Section 7) and gets set directly against oxidative phosphorylation (Section 8), which instead makes ATP indirectly, driven by the electron transport chain and chemiosmosis across the inner mitochondrial membrane. AQA regularly pairs these two terms against each other, so being able to state the difference precisely, not just name both, is worth marks on its own.

Section 3 · spec content point

⚙️ Glycolysis: the three stages

Now the mechanism itself: three linked steps that convert one six-carbon glucose molecule into two three-carbon pyruvate molecules, with a small but real net gain of ATP along the way.

📖

What the specification says: "Glycolysis involves the following stages: phosphorylation of glucose to glucose phosphate, using ATP; production of triose phosphate; oxidation of triose phosphate to pyruvate with a net gain of ATP and reduced NAD."

1
2
3

First, glucose gets two energy "stickers" (ATP) put on it — this makes it easier to work with. Then it snaps in half into two smaller matching pieces. Finally, each of those two pieces is squeezed for its own little burst of energy, making a fresh ATP sticker and handing some spare hydrogen to a delivery van (reduced NAD) — leaving behind a smaller molecule called pyruvate.

Glycolysis has three linked steps. First, glucose is phosphorylated to glucose phosphate, using up 2 ATP — this makes the molecule more reactive so the rest of the pathway can proceed. Second, the phosphorylated glucose splits into two molecules of a three-carbon compound called triose phosphate. Third, each triose phosphate is oxidised to pyruvate, and this step is where ATP and reduced NAD are actually generated — more than enough to pay back the 2 ATP spent earlier, leaving a net gain overall.

  1. Phosphorylation of glucose to glucose phosphate, using ATP. Two molecules of ATP are hydrolysed to phosphorylate glucose, which raises its reactivity and commits it to the pathway.
  2. Production of triose phosphate. The phosphorylated six-carbon molecule is split into two three-carbon molecules of triose phosphate.
  3. Oxidation of triose phosphate to pyruvate, with a net gain of ATP and reduced NAD. Each triose phosphate is oxidised (loses hydrogen) to pyruvate; this generates ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation and reduces NAD to reduced NAD.

Because step 3 happens twice (once per triose phosphate) and makes 4 ATP in total, while step 1 only uses 2 ATP once, the pathway shows a net gain of 2 ATP per glucose — always describe it as a net figure, since the raw "4 ATP made" number on its own ignores the 2 ATP spent earlier.

Section 4 · spec content point

🏃 Anaerobic respiration: lactate & ethanol

When oxygen isn't available, glycolysis can still carry on — but only if something regenerates the oxidised NAD it needs. This section is about that one purpose, achieved by two genuinely different routes depending on the organism.

📖

What the specification says: "If respiration is only anaerobic, pyruvate can be converted to ethanol or lactate using reduced NAD. The oxidised NAD produced in this way can be used in further glycolysis."

1
2
3

Picture reduced NAD as a taxi that's currently full of passengers (hydrogen). If every taxi in the fleet stays full, glycolysis has no empty taxis left to pick up new passengers, and the whole process grinds to a halt. So without oxygen, cells quickly dump the passengers somewhere — muscle turns pyruvate into lactate to empty the taxi, yeast turns pyruvate into ethanol (and lets out a puff of CO₂) to empty the taxi. Either way, the empty taxi (oxidised NAD) heads straight back to collect more passengers, so glycolysis can keep going.

Without oxygen, the electron transport chain can't run, so reduced NAD has nowhere else to be re-oxidised. Instead, pyruvate itself accepts the hydrogen from reduced NAD, which turns pyruvate into either lactate (in animals) or ethanol (in yeast and plants), and — critically — regenerates oxidised NAD. That oxidised NAD then goes straight back into glycolysis, letting it keep making its 2 ATP per glucose even with no oxygen present at all. Only the ethanol pathway also releases carbon dioxide on the way.

Two separate anaerobic pathways from pyruvate, both existing solely to regenerate oxidised NAD.

▶ 🚩 The most commonly confused pair on this topic

Never attribute CO₂ release to the lactate pathway, and never leave it out of the ethanol pathway — only ethanol production releases CO₂. Lactate formation is a single-step reduction with no decarboxylation involved at all.

Why NAD regeneration actually matters

Without regenerating oxidised NAD, glycolysis could not continue — oxidised NAD is needed to accept hydrogen again during the oxidation of triose phosphate to pyruvate, so if it ran out, all ATP production would stop completely, not just slow down. When a question is set in an anaerobic context, the process responsible for regenerating NAD is fermentation — the anaerobic conversion of pyruvate to lactate or ethanol — not the Krebs cycle or electron transport chain, both of which are stalled without oxygen anyway.

Consequences of anaerobic respiration in exercising muscle

This is a classic causal-chain question, best answered as an unbroken sequence: as oxygen supply becomes limiting, less aerobic respiration can occur, so less ATP is produced by oxidative phosphorylation; to keep meeting ATP demand, anaerobic respiration increases, producing lactate; lactate accumulation contributes to muscle fatigue, and lactate then leaves the muscle fibre by facilitated diffusion.

Why more aerobic respiration is an advantage

More ATP is produced per glucose than by anaerobic respiration alone, so the onset of anaerobic respiration is delayed, meaning less lactate is produced overall. This is usually attributed to a specific physiological cause — for example, more mitochondria, or a higher proportion of slow-twitch (Type I) muscle fibres, both of which increase aerobic capacity.

📌

Efficiency, stated precisely: anaerobic respiration yields only the 2 ATP made during glycolysis per glucose — genuinely inefficient compared with the ≈32 ATP available from the complete aerobic pathway (Section 1).

Section 5 · spec content point

🚪 Pyruvate entering the mitochondrion

A short but genuinely separate content point: how pyruvate actually gets from the cytoplasm, where glycolysis happens, into the mitochondrial matrix, where the rest of aerobic respiration takes place.

📖

What the specification says: "If respiration is aerobic, pyruvate from glycolysis enters the mitochondrial matrix by active transport."

1
2
3

Pyruvate can't just wander into the mitochondrion on its own — it needs a lift. Special carrier proteins in the membrane act like a powered doorway, using some of the cell's own energy to pull pyruvate inside, even against the flow it would naturally take.

When oxygen is available, pyruvate made by glycolysis doesn't just diffuse into the mitochondrion — it's moved in by active transport, which means specific carrier proteins in the mitochondrial membranes use ATP to move pyruvate across, often against its concentration gradient. This matters because it's a deliberate, energy-costing step, not a passive one, and it's the only way pyruvate can reach the matrix, where the link reaction and Krebs cycle are waiting for it.

FeatureDetail
MechanismActive transport — carrier proteins move pyruvate across the mitochondrial membranes, using ATP
DirectionCytoplasm (where glycolysis occurs) → mitochondrial matrix
ConditionOccurs specifically when respiration is aerobic — i.e. when oxygen is available to keep the downstream pathway running
Why not diffusion?Pyruvate does not necessarily move down a favourable concentration gradient, and reliable, regulated entry into the matrix is required for the pathway to continue efficiently

If oxygen is not available, this step simply doesn't happen in the same way — instead, pyruvate remains in the cytoplasm and is converted to lactate or ethanol (Section 4). Whether a question is really asking about aerobic or anaerobic respiration often hinges on exactly this fork in the pathway: does pyruvate get actively transported into the matrix, or converted where it already is?

📌

Precise phrasing worth reusing: "pyruvate is actively transported into the mitochondrial matrix" — naming both the mechanism (active transport) and the destination (mitochondrial matrix, not just "the mitochondrion" vaguely) is what separates a full-credit answer from a partial one.

Section 6 · spec content point

🔗 The link reaction

The first thing that happens to pyruvate once it's inside the mitochondrial matrix — two separately credited chemical changes packed into one short reaction.

📖

What the specification says: "Aerobic respiration in such detail as to show that: pyruvate is oxidised to acetate, producing reduced NAD in the process; acetate combines with coenzyme A in the link reaction to produce acetylcoenzyme A; acetylcoenzyme A reacts with a four-carbon molecule, releasing coenzyme A and producing a six-carbon molecule that enters the Krebs cycle; in a series of oxidation-reduction reactions, the Krebs cycle generates reduced coenzymes and ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation, and carbon dioxide is lost; synthesis of ATP by oxidative phosphorylation is associated with the transfer of electrons down the electron transfer chain and passage of protons across inner mitochondrial membranes and is catalysed by ATP synthase embedded in these membranes (chemiosmotic theory); other respiratory substrates include the breakdown products of lipids and amino acids, which enter the Krebs cycle." (This is one dense specification content point, covering the link reaction, the Krebs cycle, oxidative phosphorylation and other respiratory substrates. Because of how much it contains, it's split across this section and the three that follow — Sections 7, 8 and 9 — each repeating this same spec quote and covering one clause of it. This section covers the first two clauses: the oxidation of pyruvate to acetate, and its combination with coenzyme A.)

1
2
3

Pyruvate arrives in the matrix and gets two things done to it at once: it loses a bit of hydrogen (handed to a reduced-NAD delivery van), and it loses a carbon dioxide bubble. What's left — called acetate — links up with a helper molecule called coenzyme A to form a small two-carbon travel pass, ready to enter the next stage.

The link reaction happens in the mitochondrial matrix. Pyruvate is oxidised — it loses hydrogen, which reduces NAD — forming acetate. Separately, pyruvate is also decarboxylated — it loses carbon dioxide. The resulting acetate then combines with a molecule called coenzyme A to form acetyl coenzyme A, a two-carbon compound that's now ready to enter the Krebs cycle. This whole sequence happens twice per glucose, once for each pyruvate molecule.

  1. Pyruvate is oxidised to acetate, producing reduced NAD. Pyruvate loses hydrogen (dehydrogenation), which reduces NAD.
  2. Pyruvate is decarboxylated. A separate change: pyruvate loses carbon dioxide.
  3. Acetate combines with coenzyme A to produce acetylcoenzyme A. This is the actual "link reaction" step, forming a two-carbon molecule.

The link reaction occurs ×2 per glucose (once for each of the two pyruvate molecules from glycolysis): 2 acetyl coenzyme A, 2 reduced NAD and 2 CO₂ lost in total, with no ATP produced directly. It takes place in the mitochondrial matrix.

▶ 🚩 Trap — blending "oxidised" and "decarboxylated" into one vague statement

"Oxidised" and "decarboxylated" are two separately credited ideas — a frequent discriminator between full-mark and partial answers. Oxidation (dehydrogenation) is what produces reduced NAD; decarboxylation is the separate loss of CO₂. State both, and state them separately — writing only "pyruvate is broken down" or "pyruvate loses carbon and hydrogen" without naming each process individually will not earn both marks.

Section 7 · spec content point (continued)

🔄 The Krebs cycle

Acetyl coenzyme A now enters a cycle of reactions that strips it apart piece by piece, capturing its stored energy as reduced coenzymes, a little ATP, and losing carbon dioxide along the way.

📖

What the specification says (same content point as Section 6): "…acetylcoenzyme A reacts with a four-carbon molecule, releasing coenzyme A and producing a six-carbon molecule that enters the Krebs cycle; in a series of oxidation-reduction reactions, the Krebs cycle generates reduced coenzymes and ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation, and carbon dioxide is lost." (This section covers these two clauses of the same dense content point introduced in Section 6.)

1
2
3

A four-carbon "collector" molecule grabs the two-carbon travel pass (acetyl CoA), becoming a bigger six-carbon molecule. That molecule then goes round a slow, multi-step conveyor belt that gradually shrinks it back down to the same four-carbon collector — dropping off CO₂ bubbles and loading up NAD/FAD delivery vans, plus one battery of ATP, as it goes. Then the whole thing happens all over again.

Acetyl coenzyme A (2 carbons) reacts with a four-carbon compound, releasing coenzyme A (which is reused in the next link reaction) and forming a six-carbon compound. A series of oxidation-reduction reactions then steps this six-carbon compound back down to the four-carbon compound it started as, releasing carbon dioxide and reduced coenzymes (reduced NAD and reduced FAD) on the way, plus a small amount of ATP made directly. AQA wants these generic "four-carbon" and "six-carbon" terms used, not the names of the actual named intermediates.

  1. Acetylcoenzyme A reacts with a four-carbon molecule, releasing coenzyme A (which returns to the link reaction) and producing a six-carbon molecule.
  2. In a series of oxidation-reduction reactions, the six-carbon molecule is progressively decarboxylated and dehydrogenated back down to the four-carbon molecule.
  3. This releases carbon dioxide, generates reduced coenzymes (reduced NAD and reduced FAD), and produces ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation.

The cycle turns ×2 per glucose (once per acetyl CoA), so across both turns the totals are 6 reduced NAD, 2 reduced FAD, 2 ATP and 4 CO₂. It all takes place in the mitochondrial matrix.

▶ 🚩 Trap — named intermediates and the wrong reduced coenzyme

The specification deliberately does not require the names of the Krebs cycle's intermediate molecules — write "a four-carbon molecule" and "a six-carbon molecule," exactly as the specification itself phrases it, rather than naming specific compounds. Also watch for the wrong coenzyme creeping in here: "reduced NADP" (or "NADPH₂") belongs only to photosynthesis — the Krebs cycle produces reduced NAD and reduced FAD, never reduced NADP.

Reasoning through "more acetyl CoA enters the cycle"

A typical application question asks you to predict the consequence of more acetyl CoA entering the Krebs cycle. Trace it one causal step at a time, all the way to a stated outcome: more acetyl CoA entering → more reduced coenzymes generated → more ATP produced (via oxidative phosphorylation) → a named physiological consequence, such as supporting more slow-twitch muscle fibre activity or increased endurance. Never stop the chain at "more ATP" if a question specifically asks for a consequence — always finish with what that extra ATP actually enables.

Section 8 · spec content point (continued)

⚡ Oxidative phosphorylation

The stage that actually makes the bulk of the ATP — and the only point in the whole pathway that needs oxygen directly. This is the highest-value, most heavily sequenced piece of writing in the whole spec point.

📖

What the specification says (same content point as Sections 6–7): "…synthesis of ATP by oxidative phosphorylation is associated with the transfer of electrons down the electron transfer chain and passage of protons across inner mitochondrial membranes and is catalysed by ATP synthase embedded in these membranes (chemiosmotic theory)." (This section covers this clause of the same dense content point introduced in Section 6.)

1
2
3

The reduced-NAD and reduced-FAD delivery vans drop off their hydrogen passengers at a relay team of workers lined up along a wall (the inner membrane). Passing the passengers down the relay releases energy, which is used to pump water (hydrogen ions) up and over a dam wall into a reservoir. That water then flows back down through a turbine — ATP synthase — which spins and charges up ATP batteries as it turns. Right at the bottom of the relay, oxygen mops up the leftover passengers to make plain water.

Reduced NAD and reduced FAD are oxidised, releasing electrons. These electrons pass along a chain of carrier proteins — the electron transport chain — embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane, releasing energy at each step. That energy is used to actively pump hydrogen ions (protons) across the inner membrane into the intermembrane space, building up a concentration gradient. The protons then diffuse back down that gradient through ATP synthase, a protein embedded in the same membrane, and this flow drives the phosphorylation of ADP to ATP — a process called chemiosmosis. Right at the end of the chain, oxygen accepts the spent electrons and protons, forming water — the only step in the whole of respiration that directly needs oxygen.

Electrons from reduced NAD/FAD pass along carrier proteins, releasing energy that pumps H⁺ into the intermembrane space; H⁺ diffuses back through ATP synthase, phosphorylating ADP + Pi to ATP, with oxygen as the final electron acceptor.

A full, sequenced account

  1. Source of reduced coenzymes. The link reaction and Krebs cycle generate reduced NAD and reduced FAD in the mitochondrial matrix.
  2. Electron transport chain. Electrons are released from the reduced coenzymes and pass along a series of carrier proteins in the inner mitochondrial membrane (the cristae), through a series of redox reactions, releasing energy at each transfer.
  3. Chemiosmosis. That energy actively pumps protons (H⁺) across the inner mitochondrial membrane, from the matrix into the intermembrane space, building an electrochemical (proton) gradient.
  4. ATP synthase. Protons diffuse back down the gradient through ATP synthase, embedded in the inner membrane, and this flow phosphorylates ADP + Pi to ATP.
  5. Final electron acceptor. Oxygen accepts the spent electrons and protons at the end of the chain, forming water — the only point in aerobic respiration that directly requires oxygen.
▶ 🚩 Trap — "stalked particles," and naming every stage

Writing "stalked particles" instead of ATP synthase is not credited. Name the specific molecule or location at every stage of the sequence above — cristae, intermembrane space, matrix, ATP synthase — one clear sentence per stage, rather than a single vague summary sentence covering all of it at once.

Why oxidative phosphorylation is the first stage to stop without oxygen

Without oxygen to act as the final electron acceptor, electrons cannot leave the end of the chain, so the chain backs up, the proton gradient collapses, and ATP synthase stops turning — meaning oxidative phosphorylation is the first stage to stop, not glycolysis, the link reaction or the Krebs cycle, all of which can continue briefly using existing stores of oxidised coenzyme.

🔁

Synoptic link — the same principle drives photosynthesis. Oxidative phosphorylation and the light-dependent reaction of photosynthesis both use an electron transport chain, a proton gradient across a membrane, and ATP synthase (chemiosmosis) — but they differ in electron source (reduced coenzymes here, versus chlorophyll and photolysis of water in photosynthesis) and in final electron acceptor (oxygen, forming water, here; NADP, forming reduced NADP, in photosynthesis).

A synoptic application: a faulty electron-transport-chain protein

Trace this the same way as any protein-structure question: a gene mutation leads to the wrong amino acid being incorporated into the carrier protein, which alters its tertiary structure, which impairs its function in the electron transport chain — reducing oxidative phosphorylation and therefore ATP production.

Section 9 · spec content point (continued)

🍖 Other respiratory substrates

Glucose isn't the only fuel a cell can respire — lipids and amino acids can both feed into the same pathway, just entering at different points.

📖

What the specification says (same content point as Sections 6–8): "…other respiratory substrates include the breakdown products of lipids and amino acids, which enter the Krebs cycle." (This section covers this final clause of the same dense content point introduced in Section 6.)

1
2
3

Fat and protein are like backup fuel tanks. When they get broken into smaller pieces, those pieces can join the same processing line as glucose — just at different points along it. Fat pieces hop on close to the Krebs cycle, and protein pieces hop on either right at the start (as pyruvate) or also near the Krebs cycle, once their nitrogen "tag" has been removed and turned into waste.

Lipids are hydrolysed into glycerol and fatty acids: glycerol is converted into triose phosphate and joins glycolysis directly, while fatty acids are broken into two-carbon fragments that become acetyl coenzyme A and enter the Krebs cycle. Amino acids are deaminated first — the amine group is removed and converted to urea for excretion — and what's left is converted into either pyruvate or a Krebs cycle intermediate. Because fatty acids carry many more hydrogen atoms than carbohydrate, breaking them down yields many more reduced coenzymes, and therefore considerably more ATP per gram.

Lipids — fatty acidsBroken into 2-carbon fragmentsBecome acetyl coenzyme A, enter the Krebs cycle
Amino acidsDeamination — amine group removed, converted to ureaThe remainder is converted to pyruvate or a Krebs cycle intermediate
📎

Why fat yields more ATP per gram. Fatty acids carry many more hydrogen atoms than an equivalent mass of carbohydrate, so their breakdown yields many more reduced coenzymes — and therefore much more ATP once those coenzymes pass through oxidative phosphorylation. This quantitative comparison point recurs across the course wherever energy storage in lipids is discussed.

Applied scenario — a fatty-acid transport inhibitor

If a substance blocks fatty acids from entering the mitochondrion, trace the consequence step by step: fatty acids cannot enter the mitochondrion → less acetyl coenzyme A enters the Krebs cycle → fewer reduced coenzymes are generated → less ATP is produced by oxidative phosphorylation. Any correctly-named stage of aerobic respiration used correctly within that chain is worth stating explicitly, rather than jumping straight from "blocked" to "less ATP."

🧪 Required practical 9: the respirometer

The practical skill attached to this whole spec point: measuring how fast single-celled organisms respire, and how that rate changes with a chosen variable.

📖

What the specification says: "Required practical 9: Investigation into the effect of a named variable on the rate of respiration of cultures of single-celled organisms."

1
2
3

Trap some yeast in a sealed tube and let them breathe. Use a chemical sponge to soak up the CO₂ they breathe out, so the only thing left changing is how much oxygen they've used up. Watch a little bead of liquid crawl along a thin tube as that oxygen gets used — the further it crawls in a set time, the faster the yeast are respiring.

Single-celled organisms (such as yeast) are placed in one tube, with an equal volume of an inert material (such as glass beads) in a matching control tube. Soda lime or potassium hydroxide absorbs any carbon dioxide produced, so any volume change left over reflects oxygen uptake only. Both tubes are left to equilibrate in a water bath at a set temperature, then the movement of manometer fluid is recorded over a fixed time and converted into a volume, which gives a rate of oxygen uptake.

Method

  1. Place the single-celled organisms (e.g. yeast) in one tube, and an equal volume of an inert material (e.g. glass beads) in a control tube.
  2. Add soda lime or potassium hydroxide to absorb the carbon dioxide produced, so any resulting volume or pressure change reflects oxygen uptake only.
  3. Leave both tubes to equilibrate in a water bath at a set temperature before taking any readings.
  4. Close the tap and record the movement of the coloured manometer fluid over a fixed time.
  5. Calculate the volume of oxygen used (using volume = πr² × distance moved), then divide by time to give a rate, and scale to organism mass to compare samples fairly.

📌

Purpose of the control tube: it corrects for changes in atmospheric pressure and temperature during the experiment — not for biological activity, since the inert material in it has none. Any fluid movement in the control tube must be subtracted from the experimental tube's reading.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "leave it to reach equilibrium," on its own

Asked why the apparatus is left for a period of time before readings start, "to reach equilibrium" is not enough on its own — the credited idea is that oxygen needs time to be absorbed and respired before a meaningful reading can be taken. Calculations that convert raw manometer displacement into a rate (dividing by time) are also a common source of careless arithmetic error — show every step of working.

Worked example — respirometer rate

Given a capillary tube radius r = 0.5 mm, with the fluid meniscus moving 18 mm in 6 minutes:

  • r² = 0.5² = 0.25 mm²
  • volume = π × 0.25 × 18 = 4.5π ≈ 14.14 mm³
  • rate = volume ÷ time = 14.14 ÷ 6 ≈ 2.36 mm³ per minute

The redox-indicator alternative

Methylene blue or TTC can be used instead, as an artificial hydrogen acceptor: it changes from blue to colourless as it is reduced by the respiring culture, accepting electrons and hydrogen in place of oxygen. Where a "time to colour change" measurement is used instead of a manometer reading, rate = 1 ÷ time. For example, if methylene blue decolourises in 40 seconds, rate = 1 ÷ 40 = 0.025 s⁻¹.

▶ Why this spec point carries a high memorisation and precision load

Respiration packs six or seven distinct sub-points into one topic, each with its own named stages, specific location, and its own numeric yield of ATP, reduced NAD, reduced FAD and CO₂, plus a required practical on top. The genuine application skill sits in tracing causal chains — exercise-physiology reasoning, predict-the-effect-of-more-substrate questions, and synoptic mutation/protein-structure links — through to a stated consequence, not just stopping at "more" or "less" ATP. Section 12 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

Respiration is a natural "mechanism" paragraph inside a longer essay-style answer on several different themes — the trick is to state the process precisely, then close with a sentence tying it back to whatever the actual title is asking about.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around the mitochondrion as an organelle

Aerobic respiration depends entirely on the double-membraned structure of the mitochondrion: the link reaction and Krebs cycle occur in the matrix, while the folded cristae of the inner membrane provide the large surface area needed to house the electron transport chain and ATP synthase for oxidative phosphorylation. Cells with a high ATP demand — such as muscle fibres or sperm cells — characteristically contain far more mitochondria than cells with a low ATP demand, directly linking organelle abundance to the rate at which a cell can generate usable energy.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around oxygen delivery

Oxygen is the final electron acceptor at the end of the electron transport chain, and is therefore essential to sustaining the bulk of ATP production by oxidative phosphorylation. This is precisely why efficient gas exchange surfaces and a mass transport system are needed in larger organisms: without a continuous, adequate oxygen supply to respiring tissue, oxidative phosphorylation is the first stage of the pathway to stop, forcing cells to rely on the far less efficient anaerobic route instead.

Whichever theme the essay is built around, treat respiration as the underlying mechanism paragraph: name the correct stage and location precisely, then add one sentence that explicitly links it back to the title — structure, gas exchange, exercise physiology, or energy flow through an ecosystem.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Respiration & the four-stage overview

  • Four linked stages: glycolysis (cytoplasm) → link reaction (matrix) → Krebs cycle (matrix) → oxidative phosphorylation (cristae).
  • Only oxidative phosphorylation needs O₂ directly.
  • Total ≈32 ATP per glucose (≈28 from oxidative phosphorylation) — not a figure to memorise precisely, just the stage sequence and per-stage numbers.
  • Never say "produces energy" — say "produces ATP" or "releases energy."

2 · Glycolysis: location & role

  • First stage of both aerobic and anaerobic respiration; occurs in the cytoplasm; itself an anaerobic process.
  • Substrate-level phosphorylation = direct transfer of phosphate from substrate to ADP (glycolysis, Krebs cycle) — different from oxidative phosphorylation.

3 · Glycolysis: the three stages

  • Phosphorylation of glucose (uses 2 ATP) → splits into 2× triose phosphate → oxidised to pyruvate (net gain ATP + reduced NAD).
  • Per glucose: net 2 ATP, 2 reduced NAD, 2 pyruvate.

4 · Anaerobic respiration: lactate & ethanol

  • Animals: pyruvate + reduced NAD → lactate + oxidised NAD (no CO₂).
  • Yeast/plants: pyruvate → ethanal + CO₂, then ethanal + reduced NAD → ethanol + oxidised NAD (CO₂ released).
  • Purpose: regenerate oxidised NAD so glycolysis (2 ATP/glucose) can continue without O₂ — anaerobic respiration yields only that 2 ATP.
  • Muscle: less O₂ → more anaerobic respiration → lactate → fatigue → lactate leaves via facilitated diffusion.

5 · Pyruvate entering the mitochondrion

  • If aerobic, pyruvate enters the mitochondrial matrix by active transport (uses ATP, carrier proteins).

6 · The link reaction

  • Pyruvate oxidised to acetate (→ reduced NAD) AND decarboxylated (→ CO₂) — two separate ideas.
  • Acetate + coenzyme A → acetyl coenzyme A. Occurs ×2 per glucose, matrix, no ATP made directly.

7 · The Krebs cycle

  • Acetyl CoA + 4C molecule → releases CoA + forms 6C molecule → oxidation-reduction reactions back to 4C molecule.
  • Per turn: 3 reduced NAD, 1 reduced FAD, 1 ATP (substrate-level), 2 CO₂. Turns ×2 per glucose.
  • Use "four-carbon"/"six-carbon" — no named intermediates. Never "reduced NADP" here.

8 · Oxidative phosphorylation

  • Reduced NAD/FAD oxidised → electrons pass along the electron transport chain (cristae), releasing energy.
  • Energy pumps H⁺ into intermembrane space → gradient → H⁺ back through ATP synthase → ATP (chemiosmosis).
  • O₂ = final electron acceptor, forms water — only O₂-requiring step. Never "stalked particles."

9 · Other respiratory substrates

  • Lipids: glycerol → triose phosphate (glycolysis); fatty acids → acetyl CoA (Krebs cycle).
  • Amino acids: deaminated (→ urea), remainder → pyruvate or a Krebs cycle intermediate.
  • Fat yields more ATP/gram — more hydrogen atoms → more reduced coenzymes.

10 · Required practical 9: the respirometer

  • Organisms vs inert control; soda lime/KOH absorbs CO₂ so change reflects O₂ uptake only.
  • Rate = volume (πr² × distance) ÷ time. Redox-indicator alternative: rate = 1 ÷ time.
  • Control tube corrects for pressure/temperature change, not biological activity.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Respiration & the four-stage overview

❓ State the four stages of aerobic respiration in order, giving the location of each.
Glycolysis, in the cytoplasm; the link reaction, in the mitochondrial matrix; the Krebs cycle, in the mitochondrial matrix; oxidative phosphorylation, on the inner mitochondrial membrane (cristae).
❓ A student writes "respiration produces energy for the cell." Rewrite this using precise, full-credit wording.
Respiration produces ATP; hydrolysis of ATP then releases energy for use in cellular processes.

Glycolysis: location & role

❓ Explain why glycolysis is described as an anaerobic process, even in a cell that is respiring aerobically.
Glycolysis takes place in the cytoplasm and does not require oxygen at any stage; it is the shared first stage of both aerobic and anaerobic respiration, and proceeds identically regardless of whether oxygen is subsequently available.
❓ Distinguish between substrate-level phosphorylation and oxidative phosphorylation.
Substrate-level phosphorylation is the direct transfer of a phosphate group from an intermediate substrate molecule to ADP, forming ATP; it occurs in glycolysis and the Krebs cycle. Oxidative phosphorylation instead makes ATP indirectly, driven by the electron transport chain and the diffusion of protons through ATP synthase (chemiosmosis).

Glycolysis: the three stages

❓ Describe the stages of glycolysis and state the net yield of ATP and reduced NAD per glucose.
Glucose is phosphorylated to glucose phosphate, using 2 ATP; glucose phosphate splits into two molecules of triose phosphate; each triose phosphate is oxidised to pyruvate, with a gain of ATP and reduced NAD. Per glucose, 4 ATP are made and 2 are used, giving a net gain of 2 ATP, plus 2 reduced NAD and 2 pyruvate.
❓ Explain why the ATP yield of glycolysis is described as a "net" gain rather than simply stating the ATP produced.
Two ATP are used to phosphorylate glucose at the start of the pathway, while four ATP are made later by substrate-level phosphorylation; subtracting the ATP used from the ATP made gives the net gain of two ATP per glucose.

Anaerobic respiration: lactate & ethanol

❓ Compare the lactate and ethanol pathways of anaerobic respiration.
Both convert pyruvate using reduced NAD, regenerating oxidised NAD so glycolysis can continue. In animals, pyruvate is reduced directly to lactate by lactate dehydrogenase, releasing no carbon dioxide. In yeast and plants, pyruvate is first decarboxylated to ethanal, releasing carbon dioxide, and ethanal is then reduced to ethanol, also regenerating oxidised NAD.
❓ Explain the consequences of anaerobic respiration in exercising muscle.
As oxygen supply becomes limiting, less ATP is produced by oxidative phosphorylation; to meet ATP demand, anaerobic respiration increases, producing lactate; lactate accumulation contributes to muscle fatigue, and lactate leaves the muscle fibre by facilitated diffusion.
❓ Explain why an athlete with more mitochondria in their muscle fibres can sustain exercise for longer before fatiguing.
More mitochondria allow more aerobic respiration, producing more ATP per glucose than anaerobic respiration alone; this delays the onset of anaerobic respiration, so less lactate is produced and fatigue is delayed.

Pyruvate entering the mitochondrion

❓ Describe how pyruvate produced by glycolysis reaches the site of the link reaction.
Pyruvate is actively transported from the cytoplasm into the mitochondrial matrix, using ATP and specific carrier proteins in the mitochondrial membranes; this occurs when respiration is aerobic.
❓ A cell is placed in anaerobic conditions. Explain what happens to pyruvate instead of being transported into the mitochondrion.
Without oxygen, pyruvate remains in the cytoplasm and is converted to lactate or ethanol using reduced NAD, rather than being actively transported into the mitochondrial matrix for further oxidation.

The link reaction

❓ Describe the link reaction.
Pyruvate is oxidised to acetate, producing reduced NAD; pyruvate is separately decarboxylated, releasing carbon dioxide; acetate then combines with coenzyme A to produce acetylcoenzyme A. This occurs twice per glucose, in the mitochondrial matrix, and produces no ATP directly.
❓ A student states that "pyruvate is broken down in the link reaction." Explain why this answer would not gain full credit, and rewrite it.
The statement fails to separate two distinct, separately credited processes. Better: pyruvate is oxidised (loses hydrogen, reducing NAD) and independently decarboxylated (loses carbon dioxide), before the resulting acetate combines with coenzyme A to form acetylcoenzyme A.

The Krebs cycle

❓ Describe the Krebs cycle, including its yield per turn.
Acetylcoenzyme A reacts with a four-carbon molecule, releasing coenzyme A and forming a six-carbon molecule; in a series of oxidation-reduction reactions, this six-carbon molecule is decarboxylated and dehydrogenated back to the four-carbon molecule. Per turn this releases two molecules of carbon dioxide, generates three reduced NAD and one reduced FAD, and produces one ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation. The cycle turns twice per glucose.
❓ Predict the effect on ATP production of significantly more acetylcoenzyme A entering the Krebs cycle.
More acetylcoenzyme A entering the cycle generates more reduced coenzymes; these pass more electrons to the electron transport chain, so more ATP is produced by oxidative phosphorylation, which could support a stated physiological outcome such as increased endurance.

Oxidative phosphorylation

❓ Describe how ATP is made by oxidative phosphorylation.
Reduced NAD and reduced FAD are oxidised, releasing electrons; these electrons pass along a series of carrier proteins in the inner mitochondrial membrane (the electron transport chain), releasing energy; this energy actively pumps protons across the inner membrane into the intermembrane space, creating an electrochemical gradient; protons diffuse back through ATP synthase, which catalyses the phosphorylation of ADP and Pi to ATP; oxygen acts as the final electron acceptor, combining with electrons and protons to form water.
❓ Explain why oxidative phosphorylation stops almost immediately when oxygen is unavailable, while the Krebs cycle continues briefly.
Without oxygen as the final electron acceptor, electrons cannot leave the electron transport chain, so the chain becomes backed up and the proton gradient collapses, stopping ATP synthase; the Krebs cycle can continue briefly using any remaining stores of oxidised coenzyme, but will itself stop once these are used up.
❓ A mutation alters the tertiary structure of a carrier protein in the electron transport chain. Explain the effect on ATP production.
The mutation causes the wrong amino acid to be incorporated into the protein, altering its tertiary structure; since the protein's function in the electron transport chain depends on its precise shape, this impairs electron transfer, reducing oxidative phosphorylation and ATP production.

Other respiratory substrates

❓ Explain how lipids are used as a respiratory substrate.
Lipids are hydrolysed to glycerol and fatty acids. Glycerol is converted to triose phosphate and enters glycolysis. Fatty acids are broken down into two-carbon fragments that combine with coenzyme A to form acetylcoenzyme A, which enters the Krebs cycle directly.
❓ Explain why lipids yield more ATP per gram than carbohydrates when respired.
Fatty acids contain many more hydrogen atoms per gram than carbohydrate; breaking them down therefore generates many more reduced coenzymes, which yield more ATP once passed through oxidative phosphorylation.

Required practical 9: the respirometer

❓ Describe how a respirometer is used to investigate the effect of a variable on the rate of respiration of a single-celled organism.
The organisms are placed in one tube, with an equal volume of an inert material in a control tube; soda lime or potassium hydroxide is added to absorb carbon dioxide, so any change in gas volume reflects oxygen uptake only; both tubes are left to equilibrate in a water bath at a set temperature; the movement of manometer fluid is then recorded over a fixed time and converted to a volume using πr² × distance moved, then divided by time to give a rate.
❓ A capillary tube of radius 0.5 mm shows the manometer fluid moving 18 mm in 6 minutes. Calculate the rate of oxygen uptake.
Volume = π × 0.5² × 18 = 4.5π ≈ 14.14 mm³. Rate = 14.14 ÷ 6 ≈ 2.36 mm³ per minute.
❓ Explain the purpose of the control tube containing glass beads instead of respiring organisms.
The control tube corrects for changes in atmospheric pressure and temperature during the experiment, since these would otherwise also move the manometer fluid; it contains no respiring organisms, so any movement recorded in it is not due to biological activity and can be subtracted from the experimental reading.
