Control of Blood Glucose Concentration

How insulin and glucagon run two completely separate, mirror-image negative-feedback loops to hold blood glucose within narrow limits, how adrenaline shares glucagon's second-messenger machinery but not its full toolkit, and how type I and type II diabetes fail this system in two genuinely different ways.

⚠️

The one habit that protects the most marks on this topic: a hormone never "converts" glucose itself — it activates enzymes that do the converting, and it binds a receptor, never an "active site" (that word belongs to enzymes). Keep three near-identical terms straight at all times: glycogenesis (glucose → glycogen), glycogenolysis (glycogen → glucose) and gluconeogenesis (new glucose from amino acids and glycerol — never from glycogen). Using the wrong one of these three names disqualifies the mark outright, however good the rest of the answer is.

Section 1 · spec content point

⚖️ The factors that influence blood glucose concentration

Blood glucose concentration is never static — it's constantly being pushed up by some processes and pulled down by others, and it's the balance between them, held by negative feedback, that keeps it within narrow limits. This section is the overview; Sections 2–6 zoom into exactly how each hormone drives these factors.

📖

What the specification says: "The factors that influence blood glucose concentration."

1
2
3

Think of blood glucose like the fuel gauge in a car. Some things top the tank up — eating a carb-rich meal, or the liver making new fuel from spare parts. Other things drain it — muscles burning fuel when you exercise, or the body tucking fuel away into storage. The body's whole job is to stop the gauge running too high or too low, using two hormones that pull in opposite directions.

Blood glucose rises when carbohydrate is digested and absorbed from the gut, and when the liver either breaks down its stored glycogen (glycogenolysis) or builds brand new glucose from other molecules (gluconeogenesis). It falls when cells respire glucose for energy — especially exercising muscle — when the liver and muscle store glucose away as glycogen (glycogenesis), and when cells take glucose up to convert into fat. Keeping this balanced matters: too much glucose draws water out of cells by osmosis and can cause glucose to spill into the urine; too little starves cells, especially in the brain, of the fuel they need for respiration.

Raises blood glucose concentrationLowers blood glucose concentration
Digestion and absorption of carbohydrate from the diet, in the small intestineRespiration by cells, especially in exercising muscle — glucose is used up, not just stored
Glycogenolysis — glycogen broken down to glucose (liver, and to a lesser extent muscle)Glycogenesis — glucose converted to glycogen (liver and muscle)
Gluconeogenesis — new glucose made from non-carbohydrate sources, in the liverCellular uptake of glucose for storage, or conversion to fat

Why it has to be controlled so tightly

  • Too high: the water potential of the blood falls, so water leaves cells by osmosis, which can dehydrate and damage them; if the concentration in the kidney filtrate exceeds what the reabsorption co-transporter proteins in the proximal convoluted tubule can handle, the excess glucose is not reabsorbed and appears in the urine — glycosuria.
  • Too low: cells — particularly brain cells, which rely almost entirely on glucose — have too little respiratory substrate, so too little ATP is produced and normal function is impaired.
Two separate, mirror-image negative-feedback pathways — never merge them into one loop. Having two independent mechanisms, rather than one, gives tighter control than a single reversible system could (the worked example of the general homeostasis principle from 3.6.4.1).
▶ 🚩 Trap — getting the "-genesis/-lysis" names wrong

These three process names look and sound alike, and mixing them up loses the mark even if the rest of the answer is correct. In a gluconeogenesis question, ignore mentions of lipids, fats or proteins as sources — but reject glycogen as a source outright, since glycogen is a carbohydrate and converting it is glycogenolysis, not gluconeogenesis. None of the three processes happens in the pancreas — the pancreas only detects blood glucose and secretes the hormones; the conversions themselves happen in the liver (glycogenesis also in muscle).

Section 2 · spec content point

🧪 The role of the liver: glycogenesis, glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis

One organ, three named conversions — and precise definitions matter more here than almost anywhere else in the spec, because an incorrect process name disqualifies the mark outright, however sound the surrounding biology is.

📖

What the specification says: "The role of the liver in glycogenesis, glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis."

1
2
3

Imagine glucose as loose coins and glycogen as coins rolled up into a tube for storage. Rolling coins into a tube is glycogenesis. Unrolling the tube back into loose coins is glycogenolysis. And making brand new coins out of completely different raw materials — not from an existing tube — is gluconeogenesis. All three happen in the liver.

Glycogenesis converts glucose into glycogen for storage, and happens in both the liver and muscle. Glycogenolysis converts glycogen back into glucose, mainly in the liver. Gluconeogenesis makes entirely new glucose molecules from non-carbohydrate starting materials — amino acids and glycerol — and only happens in the liver. The easiest way to muddle these is to think gluconeogenesis means "glycogen turning into glucose" — it doesn't; that's glycogenolysis. Gluconeogenesis never starts from glycogen at all.

GlycogenesisGlucose → glycogen (via glycogen synthase)Liver and muscle
GlycogenolysisGlycogen → glucose (hydrolysis)Liver (glucose from muscle glycogenolysis is used locally by the muscle rather than released into the blood, since muscle cells lack the enzyme needed to release free glucose)
GluconeogenesisNew glucose made from non-carbohydrate sources — specifically amino acids and glycerol — never from glycogenLiver only
📌

The specifically recorded misconception to avoid: believing gluconeogenesis means "glycogen converted to glucose." It doesn't — that is glycogenolysis. Gluconeogenesis is defined purely by its non-carbohydrate starting materials: amino acids and glycerol.

▶ Two extra terms that get confused with these three

Two other terms sound similar but are not the ones named on this spec point: glycolysis is the breakdown of glucose during respiration (from 3.1.3/3.5), not a blood-glucose-control process at all; and "glucogenesis" is not a recognised biological term — it's simply a slip candidates make while trying to write glycogenesis or gluconeogenesis under pressure. Building a five-word glossary card — glycolysis, glycogenesis, glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis, and the non-word "glucogenesis" as a warning — and drilling the correct spelling and definition of each is one of the highest-value five minutes you can spend on this spec point.

Section 3 · spec content point

💉 The action of insulin: lowering blood glucose

A single unbroken chain from detection to effect — learn it start to finish, in order, using "activates enzymes" rather than "converts" at every step.

📖

What the specification says: "The action of insulin by: attaching to receptors on the surfaces of target cells; controlling the uptake of glucose by regulating the inclusion of channel proteins in the surface membranes of target cells; activating enzymes involved in the conversion of glucose to glycogen."

1
2
3

When there's too much sugar in the blood, special pancreas cells send out a messenger called insulin. Insulin knocks on the door of liver and muscle cells. That knock does two things: it opens more little doorways so sugar can flood into the cell, and it tells the cell to start tucking spare sugar away into storage. Both jobs bring the blood sugar level back down.

Beta cells in the pancreas detect that blood glucose has risen and release insulin into the blood. Insulin binds to receptors on liver and muscle cells, and this binding does two things: it causes more glucose channel proteins to be inserted into the cell membrane, so more glucose diffuses in, and it switches on enzymes that convert glucose into glycogen for storage. Both effects together bring blood glucose back down — a classic negative feedback response.

  1. Beta cells detect a rise in blood glucose and secrete insulin
  2. Insulin binds receptors on target cells (liver, muscle)
  3. More channel proteins are inserted into the membrane → more glucose enters
  4. Enzymes are activated that convert glucose to glycogen (glycogenesis)
  5. Blood glucose falls back to normal — negative feedback
  1. A rise in blood glucose concentration is detected by beta (β) cells in the islets of Langerhans, which secrete insulin into the blood.
  2. Insulin attaches to specific receptors on the surface membrane of target cells — mainly liver and muscle cells (also fat cells).
  3. This causes vesicles containing channel proteins to move to and fuse with the cell-surface membrane, increasing the number of channel proteins present and so increasing the rate of facilitated diffusion of glucose into the cell.
  4. Insulin also activates enzymes — including glycogen synthase — that convert glucose to glycogen: glycogenesis.
  5. Blood glucose concentration falls back towards normal, by negative feedback.
📌

Insulin's two effects — more channel proteins inserted, and enzymes activated for glycogenesis — are both separately credited; giving only one of them is an incomplete answer to "describe the action of insulin."

▶ 🚩 Two rejected phrasings to avoid completely

Glucose entering through the channel proteins is facilitated diffusion — "active transport" is rejected, since glucose is moving down its concentration gradient without requiring ATP. And insulin activates enzymes that convert glucose to glycogen — "insulin converts glucose to glycogen" is rejected, because the hormone is not the catalyst itself; it switches on the enzyme that does the converting.

Section 4 · spec content point

🩸 The action of glucagon: raising blood glucose

Glucagon's mirror-image counterpart to insulin — but note immediately that it only ever targets the liver, never muscle, and that it drives two separate glucose-raising processes rather than one.

📖

What the specification says: "The action of glucagon by: attaching to receptors on the surfaces of target cells; activating enzymes involved in the conversion of glycogen to glucose; activating enzymes involved in the conversion of glycerol and amino acids into glucose."

1
2
3

When blood sugar runs low, a different pancreas messenger, glucagon, heads straight to the liver — and only the liver, never the muscles. It tells the liver two things: unpack some of the sugar you've got stored away, and also, build some brand new sugar out of spare parts you weren't planning to use as sugar at all. Both jobs push more sugar out into the blood.

Alpha cells in the pancreas detect a fall in blood glucose and release glucagon. Glucagon binds receptors on liver cells only — muscle cells don't have glucagon receptors. Through a second-messenger cascade (covered in Section 6), it activates enzymes that break stored glycogen back down into glucose (glycogenolysis), and separately activates enzymes that build entirely new glucose molecules from glycerol and amino acids (gluconeogenesis). Glucose then diffuses out of the liver cells into the blood, raising blood glucose concentration back towards normal.

  1. A fall in blood glucose concentration is detected by alpha (α) cells in the islets of Langerhans, which secrete glucagon into the blood.
  2. Glucagon attaches to specific receptors on the surface of liver cells only — unlike insulin, it does not act on muscle cells.
  3. Via the second-messenger cascade (Section 6), glucagon activates enzymes that convert glycogen to glucose: glycogenolysis.
  4. Glucagon also activates enzymes that convert glycerol and amino acids into glucose: gluconeogenesis — never from glycogen.
  5. Glucose diffuses out of liver cells into the blood; blood glucose concentration rises back towards normal, by negative feedback.
🔁

Synoptic link: if blood (and therefore kidney filtrate) glucose concentration becomes very high, the co-transporter proteins that reabsorb glucose in the proximal convoluted tubule become saturated — already working at their maximum rate — so not all the filtered glucose can be reabsorbed, and the excess appears in the urine as glycosuria. This is the same co-transport-saturation logic reused for kidney reabsorption in 3.6.4.3, and the basis of a positive urine glucose test.

▶ 🚩 Trap — rejected phrasing for glucagon's action

"Glucagon converts glycogen to glucose" and "glucagon produces enzymes" are both rejected — the required wording is that glucagon attaches to receptors and activates/stimulates enzymes, which then carry out the conversion. As with gluconeogenesis generally, ignore mentions of lipids, fats or proteins as raw material, but reject glycogen specifically as a source.

Section 5 · spec content point

⚡ The role of adrenaline

Adrenaline shares glucagon's signalling machinery but not its full toolkit — the liver-only-versus-liver-and-muscle contrast, and the fact adrenaline never triggers gluconeogenesis, is the single most reliably tested distinction on this whole spec point.

📖

What the specification says: "The role of adrenaline by: attaching to receptors on the surfaces of target cells; activating enzymes involved in the conversion of glycogen to glucose."

1
2
3

Adrenaline is the "danger!" hormone — it doesn't come from the pancreas at all, but from glands sitting above the kidneys, and it fires during fear, stress or exercise. Like glucagon, it tells cells to unpack stored sugar. But adrenaline shouts at both the liver and the muscles, because in an emergency the muscles need that sugar right where they are, ready to burn immediately.

Adrenaline is released from the adrenal glands, not the islets of Langerhans, in response to stress, fear or exercise. It binds receptors on both liver and muscle cells — unlike glucagon, which only acts on the liver — and uses the same second-messenger cascade to activate glycogenolysis (glycogen converted to glucose). Unlike glucagon, adrenaline never triggers gluconeogenesis.

  • Secreted by the adrenal glands (the medulla) — an entirely separate endocrine organ, above the kidneys, not the islets of Langerhans — in response to stress, fear or exercise ("fight or flight").
  • Attaches to receptors on the surface of target cells: both liver and muscle cells (glucagon: liver only).
  • Uses the same second-messenger cascade as glucagon (Section 6) to activate enzymes that convert glycogen to glucose — glycogenolysis.
  • Adrenaline never causes gluconeogenesis — that remains a glucagon-only process.

🚩

The most reliably tested contrast on this spec point: do not give glucagon a muscle target, and do not give adrenaline the power to cause gluconeogenesis. If a question asks which hormone would raise blood glucose fastest during sudden exercise, the answer hinges on adrenaline acting on muscle directly, not on any difference in the underlying cascade.

Section 6 · spec content point

🔄 The second messenger model of adrenaline and glucagon action

One of the most tightly mark-scheme-policed sequences in the whole specification — many answers correctly state the initial hormone-receptor binding and the final effect, but lose marks by skipping the intermediate detail in between.

📖

What the specification says: "The second messenger model of adrenaline and glucagon action, involving adenylate cyclase, cyclic AMP (cAMP) and protein kinase."

1
2
3

Glucagon and adrenaline can't get inside the cell — they're too big and the wrong type of molecule. So instead they knock on the outside of the door (the receptor), and that knock sets off a chain reaction of messages inside the cell, each one triggering the next, like a row of dominoes, ending with stored sugar being unpacked.

Glucagon or adrenaline (the "first messenger") binds a receptor on the outside of the target cell without ever entering it. This switches on an enzyme called adenylate cyclase on the inside of the membrane, which converts ATP into a signal molecule called cyclic AMP — the "second messenger," made inside the cell. Cyclic AMP then switches on another enzyme, protein kinase, which sets off a cascade of further enzyme activations, ending in glycogenolysis. A second messenger is needed at all because glucagon and adrenaline are protein-based and can't cross the cell membrane directly, unlike a steroid hormone.

  1. Glucagon or adrenaline — the first messenger — binds to a specific receptor on the outside of the target cell's surface membrane; it does not enter the cell.
  2. This activates the enzyme adenylate cyclase, located on the inside face of the membrane.
  3. Activated adenylate cyclase converts ATP into cyclic AMP (cAMP) — the second messenger, made inside the cell.
  4. cAMP activates protein kinase.
  5. Protein kinase activates a cascade of further enzymes, including those responsible for glycogenolysis — glycogen converted to glucose, which then diffuses out of the cell and blood glucose rises.
Learn the exact chain: receptor → adenylate cyclase activated → ATP converted to cyclic AMP → protein kinase activated → glycogenolysis enzymes activated. Partial chains are the most common way marks are lost here.

Why a second messenger is needed at all

Glucagon and adrenaline are proteins or protein-derived, so they are not lipid-soluble and cannot cross the phospholipid cell-surface membrane directly — unlike a steroid hormone (e.g. oestrogen, 3.8.2.2), which diffuses straight through and binds an intracellular receptor. The signal has to be relayed across the membrane instead, which is exactly the job of the second messenger, cAMP.

📌

Keep the two messengers separate. FIRST messenger = glucagon/adrenaline — stays outside the cell, binds the receptor. SECOND messenger = cAMP — made inside the cell by adenylate cyclase, from ATP. This is also why the cascade amplifies the original signal: each activated enzyme goes on to activate many more molecules of the next enzyme in the chain, so a tiny amount of hormone produces a large, rapid response.

▶ 🚩 The single costliest omission in this sequence

Many answers correctly state the initial hormone-receptor binding and the final glycogenolysis effect, but miss the intermediate detail — that cAMP is produced from ATP, and that protein kinase is activated — even though both of these are separately credited steps. Never let "receptor binds hormone… glycogen breaks down" stand in for the full five-step chain.

Section 7 · spec content point

🩺 The causes of type I and type II diabetes and their control

Two genuinely different failures of the same control system — never swap which one is autoimmune and which one is receptor-based, and never reduce type II to "the pancreas stopped working."

📖

What the specification says: "The causes of types I and II diabetes and their control by insulin and/or manipulation of the diet."

1
2
3

In type I diabetes, the body's own immune system accidentally destroys the factory that makes insulin, so hardly any gets made at all — it usually starts when someone's young, and they need insulin injections for life. In type II diabetes, the factory is still making insulin just fine, but the doors on the cells that are supposed to respond to it have gotten sticky and don't open properly any more — it usually develops later in life and is often managed first with diet and exercise.

Type I diabetes is caused by the immune system destroying the insulin-producing beta cells, so little or no insulin is produced — it usually starts early in life and is controlled with regular insulin injections. Type II diabetes is caused by target cells, or their receptors, becoming less responsive to insulin, even though the pancreas is still producing it — it usually develops later in life, is strongly linked to obesity, diet and inactivity, and is typically controlled first through diet and exercise, with medication or (in severe cases) insulin added later. Insulin can't just be swallowed as a tablet, because as a protein it would be digested before it reached the blood.

Type IType II
CauseAutoimmune destruction of the beta cells of the islets of Langerhans — little or no insulin is producedCells/receptors become less responsive to (or resistant to) insulin, even though insulin is still produced — often fewer or faulty receptors

Why insulin cannot be taken as a tablet

Insulin is a protein, so if swallowed it would be hydrolysed by gut peptidases (protein-digesting enzymes) during digestion, breaking it down into amino acids before it could have any effect — it must be injected directly into the blood or tissue instead.

🚩

For type II diabetes, stating only "the pancreas is not damaged" is too vague. The precise idea required is that insulin is still produced, but target cells/receptors no longer respond normally to it — describing this as the body being "immune" to insulin is a specifically recorded misconception, not the credited term.

Evaluating health-advisers-versus-food-industry positions on rising type II incidence

This spec point also asks you to weigh two positions on why type II diabetes is becoming more common. Health advisers point to the correlation between high-sugar, high-fat diets and rising type II incidence, and to the resulting cost to health services. The food industry points to consumer choice, and to other contributing factors — inactivity, genetics, age, ethnicity — arguing that correlation does not prove causation. A strong answer weighs both sides and states what further evidence would help settle the disagreement, and makes both halves of the diet/exercise link explicit — as a contributing cause and as part of treatment/management — since covering only one half earns only partial credit.

Section 8 · spec content point · Required Practical 11

🧫 RP11: the glucose calibration curve

A dilution series feeding a quantitative colour-change test, read on a colorimeter, to build a calibration curve you then use to identify an unknown concentration — a technique that turns up across the specification wherever an unknown concentration needs to be measured indirectly.

📖

What the specification says: "Required practical 11: Production of a dilution series of a glucose solution and use of colorimetric techniques to produce a calibration curve with which to identify the concentration of glucose in an unknown 'urine' sample."

1
2
3

Make several test tubes with known amounts of sugar in them, from lots down to none. Add a colour-changing chemical to each — the more sugar, the more the colour changes. Use a machine that measures exactly how much the colour has changed for each known tube, and draw a graph. Then test the mystery tube the exact same way, find where its result lands on the graph, and read off how much sugar it must contain.

Make a dilution series of glucose solutions with known concentrations, and carry out a quantitative Benedict's test on each by heating with excess Benedict's reagent — the more glucose present, the more the blue colour is replaced by an insoluble red-brown precipitate. Use a colorimeter to measure the colour or absorbance of each known tube, and plot a calibration curve of concentration against colorimeter reading. Finally, treat the unknown "urine" sample identically and read its glucose concentration straight off the calibration curve.

  1. Prepare a dilution series of glucose solutions of known, different concentrations from a single stock solution.
  2. Carry out a quantitative Benedict's test on each — heat with excess Benedict's reagent for a fixed time, which is reduced to form an insoluble red-brown precipitate (more glucose present → more precipitate → less blue colour remaining).
  3. Filter or centrifuge off the precipitate (or measure the remaining blue colour directly), then use a colorimeter to measure the absorbance or percentage transmission of each known tube, and plot a calibration curve — known concentration on one axis, colorimeter reading on the other.
  4. Treat the unknown "urine" sample under identical conditions, take its colorimeter reading, and read its glucose concentration off the calibration curve.
📌

Control the variables that would otherwise invalidate the comparison: the volume of Benedict's reagent added, the temperature of heating, and the length of heating time must all be kept identical across every tube in the dilution series and the unknown sample.

The quantitative Benedict's test used here (heating a measured volume with excess reagent, then reading colour precisely on a colorimeter) is deliberately different from the qualitative/semi-quantitative version used elsewhere just to test for the presence or absence of a reducing sugar by eye — this practical needs a precise numerical readout, not just a colour band.

▶ Evaluating a urine colour-chart test against this method

A simple urine colour chart shows a positive correlation between colour and glucose concentration, but a range of concentrations can produce overlapping colours on the chart — it is only an arbitrary scale, the colour judgement is subjective, and there are relatively few distinct colour bands to choose between. The colorimeter-based calibration curve in RP11 gives a continuous, objective numerical reading instead, which is why it is the preferred quantitative method.

Section 10 · exam technique

🔁 Reasoning through blocked-pathway questions

The dominant application format on this spec point doesn't ask you to reproduce the normal sequence — it removes or blocks one step (a faulty receptor, a drug that inhibits an enzyme) and asks you to reason forward to the consequence, in strict "less/no" wording at every single step.

🧭

How to tackle any blocked-pathway question: never just restate the normal forward sequence. Identify exactly which step is blocked, then reason forward from that point, writing "less" or "no" at every subsequent step in the chain, and finishing on the effect on blood glucose concentration itself. An answer that never uses "less/no" language anywhere is typically capped well below full marks, even if the underlying idea is broadly right.

▶ 🧪 Worked example 1 — a non-functional insulin receptor

Scenario: a mutation results in a non-functional insulin receptor on liver cell membranes. Predict the consequence.

Reasoning: insulin cannot bind to the non-functional receptor → fewer/no vesicles containing channel proteins fuse with the cell-surface membrane → fewer/no channel proteins are inserted into the membrane → less/no glucose diffuses into the cell by facilitated diffusion → glycogenesis is not activated in that cell → blood glucose concentration is not lowered as effectively.

▶ 🧪 Worked example 2 — a drug that inhibits adenylate cyclase

Scenario: a drug specifically inhibits adenylate cyclase in liver cells. Predict the effect on glucagon's ability to raise blood glucose, using "less/no" wording throughout.

Reasoning: glucagon still binds its receptor normally, but with adenylate cyclase inhibited, less/no ATP is converted to cyclic AMP → less/no protein kinase is activated → less/no glycogenolysis occurs → less/no glucose is released into the blood → blood glucose concentration rises less than normal, or not at all.

🚩

Many candidates correctly identify the general idea — "insulin won't work properly" — but lose marks by never writing the intermediate steps in "less/no" form. The mark scheme rewards each individual step of the chain being explicitly weakened, not just the final outcome being stated.

Section 11 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

This content makes a strong supporting paragraph inside a longer essay-style answer on more than one theme — the trick is closing each paragraph with a sentence that ties the mechanism explicitly back to the title, rather than repeating a generic summary.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around receptor specificity

Insulin binds to receptors on the surface of target cells (liver, muscle, fat), increasing glucose uptake via channel proteins and activating glycogenesis; glucagon and adrenaline attach to receptors on target cells and activate enzymes — via the second messenger cascade of adenylate cyclase, cAMP and protein kinase — for glycogenolysis and, in glucagon's case, gluconeogenesis. In every case it is the hormone activating enzymes via a specific receptor, never converting the substrate itself. Link to title: hormonal control of blood glucose depends entirely on receptor binding at target cells — without a functioning receptor, a cell cannot respond to insulin or glucagon, which is exactly what goes wrong in type II diabetes.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around negative feedback

Blood glucose concentration is controlled by two separate, mirror-image negative feedback loops rather than one reversible system: beta cells respond to a rise by secreting insulin, which lowers blood glucose via increased uptake and glycogenesis; alpha cells respond to a fall by secreting glucagon, which raises it via glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis in the liver. Having two independent mechanisms, rather than one, gives a greater degree of control than a single system reversing itself could achieve.

Whichever essay theme this content supports, treat it as the "mechanism" paragraph: state the process precisely — naming the correct "-genesis/-lysis" term, or the exact second-messenger chain, depending on what the title is asking about — then add one sentence linking it explicitly back to that theme.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Factors affecting blood glucose

  • Raises: diet (carb digestion/absorption); glycogenolysis; gluconeogenesis.
  • Lowers: respiration (esp. exercising muscle); glycogenesis; cellular uptake for storage/fat.
  • Too high → lowers blood water potential + glycosuria; too low → too little respiratory substrate, especially for the brain.

2 · The liver's three pathways

  • Glycogenesis = glucose→glycogen (liver + muscle). Glycogenolysis = glycogen→glucose (liver). Gluconeogenesis = new glucose from amino acids/glycerol (liver only, never from glycogen).
  • Wrong process name = zero marks, however good the rest of the answer is. Never "occurs in the pancreas."

3 · Insulin

  • Beta cells detect a rise, secrete insulin → binds receptors on liver/muscle/fat cells.
  • Vesicles with channel proteins fuse with membrane → more facilitated diffusion of glucose in.
  • Activates enzymes (glycogen synthase) → glycogenesis. "Activates," never "converts"; facilitated diffusion, never "active transport."

4 · Glucagon

  • Alpha cells detect a fall, secrete glucagon → binds receptors on liver cells only.
  • Via cAMP cascade, activates glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis; glucose diffuses out into the blood.

5 · Adrenaline

  • From the adrenal glands (medulla), not the islets — released under stress/fear/exercise.
  • Binds receptors on liver and muscle; same cascade as glucagon but activates glycogenolysis only — never gluconeogenesis.

6 · Second messenger model

  • Receptor → adenylate cyclase activated → ATP→cAMP → protein kinase activated → glycogenolysis enzymes activated.
  • 1st messenger = glucagon/adrenaline (outside); 2nd messenger = cAMP (inside, from ATP). Needed because hormones are protein-based, not lipid-soluble.
  • Reversed/blocked-form answers must use "less/no" at every single step.

7 · Type I & type II diabetes

  • Type I: autoimmune beta-cell destruction, little/no insulin, early onset, insulin injections.
  • Type II: cells/receptors less responsive (insulin still produced), later onset, linked to obesity/diet/inactivity, controlled by diet/exercise/medication.
  • Insulin can't be a tablet — it's a protein, hydrolysed by gut peptidases.

8 · RP11 calibration curve

  • Dilution series of known glucose concentrations → quantitative Benedict's test (excess reagent, heat).
  • Colorimeter reading → calibration curve (known concentration vs reading).
  • Treat unknown "urine" sample identically, read its concentration off the curve. Control reagent volume, temperature, time.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Factors affecting blood glucose

❓ State two factors that raise blood glucose concentration and two that lower it.
Raises — digestion and absorption of carbohydrate from the diet; glycogenolysis (glycogen converted to glucose, mainly liver); gluconeogenesis (new glucose made from non-carbohydrate sources in the liver). Lowers — respiration by cells, especially in exercising muscle; glycogenesis (glucose converted to glycogen in liver and muscle); cellular uptake of glucose for storage or conversion to fat.
❓ Explain two problems that would result if blood glucose concentration remained very high for a prolonged period.
The water potential of the blood is lowered, so water moves out of cells by osmosis, which can dehydrate and damage them; if the filtrate glucose concentration exceeds the reabsorption capacity of the co-transporter proteins in the proximal convoluted tubule, not all the filtered glucose can be reabsorbed and it appears in the urine as glycosuria.
❓ A patient's blood glucose concentration falls sharply. Suggest why brain function is affected first.
Brain cells rely almost entirely on glucose as a respiratory substrate; too little glucose means too little ATP can be produced by respiration in these cells, impairing their function before other, more metabolically flexible tissues are affected.

The liver's three glucose pathways

❓ A student writes that gluconeogenesis is "the conversion of glycogen into glucose." Explain why this would not be awarded credit, and give the correct definition.
That definition describes glycogenolysis, not gluconeogenesis; converting glycogen — a carbohydrate — is glycogenolysis regardless of context. Gluconeogenesis is the production of new glucose from non-carbohydrate sources — principally glycerol and amino acids — occurring in the liver.
❓ Name the process, and state where in the body it occurs, for each of the following: (a) glucose converted to glycogen (b) glycogen converted to glucose (c) glucose produced from amino acids.
(a) Glycogenesis, in the liver and muscle. (b) Glycogenolysis, in the liver. (c) Gluconeogenesis, in the liver only.
❓ Explain why "occurs in the pancreas" would not be accepted for any of glycogenesis, glycogenolysis or gluconeogenesis.
All three processes are carried out in the liver (glycogenesis also in muscle); the pancreas detects blood glucose changes and secretes the hormones insulin and glucagon, but does not itself carry out these conversions.

Insulin

❓ Describe the action of insulin in lowering blood glucose concentration.
A rise in blood glucose concentration is detected by beta cells in the islets of Langerhans, which secrete insulin into the blood. Insulin binds to specific receptors on the surface membrane of target cells, mainly in the liver and muscle. This causes vesicles containing channel proteins to move to and fuse with the cell-surface membrane, increasing the number of channel proteins present and so increasing the rate of facilitated diffusion of glucose into the cell. Insulin also activates enzymes, including glycogen synthase, that convert glucose to glycogen — glycogenesis. Blood glucose concentration falls back towards normal, by negative feedback.
❓ A mutation results in a non-functional insulin receptor on liver cells. Predict the consequence for glucose uptake in these cells.
Insulin can no longer bind to its receptor, so fewer or no vesicles containing channel proteins fuse with the cell-surface membrane; fewer or no additional channel proteins are present in the membrane; less or no glucose enters the cell by facilitated diffusion, and glycogenesis is not activated in that cell.
❓ Explain why glucose entering a liver cell under insulin's influence is described as facilitated diffusion rather than active transport.
Glucose moves down its concentration gradient — from the blood, where it is higher, into the cell, where it is lower — through channel proteins, without requiring ATP; active transport would require movement against a concentration gradient using energy from ATP, which does not apply here.

Glucagon

❓ Describe the action of glucagon on liver cells.
A fall in blood glucose concentration is detected by alpha cells in the islets of Langerhans, which secrete glucagon into the blood. Glucagon binds to receptors on the surface of liver cells only (not muscle cells). Via the second messenger cascade, this activates enzymes that convert glycogen to glucose (glycogenolysis) and enzymes that convert glycerol and amino acids to glucose (gluconeogenesis). Glucose diffuses out of the liver cells into the blood, so blood glucose concentration rises back towards normal.
❓ Explain why glucagon does not affect muscle cells in the same way that it affects liver cells.
Muscle cells do not have receptors for glucagon on their surface membranes, so glucagon cannot bind to them and cannot trigger the second messenger cascade, or any downstream enzyme activation, in muscle.
❓ A researcher blocks gluconeogenesis specifically, without affecting glycogenolysis. Predict the effect on a person's ability to maintain blood glucose concentration during a prolonged fast, once liver glycogen stores are exhausted.
With glycogen stores exhausted, glycogenolysis can no longer supply further glucose; without functioning gluconeogenesis, the liver cannot produce new glucose from glycerol or amino acids either, so blood glucose concentration would continue to fall rather than being maintained.

Adrenaline

❓ State two ways in which the action of adrenaline differs from the action of glucagon.
Adrenaline binds to receptors on both liver and muscle cells, whereas glucagon binds only to receptors on liver cells; adrenaline only activates glycogenolysis, whereas glucagon activates both glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis.
❓ Explain why adrenaline, not glucagon, is secreted in a "fight or flight" response, and where it is released from.
Adrenaline is released from the adrenal glands (the medulla) in response to stress, fear or exercise, as part of the sympathetic response; because it acts on muscle cells as well as liver cells, it makes glucose available specifically at the sites where it will be used rapidly for respiration during a fight-or-flight response.
❓ A student writes: "adrenaline raises blood glucose by causing gluconeogenesis in the liver." Correct this statement.
Adrenaline raises blood glucose only by activating glycogenolysis (glycogen converted to glucose) in the liver and muscle; it never triggers gluconeogenesis — that remains a glucagon-only process.

The second messenger model

❓ Describe the second messenger model of glucagon action on a liver cell, from receptor binding to glycogenolysis.
Glucagon, the first messenger, binds to a specific receptor on the outside of the liver cell's surface membrane, without entering the cell. This activates the enzyme adenylate cyclase on the inside face of the membrane. Activated adenylate cyclase converts ATP into cyclic AMP (cAMP), the second messenger, made inside the cell. cAMP activates protein kinase, which in turn activates a cascade of further enzymes, including those responsible for glycogenolysis, converting glycogen to glucose.
❓ A drug specifically inhibits adenylate cyclase in liver cells. Predict, using "less/no" wording throughout, the effect on glucagon's ability to raise blood glucose.
Less or no ATP is converted to cyclic AMP; less or no protein kinase is activated; less or no glycogenolysis occurs; so less or no glucose is released into the blood, and blood glucose concentration rises less than normal, or not at all.
❓ Explain why glucagon and adrenaline require a second messenger system, whereas a steroid hormone does not.
Glucagon and adrenaline are proteins (or protein-derived), so they are not lipid-soluble and cannot cross the phospholipid cell-surface membrane directly; a second messenger, cAMP, generated inside the cell after the first messenger binds a surface receptor, is needed to relay the signal across the membrane. A steroid hormone is lipid-soluble and diffuses directly through the membrane to bind an intracellular receptor, so no second messenger is required.

Type I & type II diabetes

❓ Contrast the causes of type I and type II diabetes.
Type I diabetes is caused by autoimmune destruction of the beta cells in the islets of Langerhans, so little or no insulin is produced. Type II diabetes is caused by target cells or their receptors becoming less responsive — or fewer/faulty receptors being present — to insulin, even though insulin is still being produced.
❓ Explain why insulin cannot be taken as an oral tablet.
Insulin is a protein, so if swallowed it would be hydrolysed by peptidases (protein-digesting enzymes) in the gut during digestion, breaking it down into amino acids before it could have any effect; it must therefore be injected directly into the blood or tissue instead.
❓ A student claims that type II diabetes always means "the pancreas has stopped working." Explain why this is not an accurate description.
In type II diabetes, the pancreas is usually still producing insulin as normal; the problem lies in the target cells or their receptors becoming less responsive to that insulin, rather than in insulin production itself — describing the pancreas as "not working," or the body as "immune" to insulin, does not capture this precise mechanism.

RP11: the glucose calibration curve

❓ Outline how a calibration curve could be used to determine the concentration of glucose in an unknown "urine" sample.
Prepare a dilution series of glucose solutions of known concentration from a stock solution; carry out a quantitative Benedict's test on each, heating with excess Benedict's reagent for a fixed time so it is reduced to an insoluble red-brown precipitate; use a colorimeter to measure the absorbance (or percentage transmission) of each tube after removing the precipitate, and plot a calibration curve of colorimeter reading against known glucose concentration. Treat the unknown "urine" sample identically and read its glucose concentration from the calibration curve.
❓ State two variables that must be controlled in this practical to produce a valid calibration curve.
Any two of: the volume of Benedict's reagent added to each tube; the temperature at which the tubes are heated; the length of time each tube is heated for — all must be kept constant across the dilution series and the unknown sample.
❓ A student's calibration curve shows two of the known-concentration points do not fall on the line of best fit. Suggest a reason, and how the practical could be improved.
The precipitate may not have been fully removed (by filtering or centrifuging) before the colorimeter reading was taken, giving an inaccurate absorbance value for those tubes; repeating each concentration and taking a mean, and consistently removing the precipitate before reading, would improve reliability.
