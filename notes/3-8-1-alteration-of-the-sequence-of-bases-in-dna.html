Alteration of the Sequence of Bases in DNA

How a single typo in a DNA sequence — one swapped, added, deleted, reversed, duplicated or relocated base — can ripple all the way through to a genuinely different protein, and why the exam almost always wants you to finish that chain rather than stop halfway.

⚠️

The two habits that cost the most marks on this spec point: mixing up a silent, degenerate-code substitution with an insertion/deletion frame shift — they are completely different in scale — and stopping an explanation at "the primary structure changes" instead of finishing the chain through to the tertiary structure and the shape of the protein's functional region.

Section 1 · spec content point

🧬 The six named types of gene mutation

Six precise terms, and the exam's favourite way to test them is data-driven: you're given a "before" and "after" sequence and have to work out which of the six actually happened, using base-count evidence rather than a guess.

📖

What the specification says: "Gene mutations might arise during DNA replication. They include addition, deletion, substitution, inversion, duplication and translocation of bases."

1
2
3

Think of a DNA sequence like a long sentence made only of three-letter words. A mutation is just a typo made while copying that sentence out. There are six different kinds of typo the exam wants you to be able to name: swap one letter for another (substitution), squeeze in an extra letter (addition), drop a letter (deletion), flip a chunk of letters backwards (inversion), copy a chunk and paste it again right after itself (duplication), or cut a chunk out and glue it somewhere completely different (translocation).

  • Base = one "letter" of the DNA code (A, T, C or G)
  • Triplet / codon = a "three-letter word" that codes for one amino acid
  • Reading frame = the way the sentence gets chopped up into three-letter words, starting from a fixed point

A gene mutation is a random change to the base sequence of DNA, most often arising as a copying error during DNA replication. AQA wants six specific terms, not the vague phrase "point mutation": substitution (one base swapped for another), addition and deletion (a base gained or lost), inversion (a section of bases reversed in place), duplication (a section copied and repeated), and translocation (a section moved to a different part of the genome, often a different chromosome). A substitution only ever disturbs the one triplet it sits inside. An addition or deletion usually re-groups every triplet after it — unless the number of bases changed is a multiple of three, in which case whole codons are simply gained or lost intact.

TypeWhat happensEffect on the reading frame
SubstitutionOne base is replaced by a different baseOnly the one triplet containing it is affected — no frame shift (may or may not change the amino acid, since the code is degenerate)
Addition (insertion)One or more extra bases are inserted into the sequenceIf the number added is not a multiple of three, every triplet downstream is re-grouped — a frame shift
DeletionOne or more bases are removed from the sequenceIf the number removed is not a multiple of three, every triplet downstream is re-grouped — a frame shift
InversionA section of bases is reversed in orientation, in the same placeNo bases gained or lost, so no frame shift — but every triplet inside the inverted section is read in a different order, usually changing several amino acids in that stretch
DuplicationA section of bases is copied and inserted again immediately after the originalAdds bases, so a frame shift results unless the duplicated section is itself a multiple of three (e.g. one whole codon) — then extra amino acid(s) are simply repeated
TranslocationA section of DNA breaks off and re-joins a different part of the genome, often a different chromosomeDoesn't itself alter the base sequence within the moved section, but can disrupt a gene at the break point, or place a gene next to different regulatory sequences and change its expression
▶ 🚩 Trap — "point mutation" is not one of the six terms

Naming a change as a "point mutation" earns nothing on its own — always use one of the six named terms above. A closely related slip: if a question asks about a change in the base sequence, describing it as a change "of amino acid" answers a different question than the one asked — read carefully which level (base or amino acid) the question is actually about.

Working out which type occurred from a given sequence

The dominant way this gets examined is data-driven: you're handed a "before" and "after" version of a base or codon sequence, and asked to identify — and justify — which of the six types occurred. The justification has to come from actually counting bases and quoting the codons involved, not just naming a type in the abstract.

  • Line the two sequences up. If they're the same length and only one base differs, that's a substitution.
  • If a stretch of bases reads backwards compared with the original, that's an inversion.
  • If the total base count has changed, and everything after a certain point looks shifted along, that points to an addition or deletion — check whether more than one amino acid has changed as supporting evidence, since a frame shift usually affects several codons, not just one.
  • The recurring discriminator examiners come back to: has the total number of bases changed, and if so, is that change a multiple of three?
📌

Worth remembering: the full six-term list is a genuinely new demand at this stage — earlier content only ever required you to know substitution and deletion by name. All six can alter the genetic information carried, but only some of them shift the reading frame.

Section 2 · spec content point

🎲 When and why gene mutations arise

Mutation is random and spontaneous — mutagenic agents can only ever turn the dial up on how often it happens, never choose which mutation appears. Where a mutation lands, in a body cell or a sex cell, decides who ever experiences its consequences.

📖

What the specification says: "Gene mutations occur spontaneously. The mutation rate is increased by mutagenic agents. Mutations can result in a different amino acid sequence in the encoded polypeptide."

1
2
3

Copying out a very long book by hand every so often produces a random typo — nobody planned it, it just happens sometimes. That's a spontaneous mutation. Some things make typos happen more often, like trying to copy the book with a shaky hand — that's a mutagen, and it just makes mistakes more likely, it doesn't choose which word gets typo'd. If the typo happens in the "master copy" that gets passed to a new book (a sex cell), the next book inherits it. If it happens in an ordinary page of an existing book (a body cell), only that one book is affected — the next book printed is unaffected.

  • Mutagen = anything that makes mutations happen more often
  • Gamete = a sex cell — egg or sperm
  • Somatic cell = an ordinary body cell, not a sex cell

Gene mutations happen spontaneously and at random, most often as copying errors during DNA replication. A mutagenic agent increases how often mutations occur — it does not decide which mutation appears, and it never "directs" a useful change to order; that idea (the environment causing a beneficial mutation on purpose) is a common but incorrect way of thinking about evolution. Examples of mutagens include ionising radiation (X-rays, UV, gamma rays) and certain chemicals (like those in tobacco smoke). Because a mutation can change the base sequence, it can — but doesn't automatically — result in a different amino acid sequence in the protein that gene encodes. Where the mutation happens also matters: a mutation in a gamete can be passed on to offspring, while a mutation in a body (somatic) cell isn't inherited, though it can still affect that individual, for example by contributing to cancer.

Spontaneous, random, and increased by mutagenic agents

Gene mutations arise spontaneously — most often as random errors occurring during DNA replication. A mutagenic agent is any factor that increases the rate at which mutations occur.

Category of mutagenExamples
Ionising radiationX-rays, gamma rays, ultraviolet (UV) light
Chemical mutagensChemicals present in tobacco smoke, and certain other chemicals such as pesticides
▶ 🚩 Trap — mutagens increase rate, they don't choose outcome

A mutagen increases the rate at which mutations occur at random — it never "directs," "causes," or selects a specific, useful mutation to appear. Never imply that an organism's environment chooses which mutation shows up in response to a need; mutation is random and comes first, and natural selection only acts afterwards on whatever variation already exists.

Somatic versus germ-line: where the mutation lands decides who inherits it

Where the mutation occursConsequence
In a gamete (a germ-line cell)Can be passed on to offspring — every cell in the resulting organism carries it
In a somatic (body) cellNot inherited by offspring, but can still affect the individual in which it occurred — for example, a somatic mutation that activates a proto-oncogene or inactivates a tumour-suppressor gene can contribute to cancer

This distinction — which cell type a mutation lands in — is the idea that carries this spec point forward into later content on gene expression and cancer.

🔗

The link to protein structure: because a mutation changes the base sequence, it has the potential to change the amino acid sequence of the encoded polypeptide — but, as the next two sections cover, whether it actually does depends on exactly what kind of change occurred and where.

Section 3 · spec content point

🧩 Degeneracy: why a one-triplet change doesn't always matter

Because more than one triplet can spell the same amino acid, a substitution can sometimes change the DNA and yet leave the protein completely untouched — but the exam wants that shown with real codon evidence, not just the word "degenerate."

📖

What the specification says: "Some gene mutations change only one triplet code. Due to the degenerate nature of the genetic code, not all such mutations result in a change to the encoded amino acid."

1
2
3

Some words have more than one correct spelling that still mean exactly the same thing — like "colour" and "color". If a typo swaps one spelling for the other, the sentence still means the same thing — nothing about the story actually changes. That's what "degenerate" means for the genetic code: more than one three-letter code can spell the exact same amino acid, so swapping one for another sometimes changes absolutely nothing about the protein that gets built.

  • Degenerate code = more than one triplet can code for the same amino acid
  • Silent mutation = a base change that doesn't change the amino acid produced

There are 64 possible three-base triplets but only 20 amino acids, so most amino acids are coded for by more than one triplet — the genetic code is degenerate. A substitution only ever changes the one triplet it lies within. Because of degeneracy, that changed triplet might still code for exactly the same amino acid as before, in which case the protein's primary structure — and therefore its shape and function — is completely unaffected. This is only ever creditable with actual evidence: you have to quote the old codon and the new codon, using a supplied genetic code table, and show they both specify the same amino acid, rather than just writing the word "degenerate" and stopping there.

Why the code is degenerate

With four possible bases and a triplet code, there are 4³ = 64 possible codons, but only 20 amino acids are actually coded for (plus start/stop signals). Because there are so many more possible triplets than amino acids, most amino acids are specified by more than one codon — this is what "degenerate" means. Degeneracy is concentrated mainly in the third base of a codon, which is why a substitution at the third position is the one most likely to leave the amino acid, and therefore the protein, unchanged.

▶ 🎯 Application skill — proving degeneracy with real codon-table evidence

Simply asserting "the code is degenerate, so nothing changes" earns very little on its own. Using a supplied genetic code table, you're expected to quote both the old codon and the new codon and show that they specify the same amino acid. For example, a substitution changing the third base of the codon CAU (histidine) to CAC is silent, because CAU and CAC both code for histidine — this has to be demonstrated with the actual codons side by side, not just asserted in words.

How to tackle it: always write out both codons and both resulting amino acids, using the table you've been given, before concluding that the mutation is silent.

OutcomeWhat the new codon doesConsequence for the protein
SilentCodes for the same amino acid (degenerate code)Primary structure unchanged → protein structure and function unchanged
MissenseCodes for a different amino acid at that one positionDifferent R-group at that position → may or may not disrupt the protein's tertiary structure, depending on the substitute R-group and its position
NonsenseBecomes a stop codonTranslation ends early → a truncated, usually non-functional polypeptide

Silent, missense and nonsense are the three possible outcomes of any single-triplet substitution — this spec content point is specifically about the silent case, where degeneracy means the amino acid sequence, and therefore the protein, isn't affected at all.

Section 4 · spec content point

🧵 Frame shift: when every downstream triplet changes

Unlike a substitution, an addition or deletion of bases doesn't just disturb one triplet — it re-groups every triplet after it into a completely different, usually meaningless, sequence.

📖

What the specification says: "Some gene mutations change the nature of all base triplets downstream from the mutation, ie result in a frame shift."

1
2
3

Imagine copying out that same three-letter-word sentence again, but this time you accidentally add or drop one single letter partway through. From that point on, every group of three letters you read off is shifted along by one — the whole rest of the sentence turns into gibberish, even though you didn't touch most of the original letters at all. That's a frame shift: one small slip near the start wrecks everything that comes after it.

Adding or removing a base shifts the "reading frame" — the way the sequence gets chopped into three-letter triplets — for every base that comes after the mutation. From that point on, completely different triplets are read, producing a completely different (and usually meaningless) string of amino acids, often hitting a premature stop codon and cutting the protein short. There's an important exception: if the number of bases added or removed is exactly a multiple of three, no frame shift happens at all — a whole extra amino acid (or one fewer) is simply gained or lost, and everything after it reads exactly as before. Always count the bases before deciding whether a frame shift has occurred.

A substitution can only ever disturb the one triplet it sits in. An addition or deletion behaves completely differently: because every triplet is read in a fixed sequence of threes from a set starting point, adding or removing bases shifts every "reading window" after the mutation along by however many bases were added or lost — re-grouping the whole of the rest of the sequence into new, essentially meaningless triplets.

TACGGACTTAAACCG

Substitution — one base changed

TACGCACTTAAACCG
TyrAlaLeuLysPro

15 bases → 15 bases, no length change. Only codon 2 changes (Gly → Ala); codons 3, 4 and 5 read exactly as before — no frame shift.

Frame shift — insertion of 1 base

TACTGGACTTAAACC
TyrTrpThrSTOP—

15 bases → 16 bases, +1 base — not a multiple of three. Every codon from position 2 onward is misread; translation even halts early at a new stop codon, truncating the protein.

Both branches start from the same 15-base sequence. A single substitution in codon 2 changes only that one amino acid. Inserting one extra base (not a multiple of three) after codon 1 shifts the reading frame for every base that follows.
▶ ⚠️ The "multiple of three" trap

AQA sets scenarios where an addition or deletion of exactly three bases (or any multiple of three) does not cause a frame shift — a whole codon, and so a whole amino acid, is simply gained or lost, and every triplet after it reads exactly as before. Always check the actual number of bases changed before concluding a frame shift has occurred, rather than assuming any addition or deletion automatically causes one.

📌

A frame shift can also be explained in terms of its effect on start and stop codons specifically — describing how the shifted reading frame can destroy the original stop codon, or create a new one earlier than intended, is an equally valid way of explaining why the resulting protein is usually non-functional.

🚩

Never conflate the two: a silent substitution (a degenerate-code effect, no change to the protein at all) is a completely different scale of event from an insertion/deletion frame shift (a much larger, structural effect reaching almost every amino acid downstream). Examiner reports flag this confusion as one of the most common ways marks are lost on this spec point.

Section 5 · exam technique

🎯 Common mistakes & precise wording

This spec point isn't conceptually difficult, but it's examined for precision at every single link — the biology is short to learn; the exact wording is what actually loses marks.

Vague or incorrect versionWhat to say instead
"A point mutation occurred"Name the specific type — substitution, addition, deletion, inversion, duplication or translocation
"The amino acid was substituted"It's the base that is substituted — the sentence should describe a change of base, not of amino acid, unless the question specifically asks about the amino acid level
"Different amino acids formed"The sequence of amino acids changes — amino acids themselves are not newly made
Explanation stops at "the primary structure changes"Continue the chain: this alters the pattern of hydrogen, ionic and disulfide bonds → which changes the tertiary structure → which can change the shape of the protein's functional region
"Active site" used for a receptorA receptor has a binding site, not an active site — reserve "active site" for enzymes only
"Active site"/"binding site" used for a structural proteinFor a structural protein (e.g. collagen, haemoglobin), describe altered shape, solubility or strength instead
"A beneficial mutation helps the organism survive" (left there)Finish the link explicitly: increased survival/reproductive success → the allele's frequency increases in the population

The full causal chain, worth learning as one continuous idea

Whenever a mutation does change the amino acid sequence, examiners are looking for a single unbroken chain of reasoning, not isolated facts: a change in the base sequence → a different sequence of amino acids in the primary structure → this alters the pattern of hydrogen, ionic and disulfide bonds between R-groups → so the tertiary structure changes → and the protein may no longer function correctly, because its functional region is no longer the right shape.

▶ Worked example — a receptor protein (binding-site language)

A well-known real mutation produces an altered version of a receptor found on certain white blood cells, which blocks a particular virus from being able to enter the cell. Because this protein is a receptor, not an enzyme, the correct language for its functional region is a binding site — never "active site." The reasoning chain is exactly the same as any other missense mutation: altered R-group → disrupted bonding → changed tertiary structure → the receptor's binding site is no longer complementary to whatever it would normally bind.

▶ Worked example — a structural protein (shape/solubility language)

A single DNA base substitution changing one codon of a gene coding for a structural, oxygen-carrying protein can swap an acidic, water-loving amino acid for a small, water-repelling one at a single position on the protein's surface. That one substituted R-group creates a sticky, water-repelling patch, causing the affected protein molecules to clump together into rigid fibres under certain conditions — distorting a normally flexible cell into a rigid, abnormal shape. Because this protein is structural, not an enzyme or a receptor, the correct language is altered shape, solubility or strength — never "active site."

▶ 🚩 Trap — vocabulary must switch with the protein named in the question

Use active site only for an enzyme, binding site for a receptor or an antibody, and altered shape / solubility / strength (never "active site") for a structural protein such as collagen or haemoglobin. This same substitution error recurs whenever a question stem names a receptor, a transport protein or an antibody instead of an enzyme — always check what kind of protein is named before choosing the vocabulary.

📊

Where the difficulty actually sits: the six mutation types are short to memorise, and the four-step causal chain is short to write out — but this spec point is examined for precision at every link. Substitution outcomes tend to be well understood; frame-shift consequences and the silent/frame-shift distinction are less securely known, and imprecise, circular language around "positive/negative/neutral effects" that never reaches allele frequency or reproductive success is a frequent, avoidable way to lose marks.

Section 6 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

This content works as a strong supporting paragraph in essay-style questions about DNA, mutation, or evolution — the trick is finishing the causal chain all the way to a named consequence, rather than stopping at "the structure changes."

▶ Example paragraph — angled around "the importance of mutation"

A gene mutation is a random, spontaneous change in the base sequence of DNA, most often arising as an error during replication, at a rate that is increased by exposure to mutagenic agents such as ionising radiation or certain chemicals. It may take the form of a substitution, addition, deletion, inversion, duplication or translocation of bases. Because the genetic code is degenerate, a substitution sometimes codes for the same amino acid and has no effect at all; where it does matter, the chain runs: a change in the base sequence → a change in the amino acid sequence (primary structure) → altered hydrogen, ionic and disulfide bonding → a changed tertiary structure → and so the protein may no longer be complementary to its substrate or ligand. Mutation is, ultimately, the only source of entirely new alleles, which is why it sits at the root of genetic diversity and, eventually, natural selection.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around "DNA as an information-carrying molecule"

The base sequence of DNA carries the coded information needed to build a specific sequence of amino acids. Because that code is read in triplets, an addition or deletion that is not a multiple of three corrupts every triplet downstream of it — a frame shift — showing how sensitive the information stored in DNA is to even a single missing or extra base, in a way a substitution, which only ever affects one triplet, is not. This illustrates precisely why DNA's information is described as being read in a fixed reading frame, rather than as a loose collection of independent bases.

Whichever essay title this content supports, treat it as the "mechanism" paragraph: state the process precisely, using the correct vocabulary for the protein named (active site / binding site / shape-solubility-strength), then add one sentence that links the mechanism explicitly back to the essay's actual theme — stopping at "the amino acid sequence changes" without finishing the chain is the single most avoidable shortfall on this spec point.

  • Cell recognition and the immune system — mutation as a source of antigenic variability.
  • Natural selection and speciation — mutation as the ultimate source of new alleles, including receptor-mutation contexts like the one in Section 5.
  • Gene expression and cancer — somatic mutations inactivating tumour-suppressor genes or activating proto-oncogenes.
  • DNA differences, identification and diagnosis — screening for known disease-causing base changes.
🧠

Memory hook. Silent = same amino acid, nothing changes. Missense = different amino acid, one position, maybe a bond problem. Nonsense = STOP, truncated, usually a dead protein. For any missense mutation: different R-group → different bonds (hydrogen / ionic / disulfide / hydrophobic) → different tertiary structure → for an enzyme, a non-complementary active site; for a receptor, a non-complementary binding site; for a structural protein, altered shape, solubility or strength. And: substitution disturbs one triplet only; addition/deletion disturbs every triplet downstream — unless the number of bases changed is a multiple of three, in which case there's no frame shift at all.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · The six types of mutation

  • Substitution, addition, deletion, inversion, duplication, translocation — never "point mutation".
  • Only addition and deletion cause a frame shift, and only if the number of bases changed is not a multiple of three.
  • Inversion reverses a section in place (no frame shift, but several amino acids may change); duplication repeats a section; translocation moves a section to a different location.
  • To identify the type from a data stem: count the bases, check for length change, check for multiples of three.

2 · When & why mutations arise

  • Mutations are spontaneous and random — mutagens only increase the rate, never choose the outcome.
  • Mutagens: ionising radiation (X-rays, UV, gamma) and chemicals (e.g. in tobacco smoke).
  • Gamete/germ-line mutation → can be inherited. Somatic mutation → not inherited, but can still affect that individual (e.g. cancer).

3 · Degeneracy & silent changes

  • 64 possible triplets, only 20 amino acids → most amino acids have more than one codon → the code is degenerate.
  • A substitution changes only one triplet — degeneracy means it might still code for the same amino acid (silent).
  • Always prove it with the actual old/new codons and the amino acid table — the word "degenerate" alone scores little.

4 · Frame shift

  • Addition/deletion not a multiple of three → every triplet downstream is re-read → usually meaningless, often an early stop codon.
  • Addition/deletion of a multiple of three → no frame shift, just extra/missing whole amino acid(s).
  • Never confuse this (large, structural effect) with a silent substitution (no effect at all).
🧠

Silent = same, missense = one swap, nonsense = STOP. Substitution = one triplet only. Addition/deletion = every downstream triplet, unless it's a multiple of three.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in precise, full-credit wording, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

The six types of mutation

❓ A gene contains the sequence ...ATG CGT TAC... . Following a mutation it reads ...ATG CCT TAC... . Identify the type of mutation and justify your answer.
Substitution; the total number of bases is unchanged (9 bases before and after) and only one base has changed (G to C in the second triplet), so the reading frame downstream is unaffected.
❓ Explain why "point mutation" is not an acceptable answer when a question asks you to name the type of mutation shown in a given sequence.
"Point mutation" is not one of the six specification terms; the answer must name the specific type — substitution, addition, deletion, inversion, duplication or translocation — based on evidence from the given sequence.
❓ A section of a gene, GAT CCA TTG, is found reversed within the same gene, becoming GTT ACC TAG. Name this type of mutation and state its effect on the reading frame.
Inversion; no bases are gained or lost, so there is no frame shift, but every triplet within the inverted section is read in a different order, changing the amino acids coded for within that section.

When & why mutations arise

❓ Explain why exposure to UV light increases the number of mutations in skin cells, but does not increase the chance that any resulting mutation will be beneficial.
UV light is a mutagenic agent, increasing the rate at which mutations occur, most commonly as errors during DNA replication; mutation itself is random, so exposure increases how often mutations happen, not which specific mutation occurs or whether that mutation is beneficial, harmful or neutral.
❓ A mutation occurs in a skin cell of an adult and is not present in that person's gametes. Explain why this mutation cannot be passed to any offspring, but could still affect the individual.
The mutation is in a somatic (body) cell, not a germ-line cell, so it is not present in the gametes and cannot be inherited by offspring; however, it can still affect the individual in which it occurred — for example, if it inactivates a tumour-suppressor gene or activates a proto-oncogene, contributing to cancer.
❓ "A mutagenic agent causes an organism to develop a useful mutation." Identify the error in this statement and correct it.
This incorrectly implies mutation is directed towards a useful outcome; a mutagenic agent only increases the rate at which mutations occur at random — it does not cause, direct, or select for any specific, useful mutation.

Degeneracy & silent changes

❓ Using the genetic code table, a substitution changes the codon GGU to GGC. Explain why this substitution has no effect on the polypeptide produced.
GGU and GGC both code for the same amino acid (glycine); because the genetic code is degenerate, this substitution does not change the amino acid sequence of the polypeptide, so the primary, and therefore tertiary, structure of the protein is unaffected.
❓ Explain, in terms of the number of triplets and the number of amino acids, why the genetic code is described as degenerate.
There are 64 possible triplet codons but only 20 amino acids are coded for, so most amino acids are specified by more than one codon; this is what is meant by the genetic code being degenerate.
❓ "The mutation had no effect because the genetic code is degenerate." Explain why this answer, on its own, would not gain full credit.
The statement gives no evidence; full credit requires quoting the specific old and new codons from a genetic code table and showing that both specify the same amino acid, not simply asserting that the code is degenerate.

Frame shift

❓ A single base is deleted from a gene, and the number of bases lost is not a multiple of three. Explain the effect this has on the protein produced.
The deletion causes a frame shift, so every triplet downstream of the deletion is read in a new grouping, coding for a different sequence of amino acids from that point onward; this may also create a premature stop codon, producing a truncated, usually non-functional polypeptide.
❓ A mutation involves the insertion of three extra bases into a gene. Explain why this insertion does not cause a frame shift.
Three bases is a multiple of three, so one whole extra codon (and therefore one extra amino acid) is inserted into the sequence; every triplet after the insertion continues to be read in the original grouping, so the reading frame downstream is unaffected.
❓ Distinguish between the effect of a substitution and the effect of a deletion (not a multiple of three) on the reading frame of a gene.
A substitution changes only the one triplet in which it occurs, since no bases are gained or lost; a deletion that is not a multiple of three removes bases and shifts the grouping of every triplet from that point onward, changing the amino acid sequence coded for over the entire remaining length of the gene.
