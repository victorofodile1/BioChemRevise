ATP

A small, three-fact topic — what ATP is built from, how it's broken down to release energy, and how it gets built back up again — whose real difficulty is a single wording trap that resurfaces in almost every energy-related topic on the course.

⚠️

The one habit that protects the most marks on this topic: ATP hydrolysis releases energy — it never "produces," "makes" or "creates" it. That single substitution is the most commonly rejected wording anywhere on the specification, and it resurfaces in transport, muscle, synapse and respiration questions alike. The same trap reappears one level up too: mitochondria synthesise ATP via respiration — they do not simply "produce" or "store" energy as their function.

Section 1 · spec content point

🧬 What ATP actually is — structure & "nucleotide derivative"

Everything else on this spec point assumes you already know exactly what ATP is built from. It's a short, tightly worded fact — three named components, no more, no less — but the naming has to be precise.

📖

What the specification says: "A single molecule of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) is a nucleotide derivative and is formed from a molecule of ribose, a molecule of adenine and three phosphate groups."

1
2
3

Think of ATP as a little toy built from snap-together pieces. There's one base piece, one sugar piece stuck to it, and then three identical "energy beads" clipped on in a row after the sugar. That's it — that's the whole toy.

  • Base piece = adenine
  • Sugar piece = ribose
  • Energy beads = the three phosphate groups, clipped on in a chain
  • Base + sugar together = adenosine (the "adenosine" in "adenosine triphosphate" is only these two pieces, never a phosphate bead)

A normal nucleotide (like the ones DNA is built from) is one sugar, one base and one phosphate group. ATP has the same sugar-base-phosphate pattern, but with three phosphates chained on instead of one — which is exactly why it's classed as a nucleotide derivative rather than a plain nucleotide. Its base is adenine (a nitrogen-containing organic molecule), its sugar is ribose (a five-carbon, or "pentose," sugar), and together the base and sugar make up a two-part unit called adenosine. Adenosine plus three phosphate groups gives you the full molecule, adenosine triphosphate.

PartWhat it is
AdenineThe nitrogen-containing base
RiboseThe pentose (five-carbon) sugar
AdenosineAdenine + ribose joined together
Three phosphate groupsChained onto the ribose

ATP's three named components, precisely: adenine — a nitrogen-containing organic base; ribose — a pentose sugar ("pentose" alone is an acceptable way to describe it); and three phosphate groups, joined in a chain to the ribose. Adenine and ribose together form the two-part unit called adenosine — so "adenosine" on its own never counts as one of the three separate components being asked for, since it's really naming two of them combined.

Adenine (base) + ribose (sugar) = adenosine; adenosine + three phosphate groups = ATP. The dashed red bond between the 2nd and 3rd phosphate is the one hydrolysed to release energy (Section 2).

Contrasting ATP with a DNA nucleotide

Point of comparisonATPA DNA nucleotide
SugarRiboseDeoxyribose
Number of phosphate groupsThreeOne
BaseAlways adenineVaries — cytosine, thymine or guanine

A full-credit contrast always names both sides of each point — stating only ATP's side, or only the DNA nucleotide's side, is treated as an incomplete answer either way.

▶ 🚩 Trap — don't reach for uracil

Uracil belongs to RNA, not DNA — never give it as an example of a base that could vary in a DNA nucleotide. If you're asked for DNA bases, the only three that can replace adenine are cytosine, thymine and guanine.

📌

Marking habit worth building now: if a question asks you to name ATP's components, "adenosine" on its own doesn't count as one of the three — it's the name for the base-plus-sugar unit, not a standalone component. Name adenine and ribose separately.

Section 2 · spec content point

⚡ Hydrolysis of ATP — the enzyme, the products & the #1 wording trap

This is the single most heavily policed piece of wording anywhere on the specification. The chemistry is short and simple — the skill is never letting the wrong verb slip in under pressure.

📖

What the specification says: "Hydrolysis of ATP to adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and an inorganic phosphate group (Pi) is catalysed by the enzyme ATP hydrolase. The hydrolysis of ATP can be coupled to energy-requiring reactions within cells. The inorganic phosphate released during the hydrolysis of ATP can be used to phosphorylate other compounds, often making them more reactive."

1
2
3

Picture ATP as a fully charged battery. "Hydrolysis" is using that battery: a water molecule helps a special opener enzyme snap off the last energy bead (phosphate). That snapping releases energy — it doesn't make energy, the energy was already stored in the battery, waiting to be let out. The energy bead that popped off can then be stuck onto a different molecule, giving that molecule a little "power boost" that makes it more likely to react.

  • The opener enzyme = ATP hydrolase
  • What's left over = ADP (the used-up battery) + the loose energy bead (Pi)
  • Giving the bead to another molecule = phosphorylation

A water molecule is used to break the bond holding on ATP's last phosphate group, in a reaction catalysed by the enzyme ATP hydrolase: ATP + water → ADP + Pi. Breaking that bond releases energy — the credited word is always "released," never "produced," "made" or "created." That released energy can be coupled to a reaction elsewhere in the cell that needs energy to happen, so the energy is used directly rather than being wasted as heat. Often the phosphate group that's set free doesn't just float away — it gets attached to another molecule instead, a process called phosphorylation, which usually makes that molecule more reactive.

The reaction: ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pi, catalysed by the enzyme ATP hydrolase. "ADP" stands for adenosine diphosphate — worth double-checking the spelling, since "adenine diphosphate" is a common and costly slip.

▶ 🚩 The single costliest wording trap on this spec point

Never write that ATP hydrolysis "produces," "makes" or "creates" energy — the only correct verb is that it releases energy. This exact substitution recurs across essentially every energy-related context on the course: active transport, muscle contraction, synaptic transmission and respiration all get marked to the same standard. The energy was already stored in the molecule; hydrolysis simply lets it out.

Coupling — why this matters

The hydrolysis of ATP is coupled to an energy-requiring reaction: the energy released by breaking the bond is used directly to drive that reaction, rather than being lost as heat. In active transport, for instance, this coupled energy is what allows ions or molecules to be moved against their concentration gradient.

Phosphorylation

The phosphate group transferred during hydrolysis makes the receiving substrate more reactive, raises its energy level, or lowers the activation energy needed for the reaction that follows — any one of these three ways of phrasing it captures the same underlying idea. A bare "it provides energy," with nothing more specific attached, isn't precise enough to be worth much, and neither is an unqualified reference to kinetic energy.

▶ Where phosphorylation shows up elsewhere on the course

Glucose is phosphorylated right at the start of glycolysis, using two ATP molecules, which makes it more reactive and primes it for the reactions that follow. Enzymes get activated by phosphorylation in the second-messenger model of hormone action, where phosphorylation changes the enzyme's tertiary structure so its active site becomes complementary to its substrate. And at a neuromuscular junction, the myosin head is reset — detached and re-cocked — by ATP binding to it, ready for the next power stroke.

Why ATP makes such a good immediate energy source

  • It releases a relatively small amount of energy each time — so little is lost as heat, and there's little risk of thermal damage to the cell.
  • It releases that energy instantaneously, in a single reaction — unlike the multi-step breakdown of glucose, which takes far longer to yield usable energy.
  • It phosphorylates other compounds as it breaks down, making them more reactive in the same step that supplies the energy.
  • It can be rapidly resynthesised (Section 3), so supply keeps up with demand.
  • It is not lost from cells — unlike some other potential energy carriers, it stays where it's needed.
💡

Why ATP isn't a storage molecule. ATP is unstable, so it can't be stockpiled the way a molecule like glycogen or starch can — it has to be continually resynthesised instead. An active cell may turn over its entire ATP pool many times a minute.

Section 3 · spec content point

🔧 Resynthesis of ATP — the other enzyme & the three phosphorylation routes

The mirror image of Section 2: a different enzyme, a different reaction type, and three named routes for where it actually happens in the cell.

📖

What the specification says: "ATP is resynthesised by the condensation of ADP and Pi. This reaction is catalysed by the enzyme ATP synthase during photosynthesis, or during respiration."

1
2
3

Now picture recharging that battery. The used-up ADP piece and a loose energy bead (phosphate) get glued back together by a different helper — a "builder" enzyme rather than the "opener" enzyme from Section 2 — and a tiny drop of water is squeezed out as they join. The power to do this gluing comes from either sunlight-capturing (photosynthesis) or food-burning (respiration).

  • The builder enzyme = ATP synthase (never shortened — it's a different enzyme from ATP hydrolase)
  • Where the power comes from = respiration, or photosynthesis in plant cells

Resynthesis is the reverse of hydrolysis: ADP and Pi join together in a condensation reaction, releasing a water molecule as they do, catalysed by the enzyme ATP synthase. It's easy to muddle the two enzyme names, so keep the pair straight — ATP hydrolase breaks ATP down; ATP synthase builds it back up. ATP gets resynthesised in three different ways depending on where in the cell it's happening and what's powering it: during the light-dependent reaction of photosynthesis, during the later stages of respiration, and directly in glycolysis and the Krebs cycle.

The reaction: ADP + Pi → ATP, a condensation reaction that releases a water molecule, catalysed by ATP synthase — the reverse of hydrolysis. "Phosphodiester" is not the right term to use here; that bond belongs to nucleic acids, not to ATP.

▶ 🚩 Trap — never shorten the enzyme to "ATPase"

Name it in full: ATP synthase. And check which direction a question is actually asking about before naming an enzyme — elsewhere on the course (muscle contraction), a question rejects "ATP synthase" in a context where ATP hydrolase is the enzyme actually required. The two names are easy to swap under time pressure, so it's worth a deliberate pause.

The three ways ATP is made

ProcessWhere and how
PhotophosphorylationIn chlorophyll-containing plant cells, during the light-dependent reaction — protons cross the thylakoid membrane through ATP synthase.
Oxidative phosphorylationIn the mitochondria of all cells — protons cross the inner mitochondrial membrane through ATP synthase; this is the source of the bulk of the ATP made during respiration.
Substrate-level phosphorylationIn the cytoplasm (glycolysis) and in the Krebs cycle — a phosphate group is transferred directly from a substrate molecule onto ADP, with no membrane or ATP synthase involved.
📎

Also worth knowing — phosphocreatine. In muscle, ATP can be rapidly regenerated from a store of phosphocreatine: ADP + phosphocreatine → ATP + creatine, where the phosphocreatine donates its phosphate to ADP. Don't write "phosphorus" when the correct term is "phosphate" — the two are not the same thing.

▶ Reasoning through inhibitor data on ATP production

A respiratory inhibitor that acts specifically on a membrane (for example, blocking oxidative phosphorylation at the inner mitochondrial membrane) stops ATP production there, but not in the cytoplasm — because glycolysis, and its substrate-level phosphorylation, doesn't depend on that membrane at all. The general shape of this reasoning: identify which process is being studied (often active transport, since that's the process most directly powered by ATP hydrolysis), note that the inhibitor reduces or stops the process, then explain that ATP production stops specifically where the inhibited enzymes are located, while continuing wherever unaffected enzymes are still working.

Section 4 · bringing it together

🔬

Why cells that do a lot of active transport are packed with mitochondria. Mitochondria are the site of aerobic respiration, so they synthesise more ATP, which is then hydrolysed to release the energy that powers transport. That three-step chain — mitochondria respire → ATP is synthesised → ATP is hydrolysed — is worth having ready as a complete answer, since a partial version of it (stopping after "more mitochondria means more energy") tends to score less well.

▶ How this connects to practical work elsewhere on the course

No required practical sits directly on this spec point, but it underpins several others. Measuring the rate of respiration, and measuring the rate of photosynthesis, are both — mechanistically — measurements of how fast a cell can resynthesise ATP by oxidative phosphorylation or photophosphorylation. Investigations into active transport and membrane permeability test the same idea in reverse: ATP hydrolysis powering a carrier protein against a concentration gradient. Whenever a practical-based question asks why a rate changed, the answer is very often a hydrolysis or resynthesis fact from this page — for example, "adding a respiratory inhibitor slows the rate because oxidative phosphorylation, and therefore ATP resynthesis, has stopped."

Section 5 · exam technique

🎯 Common mistakes & precise wording

This spec point is short and conceptually simple — almost all of its difficulty comes from precision, not from the underlying biology. These are the slips that come up again and again.

Vague or wrong versionWhat to write instead
"ATP hydrolysis produces / makes / creates energy""Hydrolysis of ATP releases energy" — every time, in every context
"Mitochondria produce / store energy"Mitochondria synthesise ATP via respiration — describe the process, not a vague output
Shortening ATP synthase to "ATPase"Name it in full: ATP synthase (and check whether the question actually needs ATP hydrolase instead)
"Adenine diphosphate"Adenosine diphosphate — check the spelling carefully

▶ How the command words on this spec point actually get used

Name wants an exact technical term — "ATP hydrolase," not "ATPase"; "adenine," not "adenosine" — nothing looser will do. Describe ("describe the hydrolysis/resynthesis of ATP") wants the reactants, products and enzyme woven into one flowing statement, not a bare definition. Explain ("explain phosphorylation") wants the mechanism — more reactive, raised energy level, or lowered activation energy — not just a statement that phosphorylation happens. Contrast ("contrast ATP with a DNA nucleotide") needs both halves of every difference stated, not just one side. Interpret (inhibitor-data questions on ATP production) wants a conclusion tied to a mechanism — which phosphorylation route has been affected — not just a description of the trend in the data.

▶ Why this small topic still catches people out

The content itself is short — three tightly worded facts, low memorisation load. What makes it harder than it looks is that misconceptions about "producing energy" are extremely common, and the terminology precision demanded (adenine vs adenosine, hydrolase vs synthase, phosphate vs phosphorus) is unusually strict for how little content there is. Later in the course, the same ideas also have to be applied to genuinely different contexts — inhibitor data, muscle contraction, synaptic transmission — which is where phosphorylation and coupling stop being abstract definitions and start needing to be reasoned through in an unfamiliar scenario.

Section 6 · exam technique

✍️ Using ATP in longer written answers

ATP works brilliantly as the "mechanism" paragraph inside a longer essay-style answer — energy transfer, movement, or cycles in biology can all lean on it — provided every paragraph closes by tying the mechanism explicitly back to the essay's actual theme, rather than just restating the biology in isolation.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around active transport

ATP is a nucleotide derivative made of adenine, ribose and three phosphate groups; hydrolysis of ATP to ADP and inorganic phosphate, catalysed by ATP hydrolase, releases energy that can be coupled directly to energy-requiring reactions such as the active transport of ions against a concentration gradient. Because active transport depends on a continuous supply of ATP, cells specialised for it — such as ileum epithelial cells or kidney tubule cells — contain unusually large numbers of mitochondria, which synthesise the ATP that is then hydrolysed to power the carrier proteins.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around cycles in biology

The ATP/ADP cycle is itself a clear example of a biological cycle: ATP is hydrolysed to ADP and inorganic phosphate, releasing energy that drives a huge range of cellular processes, and is then resynthesised from ADP and phosphate by the enzyme ATP synthase, using energy transferred from respiration or, in plant cells, photosynthesis. Because ATP is unstable and cannot be stored, this cycle has to turn over continuously and rapidly — an active cell may recycle its entire ATP pool many times a minute.

Whichever theme the essay title is built around, treat this content as the mechanism paragraph every other paragraph "cashes in" on: whenever a later paragraph needs energy for a process, name it explicitly as ATP hydrolysis coupled to that reaction, and state where the ATP came from — ATP synthase, during respiration or photosynthesis.

🚩

Never let "produces energy" slip in under essay-writing time pressure — it's the easiest mark to lose in a rushed final paragraph.

Section 7

🧷 Where this reappears, memory hook & final checklist

Where 3.1.6 reappears across the rest of the specification

  • Transport across cell membranes — ATP hydrolase driving active transport and co-transport, including the sodium–potassium pump.
  • Photosynthesis — ATP synthase in the light-dependent reaction; ATP used to reduce GP to triose phosphate in the Calvin cycle.
  • Respiration — substrate-level phosphorylation in glycolysis and the Krebs cycle; oxidative phosphorylation via ATP synthase.
  • Nervous coordination — ATP maintaining the resting potential and powering synaptic vesicle movement and neurotransmitter recycling.
  • Muscle contraction — ATP hydrolysis for the myosin power stroke and head detachment; phosphocreatine regenerating ATP.
  • DNA replication and nitrogen fixation — condensation reactions and the action of nitrogenase, both powered by the same phosphate-bond-hydrolysis principle.

Rule of thumb: if a question anywhere on the course asks what powers a process, "hydrolysis of ATP releases energy" is almost always part of the answer — and if it asks where that ATP came from, the answer is respiration or photosynthesis, via ATP synthase.

🧠

Memory hook — three lines to reuse everywhere. "ATP is made of adenine, ribose and three phosphate groups." · "Hydrolysis of ATP, catalysed by ATP hydrolase, gives ADP + Pi and releases energy." · "ATP is resynthesised by the condensation of ADP and Pi, catalysed by ATP synthase." Never write "produces energy."

Self-check before moving on

  • I can name all three components of ATP and explain why it's called a "nucleotide derivative."
  • I can describe hydrolysis (ATP + water → ADP + Pi, ATP hydrolase, energy released) and resynthesis (ADP + Pi → ATP, ATP synthase, condensation) without swapping the two enzymes.
  • I never write "ATP produces/makes/creates energy" — only "hydrolysis of ATP releases energy."
  • I can name the three phosphorylation routes (photophosphorylation, oxidative, substrate-level) and where each occurs.
  • I can spell "adenosine" correctly and never confuse it with "adenine."

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Structure of ATP

  • Three named components: adenine (nitrogen-containing base), ribose (pentose sugar), three phosphate groups.
  • Adenine + ribose = adenosine — never count "adenosine" as one of the three components on its own.
  • ATP is a nucleotide derivative: same sugar-base-phosphate pattern as a nucleotide, but three phosphates instead of one.
  • vs a DNA nucleotide: ribose not deoxyribose, three phosphates not one, base always adenine not variable.

2 · Hydrolysis of ATP

  • ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pi, catalysed by ATP hydrolase.
  • Energy is released — never "produced/made/created." This is the single most policed phrase on the spec.
  • Coupling: released energy drives an energy-requiring reaction directly, instead of being lost as heat.
  • Phosphorylation: the freed phosphate makes a substrate more reactive / raises its energy level / lowers activation energy.
  • Good immediate energy source because: small release each time, instantaneous, phosphorylates as it goes, rapidly resynthesised, stays in the cell.

3 · Resynthesis of ATP

  • ADP + Pi → ATP, a condensation reaction (releases water), catalysed by ATP synthase — never shorten to "ATPase."
  • Three routes: photophosphorylation (thylakoid membrane, light-dependent reaction), oxidative phosphorylation (inner mitochondrial membrane, bulk of respiration's ATP), substrate-level phosphorylation (cytoplasm/glycolysis and Krebs cycle, no membrane needed).
  • Phosphocreatine can rapidly regenerate ATP in muscle: ADP + phosphocreatine → ATP + creatine.
🧠

Three lines to reuse everywhere. "ATP is made of adenine, ribose and three phosphate groups." · "Hydrolysis of ATP, catalysed by ATP hydrolase, gives ADP + Pi and releases energy." · "ATP is resynthesised by the condensation of ADP and Pi, catalysed by ATP synthase." Never write "produces energy."

Structure of ATP

❓ Name the components that make up a single molecule of ATP.
Adenine — a nitrogen-containing organic base; ribose — a pentose sugar; and three phosphate groups, joined in a chain to the ribose. "Adenosine" does not count as a separate component — it is the name for adenine and ribose combined.
❓ Contrast the structure of ATP with the structure of a DNA nucleotide.
ATP contains ribose, whereas a DNA nucleotide contains deoxyribose; ATP has three phosphate groups, whereas a DNA nucleotide has one; ATP's base is always adenine, whereas a DNA nucleotide's base varies (cytosine, thymine or guanine). Both halves of each point must be given for credit.
❓ Explain why ATP is described as a "nucleotide derivative" rather than simply a nucleotide.
It has the same basic pattern as a nucleotide — one sugar, one base and phosphate groups — but carries three phosphate groups chained together instead of the single phosphate group a standard nucleotide has.

Hydrolysis of ATP

❓ Describe the hydrolysis of ATP.
A water molecule is used to break the bond holding on the terminal phosphate group of ATP, in a reaction catalysed by the enzyme ATP hydrolase: ATP + water → ADP + Pi (adenosine diphosphate and inorganic phosphate).
❓ Explain the function of ATP hydrolase in active transport.
ATP hydrolase catalyses the hydrolysis of ATP to ADP and Pi, which releases energy; this energy allows ions or molecules to be moved against their concentration gradient, i.e. it drives active transport. (Energy is released, never "produced," "made" or "created.")
❓ Explain what is meant by "phosphorylation" and why it matters when ATP is hydrolysed.
Phosphorylation is the transfer of a phosphate group to a substrate; the phosphate released during ATP hydrolysis can be transferred to another compound, making it more reactive, raising its energy level, or lowering the activation energy of the reaction that follows.

Resynthesis of ATP

❓ Describe the resynthesis of ATP.
ADP and Pi condense together, releasing a water molecule, in a reaction catalysed by the enzyme ATP synthase — the reverse of hydrolysis: ADP + Pi → ATP.
❓ Name the three processes by which ATP can be resynthesised, and state where each occurs.
Photophosphorylation — in chlorophyll-containing cells, during the light-dependent reaction, as protons cross the thylakoid membrane through ATP synthase. Oxidative phosphorylation — in the mitochondria, as protons cross the inner mitochondrial membrane through ATP synthase; this provides the bulk of the ATP made during respiration. Substrate-level phosphorylation — in the cytoplasm during glycolysis and in the Krebs cycle, where a phosphate group is transferred directly from a substrate molecule to ADP.
❓ A respiratory inhibitor is added to a cell culture and acts only on the inner mitochondrial membrane. Predict and explain the effect on ATP production.
ATP production by oxidative phosphorylation stops on the mitochondrial membrane, since the enzymes there are no longer working; however, ATP production continues in the cytoplasm by substrate-level phosphorylation during glycolysis, which does not depend on that membrane, so a reduced amount of ATP is still made overall.
