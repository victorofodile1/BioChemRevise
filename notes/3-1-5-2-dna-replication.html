DNA Replication

A small but vicious topic — only two official content points, yet one of the highest-difficulty spec points in the whole of 3.1. Helicase versus polymerase, the 5–6 mark mechanism sequence, why replication only runs one way, and the Meselson–Stahl evidence that proved the whole model, all need to be word-perfect.

⚠️

The single rule that costs the most marks on this spec point: DNA helicase and DNA polymerase never do each other's job. Helicase unwinds the double helix and breaks hydrogen bonds — it forms no bond at all. Polymerase joins adjacent nucleotides, forming phosphodiester bonds — it never "forms hydrogen bonds" and never "joins bases." One enzyme breaks, the other builds. Never swap them.

Section 1 · spec content point

🧬 Semi-conservative replication — what it means and why it matters

Only one sentence long, but every word carries marks. AQA tests this by asking you to unpack what "semi-conservative" actually means, not just recite the label.

📖

What the specification says: "The semi-conservative replication of DNA ensures genetic continuity between generations of cells."

1
2
3

Imagine photocopying a two-page contract by keeping one original page exactly as it is, and writing a fresh matching copy of the other page from it — rather than writing a whole new contract from memory. Every "copy" made this way still has one genuine original page inside it, so the words can never quietly drift or change by accident. That's exactly what DNA does every time a cell is about to divide: instead of building two brand-new molecules, it keeps one original half and builds one new half to match it precisely. Because a real original half is always sitting there as the check-copy, the genetic "text" stays identical every single time.

  • Semi-conservative = "half-kept" — half of every new molecule is the original.
  • Genetic continuity = passing on exactly the same genetic information, generation after generation.
  • Interphase = the stage of the cell cycle when DNA gets copied, before the cell actually divides.

"Semi-conservative" describes what happens to DNA's two strands when it copies itself: rather than building a whole new molecule from scratch, or completely scrambling old and new material together, each new DNA molecule ends up with one strand from the original molecule plus one freshly built strand. Keeping an original strand around as a template means the sequence of bases gets copied precisely, so when a cell divides by mitosis, both resulting cells end up with DNA carrying exactly the same genetic information as each other and as the parent cell. All of this happens before division even starts, during interphase of the cell cycle — by the time chromosomes are visibly condensing at the start of mitosis or meiosis, the DNA has already been copied.

The definition, precisely: semi-conservative replication means that every new DNA molecule produced is made of one original (parental / template) strand and one newly synthesised strand — never two brand-new strands, and never two original strands staying together.

Why that guarantees genetic continuity: because one original strand survives intact in every new molecule and acts as the template for building the other strand, complementary base pairing copies the base sequence exactly. That precise copying is what "ensures genetic continuity between generations of cells" actually means — mitosis then distributes one of these identical, newly completed DNA molecules to each daughter cell, so every daughter cell inherits the same genetic information.

Timing: replication happens during interphase (specifically the S phase) of the cell cycle, before mitosis or meiosis begins — the chromosomes you see condensing at the start of division have already been fully copied by that point.

📌

Marking habit worth building now: if a question asks you to explain what "semi-conservative" means, naming the term again earns nothing. You have to say explicitly that each new DNA molecule consists of one original strand and one new strand — that sentence is the mark, not the label.

Section 2 · spec content point

🔗 The replication process — unwinding, base pairing & the two enzymes

This is the single most heavily and precisely tested sentence on this spec point. A 5–6 mark "describe semi-conservative replication" question is really six separate marking points in a strict order — learn it as a sequence of short sentences, not a jumble of facts you hope covers everything.

📖

What the specification says: "The process of semi-conservative replication of DNA in terms of: unwinding of the double helix • breakage of hydrogen bonds between complementary bases in the polynucleotide strands • the role of DNA helicase in unwinding DNA and breaking its hydrogen bonds • attraction of new DNA nucleotides to exposed bases on template strands and base pairing • the role of DNA polymerase in the condensation reaction that joins adjacent nucleotides."

1
2
3

Think of the double helix as a long zip fastener. One helper — helicase — runs along the zip and pulls it apart, breaking the tiny "clips" (hydrogen bonds) holding the two sides together. That exposes both sides of the zip at once. New teeth (free nucleotides) floating nearby click into place against the exposed pattern on each side — each tooth only fits one specific partner. A second helper — polymerase — then runs along and welds each new tooth firmly onto the next one, building a brand-new side of the zip. When it's finished, you have two zips, and each one is half old teeth, half new teeth.

  • Helicase = the helper that unzips and breaks the clips (hydrogen bonds).
  • Polymerase = the helper that welds the new teeth together (phosphodiester bonds).
  • Nucleotide = one "tooth" — a single DNA building block.

DNA helicase acts like the zip's slider: it moves along the double helix, breaking the hydrogen bonds that hold the two strands' bases together, so the two strands separate and unwind. Both exposed strands then act as templates. Loose nucleotides floating nearby pair up against the exposed bases on each strand — A always pairs with T, and C always pairs with G. DNA polymerase then moves along each template, physically joining these newly paired nucleotides into a continuous new strand, using a condensation reaction that links each nucleotide's sugar to the next nucleotide's phosphate group — this bond is called a phosphodiester bond. Once finished, you're left with two DNA molecules, and each one is a mix of one original strand and one newly built strand.

  1. DNA helicase unwinds the double helix, breaking the hydrogen bonds between the two strands
  2. Both strands are now exposed and act as templates — not just one
  3. Free nucleotides pair up with the exposed bases by complementary base pairing (A–T, C–G)
  4. DNA polymerase joins the newly paired nucleotides together
  5. This joining forms phosphodiester bonds, in a condensation reaction
  6. Each new DNA molecule ends up with one original strand and one new strand
  1. DNA helicase moves along the DNA molecule, breaking the hydrogen bonds between the complementary bases on the two strands, and so unwinds the double helix.
  2. This exposes the bases on both strands — both act as templates for building a new strand, not just one.
  3. Free DNA nucleotides in the surrounding nucleoplasm are attracted to the exposed bases and line up by complementary base pairing — A with T, C with G.
  4. DNA polymerase moves along each template strand, joining the adjacent free nucleotides of the new strand together.
  5. This joining is a condensation reaction, forming phosphodiester bonds between the sugar of one nucleotide and the phosphate of the next (releasing water) — building the new strand's sugar–phosphate backbone.
  6. The result is two new DNA molecules, each made of one original (template) strand and one newly built strand.

▶ 🚩 The single costliest omission

The most common half-mark loss on this spec point is writing "one strand acts as a template," when the mark scheme needs both strands stated explicitly. Never let this collapse into a vague single-strand statement — say "both strands act as templates" every time you describe the mechanism.

▶ 🚩 Never swap the enzymes' jobs

Never credit DNA polymerase with forming hydrogen bonds or "joining bases" — its job is strictly building the phosphodiester backbone. And never describe helicase's action as "hydrolysis of hydrogen bonds" written as one garbled phrase — "breaks" or "hydrolyses" the bonds is fine on its own, but don't merge the two words together.

📌

Answer the question actually asked: if a full replication mechanism answer is written in response to a question about transcription instead, it is capped at a low mark — a memorised sequence you're comfortable reciting is not a substitute for reading what the question asks.

Section 3 · exam technique & application

➡️ Why replication runs in one direction on each strand

This is a step up from reciting the mechanism. AQA shows a diagram of antiparallel strands and asks you to reason, from first principles, why DNA polymerase can only extend a strand in one direction — not to recite base pairing again.

DNA polymerase can only add a new nucleotide onto a free 3′–OH group at the end of a growing strand, so every new strand it builds grows in the 5′→3′ direction. The reasoning needs to be anchored specifically on DNA polymerase, not "the enzymes" in general: it is a highly specific enzyme with an active site of a particular shape, and only substrates presented in a complementary orientation can bind to that active site at all. Because the two parental template strands are antiparallel — one running 5′→3′ and the other 3′→5′ — the nucleotides on each template are aligned in opposite orientations. That forces DNA polymerase to synthesise new DNA in opposite physical directions along the two templates at the same time.

DNA polymerase can only extend a free 3′–OH end, so every new strand grows 5′→3′. Because the two templates are antiparallel, synthesis runs in opposite physical directions along them at once.
▶ 🚩 Never give hydrogen bonds or base pairing as the reason

Hydrogen bonding and base pairing explain which bases pair up — they do not explain which end DNA polymerase is able to add a nucleotide to. If a question specifically asks why replication is directional, the reasoning has to rest on DNA polymerase's active-site specificity and the antiparallel arrangement of the strands, not on base pairing again.

Section 4 · the named "evaluate the work of scientists" skill

🧪 Validating the Watson–Crick model — the Meselson–Stahl experiment

This is the single hardest and most cross-topic angle on 3.1.5.2. It demands reasoning, not recall: describing an experiment and using it to argue why semi-conservative replication was accepted over two rival models.

Before this experiment, three rival models of replication were still on the table: conservative (the original molecule stays completely intact, and an entirely new molecule is built from scratch), semi-conservative (each new molecule has one old strand and one new strand), and dispersive (new molecules are a patchwork mixture of old and new fragments scattered throughout).

The experiment

  1. Bacteria were grown for many generations in a growth medium containing the heavy nitrogen isotope ¹⁵N, so every base in their DNA incorporated ¹⁵N.
  2. They were then transferred into a medium containing the lighter isotope ¹⁴N and allowed to keep dividing.
  3. DNA was extracted after each generation and spun in a centrifuge to separate it by density — heavier DNA settles lower in the density gradient.
  4. After one generation, all of the DNA banded at a single, intermediate density — this rules out the conservative model, which would instead have produced two separate bands, one fully heavy and one fully light.
  5. After two generations, there were two bands — half intermediate density, half light — which rules out the dispersive model, since that would instead give a single band of steadily decreasing density rather than two distinct bands.
  6. This pattern is exactly what semi-conservative replication predicts.
Generation 1's single intermediate band rules out the conservative model; Generation 2's two bands (intermediate + light, equal amounts, no fully heavy band ever again) rule out the dispersive model — leaving semi-conservative replication as the only model consistent with both results.
📌

What a full-mark answer needs: say explicitly what each generation's result rules out, not just what it shows. The marks are for that reasoning chain — not for simply naming Meselson and Stahl.

How this illustrates the scientific process

  • Watson and Crick's model made a testable prediction about how DNA would replicate.
  • Other scientists designed an experiment whose results could have falsified that prediction — the strength of the evidence lies in the fact that the rival models were actively ruled out, not just that the data happened to "fit."
  • The work was published, peer-reviewed, and the results were repeated by others before the model was widely accepted.
  • Acceptance ultimately rested on independent lines of evidence agreeing with each other — X-ray diffraction data, Chargaff's base ratios, and this replication experiment.

Predicting results for an unfamiliar generation number

Work this out from first principles rather than memorising a table. After n generations grown in the light (¹⁴N) medium, the total number of DNA molecules is 2ⁿ, and exactly 2 of those molecules still contain an original heavy strand. So the proportion of intermediate-density DNA is 2 ÷ 2ⁿ, and the rest is light.

A favourite deduction: no fully heavy DNA is ever seen again after the very first generation — every molecule from generation 1 onward contains at least one newly synthesised (light) strand.

▶ What AQA's examiner reports flag most often

The roles of DNA helicase and DNA polymerase are a very frequent source of lost marks: common errors include describing helicase's action only as "unzips" or "unwinds" without the specific hydrogen-bond detail expected at this level, crediting DNA polymerase with "forming hydrogen bonds between bases" (it doesn't — helicase breaks hydrogen bonds, polymerase never forms them), and confusing DNA polymerase's role with mRNA or protein synthesis. Many answers describe phosphodiester bond formation well as a condensation reaction, but a minority incorrectly attribute the bond formed to hydrogen bonds instead. Others believe DNA "actively pulls" free nucleotides into place, rather than nucleotides being attracted to exposed bases and pairing passively by complementary base pairing.

▶ Command words on this spec point

Describe ("give an account of") wants the full ordered sequence of events for the replication mechanism, not just a summary label. Explain ("give reasons for / make relationships clear") is used both for what "semi-conservative" means and for why replication proceeds in one direction — both want the underlying mechanism or reasoning, not a restatement of the fact itself. State ("express in clear terms") is used for the role of each enzyme — it wants one precise, separate statement per enzyme, not the two roles merged into a single sentence about "the enzymes." Evaluate ("weigh up evidence and arguments to reach a judgement") is the named skill for the Meselson–Stahl section — it wants an assessment of how the results ruled out rival models, not just a description of the procedure. Predict ("give a plausible outcome from given information") applies to working out the proportion of intermediate-density DNA after an unfamiliar number of generations, using 2 ÷ 2ⁿ.

Where this reappears elsewhere in the course

  • Mutation (gene mutations): mutations can arise spontaneously during replication if a nucleotide is inserted in the wrong place; DNA polymerase proofreads as it goes, which is why errors stay rare.
  • The cell cycle & binary fission: replication occurs in interphase, before mitosis, so daughter cells receive identical DNA. In binary fission, the circular bacterial DNA and any plasmids are replicated before the cytoplasm divides.
  • Gene technologies & PCR: PCR is an artificial copy of the same underlying chemistry — heat replaces helicase, and heat-stable Taq polymerase replaces DNA polymerase — but both natural replication and PCR rely on complementary base pairing and phosphodiester bond formation.

5-Minute Cram

Every high-yield fact on this spec point, cut down to what carries the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Semi-conservative replication

  • Each new DNA molecule = one original strand + one new strand. Never just say "semi-conservative" — spell out the strand composition.
  • This ensures genetic continuity: an original strand as template copies the base sequence exactly.
  • Happens during interphase, before mitosis or meiosis begins.

2 · The replication process

  • Order: helicase unwinds & breaks H bonds → both strands act as templates → free nucleotides pair (A–T, C–G) → polymerase joins nucleotides → phosphodiester bonds form (condensation) → 2 daughter molecules.
  • Helicase breaks hydrogen bonds only. Polymerase forms phosphodiester bonds only — never swap these.
  • "One strand acts as a template" is a common half-mark loss — always say both.

3 · Why replication runs one way

  • DNA polymerase can only extend a free 3′–OH end, so new strands always grow 5′→3′.
  • Templates are antiparallel, so synthesis runs in opposite physical directions on the two strands at once.
  • Never cite hydrogen bonding/base pairing as the reason — anchor on polymerase's active-site specificity.

4 · Validating the model — Meselson–Stahl

  • Bacteria grown in ¹⁵N, switched to ¹⁴N; DNA density-gradient centrifuged after each generation.
  • Gen 1 → one intermediate band, rules out conservative. Gen 2 → intermediate + light bands, rules out dispersive.
  • Say what each result rules out, not just what it shows — that reasoning chain is where the marks are.
  • Proportion intermediate after generation n = 2 ÷ 2ⁿ. No fully heavy DNA is ever seen again after generation 1.

5 · Common mistakes

  • Never: "polymerase forms hydrogen bonds," "joins bases," or garbled "hydrolysis of hydrogen bonds."

Semi-conservative replication

❓ A textbook states that DNA replication is "semi-conservative." Explain precisely what this term means.
Each new DNA molecule produced consists of one original (parental / template) strand and one newly synthesised strand — not two entirely new strands, and not two original strands remaining together.
❓ Explain why semi-conservative replication ensures genetic continuity between generations of cells.
Because one original strand is kept intact in every new molecule and used as a template, complementary base pairing copies the base sequence exactly; the resulting daughter cells therefore inherit DNA with an identical base sequence to the parent cell.
❓ At what stage of the cell cycle does DNA replication occur, and why does this timing matter?
During interphase, before mitosis or meiosis begins, so that a complete, identical copy of the DNA exists and is ready to be distributed to daughter cells once division occurs.

The replication process

❓ Describe the process of semi-conservative replication of DNA.
DNA helicase unwinds the double helix and breaks the hydrogen bonds between the two strands; both strands act as templates; free DNA nucleotides line up in complementary pairs against the exposed bases (A with T, C with G); DNA polymerase joins the nucleotides of the new strand together, forming phosphodiester bonds in a condensation reaction; each new DNA molecule consists of one original strand and one new strand.
❓ State the role of DNA helicase and the role of DNA polymerase separately.
DNA helicase: unwinds the DNA double helix and breaks the hydrogen bonds between the two strands (between base pairs, A–T and G–C). DNA polymerase: joins adjacent nucleotides of the new strand together, forming phosphodiester bonds / the sugar–phosphate backbone.
❓ A student writes "DNA polymerase joins the bases together to re-form the double helix." Identify and correct the error in this statement.
DNA polymerase does not join bases together — it joins adjacent nucleotides by forming phosphodiester bonds between the sugar of one nucleotide and the phosphate of the next, building the sugar–phosphate backbone; base pairing (between complementary bases) is a separate, earlier step that happens by attraction, not by an enzyme joining bases.

Why replication runs one way

❓ A diagram shows DNA polymerase moving along two antiparallel template strands during replication. Explain why the new strand can only be synthesised in one direction on each template.
DNA polymerase is a specific enzyme with an active site of a particular shape; that active site can only bind free nucleotides that are correctly orientated to be added to a free 3′–OH group, so it can only extend a new strand in the 5′→3′ direction; because the two template strands are antiparallel, the nucleotides are aligned differently on each one, forcing DNA polymerase to synthesise in opposite physical directions along the two templates.
❓ Explain why hydrogen bonding cannot be used to explain the directionality of DNA replication.
Hydrogen bonding explains which bases pair with which (A with T, C with G) — it does not explain which end of a nucleotide DNA polymerase's active site can bind to, which is instead determined by the enzyme's specificity for a free 3′–OH group.

Validating the model — Meselson–Stahl

❓ Describe an experiment that could distinguish between the conservative, semi-conservative and dispersive models of DNA replication.
Bacteria are grown for many generations in a medium containing the heavy isotope ¹⁵N, so all their DNA incorporates ¹⁵N; they are then transferred to a medium containing the lighter isotope ¹⁴N and allowed to divide; DNA is extracted after each generation and separated by density-gradient centrifugation, with heavier DNA settling lower in the tube; after one generation, a single intermediate-density band appears, ruling out the conservative model; after two generations, two bands appear — intermediate and light — ruling out the dispersive model; this pattern is exactly what semi-conservative replication predicts.
❓ Explain how the results of this experiment illustrate the wider scientific process, not just the result itself.
The model made a testable prediction; other scientists designed an experiment capable of falsifying it, and its strength lies in ruling out rival models rather than simply fitting the data; the work was published, peer-reviewed and repeated by others before being accepted; acceptance was reinforced by independent lines of evidence (X-ray diffraction, Chargaff's base ratios) agreeing with the replication result.
❓ Predict the percentage of intermediate-density DNA present after four generations of growth in the light medium, and explain your reasoning.
12.5% intermediate density. After n generations the number of DNA molecules is 2ⁿ, of which exactly 2 still contain an original heavy strand, so the proportion of intermediate DNA is 2 ÷ 2ⁿ; for n = 4 this is 2 ÷ 16 = 12.5%, with the remaining 87.5% being light.
