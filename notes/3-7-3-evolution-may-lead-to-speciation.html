Evolution May Lead to Speciation

How variation, selection and isolation combine to turn one population into two separate species — the fixed order AQA marks the causal chain in, the two named routes (allopatric and sympatric), and why chance — genetic drift — matters most when a population is small.

The single habit that protects the most marks on this spec point: never reverse the causal chain. The order is fixed — isolation first, then mutation and differing selection pressures, then divergent allele frequencies, then reproductive isolation as the end-point. Starting an answer with selection, or describing allopatric mechanisms in a sympatric answer, is capped hard even when every individual biological fact stated is correct. The second most costly slip: naming "an isolating mechanism" generically instead of a specific named type — temporal, behavioural, mechanical, gametic or polyploidy.

Section 1 · spec content point

🧬 Where phenotypic variation comes from

Everything else on this page — selection, drift, speciation — depends on one starting fact: individuals within a population differ from one another. This section is about where that difference actually comes from, and which sources of it can be inherited.

📖

What the specification says: "Individuals within a population of a species may show a wide range of variation in phenotype. This is due to genetic and environmental factors. The primary source of genetic variation is mutation. Meiosis and the random fertilisation of gametes during sexual reproduction produce further genetic variation."

1
2
3

No two rabbits in a field look exactly the same — some are bigger, some are faster, some have slightly different fur. Some of that comes from what happens to them during life (what they eat, how much they run around) and some of it comes from what they were born with, coded in their DNA. Only the "born with it" kind can be passed on to their babies. Brand new versions of that code appear completely at random every so often — like a typo appearing in a recipe — and mixing and matching existing recipes between two parents makes even more different combinations in the children.

  • Phenotype — what an organism actually looks like or how it behaves
  • Genotype — the genetic code (alleles) an organism carries
  • Mutation — a random change in DNA that creates a brand new version of a gene
  • Gamete — a sex cell (sperm or egg) carrying half the normal number of chromosomes

Variation in phenotype has two components. Environmental factors — diet, climate, disease exposure and other external conditions — change how an organism turns out without changing its underlying genetic code, so this kind isn't passed on. Genetic factors are the heritable part, and they come from three places: mutation creates brand new alleles that never existed before; meiosis shuffles existing alleles into new combinations, both by chromosomes lining up randomly and by chromosomes swapping segments with each other; and random fertilisation shuffles them again by combining whichever sperm happens to meet whichever egg. Only mutation makes anything genuinely new — the other two just deal out new hands from the same pack of cards.

Two components of phenotypic variation

  • Environmental factors — diet, climate, disease and other external conditions alter phenotype without altering the underlying genotype, so this component is not inherited.
  • Genetic factors — differences in the alleles an organism carries; this is the heritable component, and it has three distinct sources.
Source of genetic variationWhat it actually doesCreates new alleles, or new combinations?
MutationA random change to the base sequence of DNA, producing a version of a gene that did not exist in the population beforeNew alleles — this is the primary, and only, source of genuinely new genetic variation
Independent segregation (meiosis I)Homologous chromosome pairs line up randomly at the equator and are pulled to opposite poles independently of each otherNew combinations of existing alleles
Crossing over (meiosis I)Non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes exchange sections, swapping alleles between themNew combinations of existing alleles
Random fertilisationAny one of millions of genetically varied sperm can fuse with any one genetically varied eggNew combinations of existing alleles, at the zygote stage
📚

Vocabulary used across this whole page — worth learning precisely now: a gene pool is all the alleles present in a population at a given time (the definition must reference a population, not just a species); allele frequency is the proportion of one particular allele within that gene pool; speciation is the process by which new species arise from existing species; reproductive isolation is anything that prevents gene flow (interbreeding) between two populations, so their gene pools can no longer merge; genetic drift is a random, non-selective change in allele frequency caused purely by chance in which individuals survive and reproduce, and matters most in small populations. Recall from earlier work on classification: a species is a group able to interbreed to produce fertile offspring — this is exactly the test speciation is judged against.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "gene" is not the same word as "allele"

It is allele frequency, not gene frequency, that changes during evolution. A gene (say, the gene for coat colour) can stay present in a population at a completely stable rate while its different alleles (black-coat allele versus brown-coat allele) shift in proportion underneath it. Writing "gene frequency changes" where "allele frequency changes" is meant is marked as imprecise throughout this whole topic.

Section 2 · spec content point

⚔️ Natural selection: what actually causes it

Natural selection isn't a single event — it's a name for a pattern that emerges whenever certain pressures act on a varied population. This section is about naming those pressures precisely.

📖

What the specification says: "Predation, disease and competition for the means of survival result in differential survival and reproduction, ie natural selection."

1
2
3

Life is tough for every population: something might try to eat you, something might make you sick, and there's only so much food, space and mates to go around. Individuals that happen to cope better with these problems tend to survive longer and have more babies than individuals who cope worse. Over time, that means more of the population is made up of the "coped better" type. That whole pattern is what "natural selection" means.

  • Selection pressure — anything in the environment that makes survival or reproduction harder for some individuals than others
  • Differential survival — some individuals surviving more than others, because of a phenotypic difference

Three named selection pressures sit behind natural selection: predation (being eaten), disease (being infected) and competition for resources needed to survive — food, water, space, light, mates. Whichever pressure is acting, individuals with a phenotype that copes with it better survive for longer and reproduce more than individuals with a phenotype that copes worse. That uneven pattern of survival and reproduction, caused by those pressures, is natural selection — it isn't a separate step that happens afterwards.

The three named selection pressures

  • Predation — being eaten by another organism; individuals better able to avoid, escape or deter a predator survive to reproduce more often than those less able to.
  • Disease — infection by a pathogen; individuals with a phenotype more resistant to a disease are more likely to survive it and reproduce.
  • Competition for the means of survival — for finite resources such as food, water, space, light or mates; individuals better able to obtain these outcompete others for them.

Whichever pressure is acting, the result is the same underlying pattern: differential survival and reproduction — some individuals in the population survive and reproduce more successfully than others, because of a difference in phenotype. This pattern, caused by predation, disease and/or competition, is exactly what the term natural selection means; it is a definition, not a separate consequence.

▶ 🚩 Trap — organisms don't "adapt to" a pressure, and the pressure doesn't cause the mutation

Both "bacteria adapt to the antibiotic" and "the antibiotic causes the mutation" are rejected phrasing. A resistance allele already exists in the population at random, before the selection pressure is ever applied — the pressure (the antibiotic, the predator, the disease) only selects for individuals who already happen to carry an advantageous allele; it does not create that allele, and organisms do not choose or develop the trait in response to the pressure.

📌

Keep this section and Section 3 mentally separate: this section is about naming what causes differential survival and reproduction (the three pressures). Section 3 is about what that differential reproductive success then does to allele frequencies in the gene pool — the two content points are tested separately, even though they describe one continuous process.

Section 3 · spec content point

📈 Selective advantage and its effect on allele frequency

This is the mechanism question examiners ask constantly, in almost every series, usually dressed up as an antibiotic-resistance scenario: trace what a selective advantage actually does to a gene pool, generation after generation.

📖

What the specification says: "Those organisms with phenotypes providing selective advantages are likely to produce more offspring and pass on their favourable alleles to the next generation. The effect of this differential reproductive success on the allele frequencies within a gene pool."

1
2
3

Picture a race where the fastest runners are the ones most likely to have children who are also fast — because the "fast" version of the running gene keeps getting passed down, generation after generation, the whole crowd of runners gradually gets faster on average. That's what happens with any helpful trait: the individuals who have it tend to leave behind more children who also have it, so more and more of the population ends up carrying it.

  • Selective advantage — a phenotype that makes an individual more likely to survive and reproduce
  • Offspring — the next generation, produced by reproduction

An organism with a phenotype that gives it a selective advantage in its environment is more likely to survive long enough to reproduce, and more likely to produce a larger number of offspring, than an organism without that advantage. Because it passes its favourable allele on to those offspring, and its offspring do the same in turn, the proportion of that allele in the population's overall gene pool rises with each generation — this rise in frequency, produced purely by differences in reproductive success, is what natural selection actually changes at the level of the whole population.

The five-step chain from mutation to a shifted allele frequency

  1. A random mutation has previously produced a new allele, already present at low frequency in the population;
  2. In a given environment, individuals carrying that allele have a selective advantage (for example, resistance to an antibiotic, or a more favourable body size for the conditions);
  3. Those individuals are more likely to survive and reproduce than individuals without the allele;
  4. They pass the favourable allele on to their offspring;
  5. Over many generations, the allele's frequency increases within the population's gene pool — this rise in allele frequency is what evolution, at the population level, actually is.

Worked example: antibiotic resistance in bacteria

StageWhat happens
1Random mutation has already produced variation in the bacterial population; some bacteria carry an allele conferring resistance to the antibiotic.
2The antibiotic acts as a selection pressure: non-resistant bacteria are killed.
3Resistant bacteria survive and reproduce, passing the resistance allele to their offspring.
4Because bacteria reproduce extremely rapidly, the frequency of the resistance allele rises quickly across generations.

Resistance alleles can also spread between bacteria directly, by the transfer of plasmids during conjugation — a route worth mentioning where a question allows for it, alongside the standard selection route above.

▶ 🚩 Trap — the direction of causation

Reject any phrasing implying the antibiotic causes the mutation, or that the bacteria "become resistant" / "adapt" / "are immune" in response to exposure. The correct causal order is: the resistance allele already exists at random before the antibiotic is ever applied; the antibiotic then selects for the bacteria that already happen to carry it. "Immune" is also the wrong word for a bacterium that survives an antibiotic — the precise term is resistant.

Section 4 · spec content point

⚖️ Stabilising, directional and disruptive selection

Three different shapes the same underlying process can take, depending on which part of a population's variation is being selected against. Graph-reading questions on this section want the shift or narrowing described using the axes, not just the name of the pattern.

📖

What the specification says: "The effects of stabilising, directional and disruptive selection."

1
2
3

Imagine a class lined up by height. Stabilising selection punishes the shortest and the tallest, so the line stays clustered in the middle over time. Directional selection punishes just one end — say, all the short ones — so the whole line gradually shifts taller. Disruptive selection punishes the ones in the middle instead, so the line splits into a short group and a tall group, with hardly anyone left in between.

  • Mean — the average value of a measured feature across the population
  • Extreme — an individual with a very high or very low value of that feature

All three types select against a different part of the range of variation. Stabilising selection removes both extremes, so the population's mean stays put but the spread of variation narrows — a taller, narrower peak on a graph. Directional selection removes one extreme only, so the mean itself shifts towards the other end over generations — the whole peak slides sideways. Disruptive selection removes the middle, favouring both extremes instead — the single peak can split into two separate peaks, one at each extreme, which is the selection pattern that can start sympatric speciation.

TypeWhat happens to the populationWorked example
StabilisingSelects against both extremes; the mean is unchanged but the range of variation narrows (a taller, narrower peak on a graph)Human birth weight — both very low and very high birth weights have reduced survival, so the population stays clustered around an intermediate optimum
DirectionalSelects for one extreme; the mean shifts in that direction over generations (the whole peak moves)Antibiotic resistance in bacteria — the mean level of resistance rises generation on generation as the antibiotic keeps killing non-resistant individuals
DisruptiveSelects against the mean/intermediate, favouring both extremes; the single peak can split into two (a bimodal distribution) — this is the selection pattern that can start sympatric speciationA population exploiting two distinct resource types, each favouring a different extreme phenotype, with intermediates disadvantaged at both
📌

Graph-reading habit: describe the shift or narrowing with figures read from the axes ("the mean shifted from roughly 3.1 kg to roughly 3.4 kg" / "the range narrowed from 2.0–4.5 kg to 2.8–3.8 kg") — naming the correct type of selection on its own is not enough for full marks on a graph-interpretation question.

Section 5 · spec content point

🔄 Evolution as a change in allele frequency

A short but exact content point — this is the precise definition to reach for whenever a question simply asks "what is evolution?", rather than a looser description of organisms "changing over time".

📖

What the specification says: "Evolution as a change in the allele frequencies in a population."

1
2
3

Evolution doesn't mean one animal changing during its own lifetime — it means the whole group changing, generation after generation, because more copies of certain versions of genes are being carried by the population than before. It's a "whole crowd" kind of change, not a "one individual" kind of change.

  • Population — the whole group whose gene pool is being measured
  • Generation — one full cycle from parents to offspring

Evolution is defined precisely as a change in the frequencies of alleles within a population's gene pool, measured across generations — not a change within a single organism during its own lifetime, and not simply "organisms getting better suited to their environment" as a loose description. Section 3's mechanism (selective advantage → differential reproduction → allele frequency shift) is one route that produces this change; Section 8's genetic drift is another, entirely separate route that can also shift allele frequencies, just by chance rather than by advantage.

State it exactly like this on a definition question: evolution is a change in the allele frequencies within a population, over time / across generations. Two things are worth being precise about:

  • It is measured at the level of the population's gene pool, not any single individual — no organism "evolves" during its own lifetime.
  • It doesn't specify a mechanism — both natural selection (a directional, non-random change, covered in Sections 2–4) and genetic drift (a random, non-directional change, covered in Section 8) are routes by which allele frequencies can change, and both therefore count as evolution occurring.

This definition is also the quantitative background for speciation: Sections 6 and 7 describe what happens when this ongoing change in allele frequency proceeds independently, and for long enough, in two separated parts of what was once a single population.

🔗

How Sections 3, 4, 5 and 8 fit together: Sections 2–3 describe the mechanism (selective advantage → differential reproduction), Section 4 describes the three shapes that mechanism can take, Section 5 is the formal definition of the outcome, and Section 8 adds the one major route to the same outcome that has nothing to do with selective advantage at all.

Section 6 · spec content point

🚧 Reproductive separation and the accumulation of genetic difference

Before naming the two specific routes in Section 7, the specification first states the general principle underneath both of them — this section is that general principle, and it's worth being able to state on its own.

📖

What the specification says: "Reproductive separation of two populations can result in the accumulation of difference in their gene pools. New species arise when these genetic differences lead to an inability of members of the populations to interbreed and produce fertile offspring. In this way, new species arise from existing species."

1
2
3

Imagine two friendship groups that stop being able to hang out together. At first they're basically the same, but because they're no longer mixing, each group slowly starts doing its own thing, picking up its own habits. Given enough time apart, they become so different that even if they got back together, they wouldn't really fit in with each other any more. That's the same idea as two populations that stop being able to breed together — given enough time, they drift apart genetically until they're two separate species.

  • Gene flow — alleles moving between populations through interbreeding
  • Fertile offspring — offspring that can themselves reproduce successfully

When two populations of the same species become reproductively separated — unable to interbreed — there is no longer any gene flow between them, so each population's gene pool changes independently of the other's from that point onward. Because each is accumulating its own mutations and facing its own selection pressures (and possibly its own genetic drift), genetic differences between the two gradually build up. If those differences ever become great enough that members of the two populations can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring, they are, by definition, now separate species — new species have arisen from what was originally one existing species.

The general principle, stated precisely

  1. Two populations of the same species become reproductively separated — gene flow between them stops;
  2. With no gene flow, each population's gene pool now changes independently of the other's;
  3. Genetic differences between the two gene pools accumulate over many generations;
  4. Eventually, the accumulated genetic difference is great enough that members of the two populations are unable to interbreed and produce fertile offspring;
  5. At that point, by definition, the two populations are separate species — new species have arisen from one existing species.

Notice this content point does not yet specify how the two populations became reproductively separated in the first place — that's exactly what Section 7 supplies, by naming the two possible routes (a geographical barrier, or a non-geographical isolating mechanism). It also relies directly on the species definition used elsewhere in the course: a species is a group able to interbreed to produce fertile offspring, which is precisely the test being failed at step 4 above.

📌

Why "fertile" always has to be there: two populations can sometimes still mate and produce offspring even after a great deal of genetic divergence — the offspring (a hybrid) simply fails to survive, or survives but is sterile (unable to reproduce itself). Either outcome still counts as reproductive isolation and still satisfies this content point, which is why the species test is specifically about fertile offspring, not simply "offspring".

Section 7 · spec content point

🗺️ Allopatric and sympatric speciation

The two named routes into the general principle from Section 6. Both follow the same underlying chain in the same fixed order — what differs is only how gene flow gets stopped in the first place. This is the highest-stakes section on the whole page: the extended "explain how a new species could arise" question is built entirely from what follows.

📖

What the specification says: "Allopatric and sympatric speciation."

1
2
3

Allopatric ("all apart") speciation is what happens when a river, mountain or sea physically splits one population into two groups that literally cannot reach each other any more. Sympatric ("same place") speciation is trickier — the two groups never move apart at all, but something else stops them mating with each other, like breeding at different times of year, or doing a different mating "dance" that the other group doesn't recognise. Either way, once nothing is mixing the two gene pools together any more, each group starts to change on its own, until eventually neither group can breed with the other — two brand new species.

  • Allopatric — split apart by physical distance/a barrier
  • Sympatric — stays in the same place, split by behaviour or biology instead
  • Polyploidy — an organism suddenly ending up with extra whole sets of chromosomes

Both routes run through the same four-stage story, just with a different opening move. In allopatric speciation, a geographical barrier — a river, mountain range or stretch of sea — physically splits one population into two, so they can no longer meet to breed. In sympatric speciation, the two groups stay in the same physical location the whole time, but a specific non-geographical mechanism stops them breeding with each other instead — different breeding seasons, different courtship behaviour, incompatible mating anatomy, or gametes that simply can't fuse. Either way, from that point on: mutation keeps producing variation independently in each group, the two groups face different selection pressures, different alleles get favoured in each, and allele frequencies drift apart generation after generation — until eventually the two groups can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring, even if reunited. Two new species now exist where one used to be.

Allopatric speciation — the fixed seven-stage sequence

AQA fixes this as a seven-stage sequence, and marks are lost for reordering it — isolation always comes first, mutation always comes before selection:

  1. A geographical barrier arises (river, mountain range, sea, ice sheet) and physically separates the population into two;
  2. This causes reproductive isolation — no gene flow between the two groups, since they can no longer meet to mate;
  3. Mutation creates (further) variation, independently in each group;
  4. The two groups experience different selection pressures (different environments/conditions on each side of the barrier);
  5. Different alleles are favoured in each group — those individuals survive and reproduce more, passing the alleles on;
  6. Allele frequencies diverge between the two groups, generation after generation;
  7. Eventually the populations can no longer interbreed (to produce fertile offspring) — even if reunited — so they are now separate species.
Both rows run through the identical chain — mutation, different selection, divergent allele frequencies, reproductive isolation. What changes is only how gene flow gets stopped at the start: a physical barrier for allopatric, a named mechanism (Temporal / Behavioural / Mechanical / Gametic) for sympatric, with the outer boundary never splitting.
▶ 🚩 The single costliest slip on this spec point

Isolation must be at the beginning of the process, not the end — and mutation must be placed before selection. An answer that starts with selection, or that reverses this order, loses marks even if every individual biological fact stated is correct. Second most costly: describing allopatric (geographical) mechanisms when a question specifically asks about sympatric speciation is capped hard — nationally, most students default to allopatric content even when a question explicitly directs them to sympatric, and are marked down accordingly.

Sympatric speciation — the same chain, a different opening move

The sequence is identical from mutation onward. What differs is what stops gene flow in the first place: instead of a geographical barrier, a specifically named non-geographical isolating mechanism arises within one shared location. A generic "isolating mechanism" is never enough — at least one must be named and defined:

  • Temporal (seasonal) isolation — subgroups breed, flower, or are fertile/receptive at different times of year (or different times of day), so they are never able to mate with each other;
  • Behavioural isolation — subgroups evolve different courtship displays, mating rituals, calls or songs, so individuals no longer recognise each other as potential mates;
  • Mechanical isolation — physical or anatomical differences prevent successful mating, e.g. genital incompatibility, or a change in flower structure that stops the same pollinator transferring pollen between the two forms;
  • Gametic isolation — the gametes of the two forms are chemically or structurally incompatible and fail to fuse (or, in plants, pollen fails to germinate on the wrong stigma).

From there, the sequence rejoins the same chain: no gene flow between the two forms → different selection pressures and/or genetic drift act independently on each (even within one broad shared area, different micro-habitats or resources may still apply) → allele frequencies diverge → reproductive isolation → two new species.

🌱

Polyploidy — a one-generation shortcut, mainly in plants. An error in meiosis or mitosis can give an individual a whole extra set (or sets) of chromosomes. That polyploid individual's chromosomes fail to pair correctly at meiosis with the original parent species, so it cannot produce fertile offspring with them — but it can reproduce successfully with other individuals sharing the same new chromosome number, or asexually, founding a new, reproductively isolated species immediately, without any of the multi-generation steps above.

Classifying the named isolating mechanisms

MechanismWhich route it belongs to

All of these are credited through the same underlying idea — whichever mechanism is named, it must be explained as something that prevents gene flow between the two gene pools.

Section 8 · spec content point

🎲 Genetic drift in small populations

A route to changed allele frequency that has nothing to do with any allele being better or worse — purely a matter of chance, and one that only really matters when a population is small.

📖

What the specification says: "The importance of genetic drift in causing changes in allele frequency in small populations. Students should be able to explain why genetic drift is important only in small populations."

1
2
3

Imagine picking 5 sweets at random from a jar with 50 red and 50 blue sweets in it — you could easily end up with 4 red and 1 blue, just by luck, even though the jar is an even split. Now imagine picking 1000 sweets from that same jar — you'd end up much closer to a 50/50 split, because with so many picks, the luck evens itself out. A small population is like the small handful: pure chance can swing its make-up a long way, purely because there aren't many individuals to average things out over.

  • Inbreeding — mating between close relatives, more likely in a small population
  • Founder event — a new population starting from just a few individuals
  • Bottleneck — a population crashing down to a small size before recovering

In a small, isolated population, inbreeding is more likely, and by pure chance, only some individuals happen to survive and reproduce in any given generation — nothing to do with which alleles they carry being better or worse. Because only some individuals pass on their alleles, an allele's frequency can shift randomly from one generation to the next, and an allele can even become fixed (reach 100%) or be lost (reach 0%) from the population entirely, purely by chance. This effect matters far more in small populations than large ones, because in a large population these chance fluctuations tend to average out; in a small one, they don't.

How genetic drift works

  • In a small, isolated population with a small gene pool, inbreeding is more likely;
  • By chance, only some individuals happen to survive and reproduce, so the alleles they carry are passed on and others are not — purely by chance, not because of any selective advantage;
  • This can cause an allele's frequency to change randomly, generation to generation, and an allele can even become fixed (reach 100%) or lost (reach 0%) from the population entirely, by chance;
  • The effect is much greater in a small population, where chance has a proportionally larger influence on the outcome.
▶ 🚩 Trap — "inbreeding" is not "interbreeding"

Never write "interbreeding" where "inbreeding" is required — the two are opposite in meaning here. Inbreeding means mating between close relatives, and is what makes drift more likely in a small population. Interbreeding means mating between two different populations or groups, and is the opposite idea — it's what reproductive isolation prevents. Also avoid purposive language such as "in order to adapt" when describing drift — drift has no direction and confers no advantage, unlike selection.

Drift versus selection

Natural selectionGenetic drift
Why allele frequency changesThe allele affects survival or reproductionPurely by chance — nothing to do with the allele's effect
DirectionDirectional — produces adaptation to the environmentRandom — can just as easily remove a beneficial allele as a harmful one
Relationship to population sizeEffective regardless of population sizeInversely related to population size — large populations average chance effects out, small ones don't

Both processes change allele frequency, and both can contribute to speciation, but only selection explains why a population becomes better suited to its environment. Drift is most significant after a founder event (a new population starting from a small number of individuals) or a genetic bottleneck (a population crashing to a small size before recovering) — both scenarios where a small, non-representative sample of the original gene pool ends up founding or repopulating the group.

▶ Why this spec point trips people up more than it should

The vocabulary here is recalled reasonably well as isolated terms — most students can define allopatric and sympatric speciation on request. Where marks are actually lost is in application: explaining how a specific, unfamiliar geographic or reproductive barrier leads to speciation, in the exact fixed order, for an organism the question invents on the spot. Sympatric speciation is a persistent weak point nationally — even when a question explicitly directs students to it, many still default to describing allopatric (geographical) speciation instead, and lose marks as a result. Genetic drift is also frequently confused with natural selection as the explanation for allele frequency change, especially in founder-effect and bottleneck scenarios, where drift is usually the better answer. A useful habit before writing any extended speciation answer: silently label each sentence with its stage number (1–7) from the fixed sequence before committing it to paper, and check that a specific named mechanism appears if the question says "sympatric."

Section 10 · exam technique

✍️ Using this spec point in longer written answers

Speciation is a natural payoff paragraph in essay-style questions about natural selection, variation or diversity — it's the point where accumulated variation becomes an irreversible, population-level outcome. The trick is closing the paragraph by returning explicitly to the essay's title word, rather than repeating a generic summary of the mechanism.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around the importance of natural selection

Speciation is the largest-scale, longest-term consequence natural selection can produce. Once two populations become reproductively isolated — whether by a geographical barrier or by a named non-geographical mechanism — no gene flow occurs between them, so different selection pressures can favour different alleles in each population independently. Over many generations, allele frequencies diverge until the two populations can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring: an entirely new species has formed. This shows natural selection's importance is not limited to fine-tuning a single population to its environment — given enough time and reproductive isolation, it can generate the biodiversity of species themselves.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around the causes and importance of variation

Genetic variation, arising from mutation and reshuffled by meiosis and random fertilisation, is the raw material every stage of this process depends on. Without it, isolated populations would have nothing for selection or drift to act on, and no divergence could ever occur. Speciation is where that variation's importance becomes permanent: two once-interbreeding populations accumulate enough genetic difference, through independent mutation and differing selection pressures, that interbreeding to produce fertile offspring is no longer possible. In this way, the variation present within a single population is ultimately what allows entirely new species to arise from it.

🧭

Essay technique worth keeping: placing selection before isolation is a scored error in extended speciation writing, exactly as it is in shorter structured questions — always place isolation first in the sentence order. Close the paragraph by returning explicitly to the essay's title word (e.g. "importance" or "variation") and stating plainly that speciation is the point at which accumulated variation becomes irreversible.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Where variation comes from

  • Two components: environmental (not inherited) and genetic (inherited).
  • Only mutation creates brand new alleles — it's the primary source.
  • Meiosis (independent segregation + crossing over) and random fertilisation only create new combinations of existing alleles.
  • Never write "gene frequency" — it's allele frequency that changes.

2 · Natural selection defined

  • Three named pressures: predation, disease, competition for the means of survival.
  • Result = differential survival and reproduction — that pattern is natural selection, not a separate step after it.
  • Never say organisms "adapt to" a pressure — the allele already existed at random before the pressure acted.

3 · Selective advantage & allele frequency

  • Five-step chain: mutation → selective advantage → more survival/reproduction → allele passed to offspring → allele frequency rises.
  • Antibiotic resistance is the classic worked example — resistance allele exists first, antibiotic selects for it.
  • Reject "bacteria adapt" / "the antibiotic causes the mutation" / "immune" for resistant.

4 · Stabilising, directional, disruptive

  • Stabilising: both extremes selected against, mean unchanged, range narrows (human birth weight).
  • Directional: one extreme selected for, mean shifts (antibiotic resistance).
  • Disruptive: mean selected against, peak splits in two — can start sympatric speciation.
  • Graph questions want figures from the axes, not just the type's name.

5 · Evolution defined

  • Evolution = a change in allele frequencies within a population's gene pool, over generations.
  • Not a change in one organism during its own lifetime.
  • Both selection (directional) and drift (random) count as routes to evolution.

6 · Reproductive separation

  • No gene flow → gene pools change independently → genetic difference accumulates → eventually can't interbreed to produce fertile offspring → new species.
  • "Fertile" offspring is the exact test — a surviving but sterile hybrid still counts as isolation.

7 · Allopatric & sympatric speciation

  • Fixed order for both: isolation → mutation → different selection → allele frequencies diverge → reproductive isolation.
  • Allopatric: geographical barrier splits the population.
  • Sympatric: named mechanism instead — temporal, behavioural, mechanical, gametic, or polyploidy (one generation, mainly plants).
  • Describing allopatric content in a sympatric answer is capped hard — always name a specific non-geographical mechanism.

8 · Genetic drift

  • Small population → inbreeding → by chance only some individuals reproduce → allele frequency changes randomly → fixation or loss possible.
  • Matters only in small populations — large populations average chance out.
  • "Inbreeding" ≠ "interbreeding" — do not mix these up.
  • Most significant after a founder event or genetic bottleneck.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Where variation comes from

❓ State the three sources of genetic variation in a population, and identify which one is the only source of entirely new alleles.
The three sources are mutation, meiosis (independent segregation and crossing over) and random fertilisation of gametes. Only mutation is the source of entirely new alleles — meiosis and random fertilisation only produce new combinations of alleles that already exist in the population.
❓ A student writes "environmental factors cause genetic variation." Explain why this is incorrect.
Environmental factors (such as diet or climate) alter an organism's phenotype without altering its genotype, so this variation is not genetic and is not heritable; genetic variation instead arises from mutation, meiosis and random fertilisation.
❓ Name the two processes occurring during meiosis I that each produce new combinations of alleles.
Independent segregation of homologous chromosomes, and crossing over between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes.

Natural selection defined

❓ State the three named pressures that result in natural selection, according to the specification.
Predation, disease, and competition for the means of survival (such as food, water, space or mates).
❓ Explain why "bacteria develop resistance in response to an antibiotic" is an imprecise description of natural selection.
It implies the antibiotic causes the resistance to arise, or that bacteria change in response to it; in reality, the resistance allele already exists at random in the population before the antibiotic is applied, and the antibiotic simply selects for individuals that already carry it, killing those that do not.

Selective advantage & allele frequency

❓ Explain, using the correct sequence, how a selective advantage leads to a change in allele frequency within a gene pool.
Individuals with a phenotype giving a selective advantage in a given environment are more likely to survive and reproduce than individuals without it; they pass the favourable allele on to their offspring; over many generations, the frequency of that allele increases within the population's gene pool.
❓ A population of insects is sprayed repeatedly with an insecticide over several years. Predict and explain the change in the frequency of the resistance allele over this period.
Variation already exists in the population due to mutation, with some insects carrying a resistance allele; the insecticide acts as a selection pressure, killing non-resistant insects; resistant insects survive and reproduce, passing on the resistance allele; so the frequency of the resistance allele increases in the population over successive generations.

Stabilising, directional, disruptive selection

❓ A graph shows the distribution of beak depth in a bird population before and after a drought. After the drought, the mean beak depth has increased and the range of variation has narrowed slightly around the new mean. Identify and explain the type of selection shown.
Directional selection — individuals with one extreme phenotype (deeper beaks, better able to crack the harder seeds available after the drought) were more likely to survive and reproduce, so the mean beak depth shifted towards that extreme over generations.
❓ Explain how disruptive selection could lead to a single population splitting into two distinct phenotypic groups.
Individuals with intermediate phenotypes are selected against, while individuals at both extremes are selected for; this removes the middle of the distribution over generations, so the single peak of variation splits into two separate peaks (a bimodal distribution) — this is the pattern of selection that can begin sympatric speciation if it is later paired with reproductive isolation.

Evolution defined

❓ Define evolution in terms used by the specification.
Evolution is a change in the allele frequencies within a population, over time / across generations.
❓ Explain why "an individual giraffe evolved a longer neck during its lifetime" is scientifically incorrect.
Evolution is a change in allele frequency at the level of a population's gene pool across generations, not a change occurring within a single organism during its own lifetime; an individual cannot evolve.

Reproductive separation

❓ Explain how reproductive separation of two populations can eventually result in the formation of a new species.
With no gene flow between the two populations, each gene pool changes independently through mutation and differing selection pressures; genetic differences between the two populations accumulate over many generations; eventually, the accumulated differences are great enough that members of the two populations can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring, so they are now separate species.
❓ Two previously separated populations are reunited and are able to mate, producing offspring that are unable to reproduce. Explain whether this counts as evidence that the two populations are separate species.
Yes — the species test is the ability to interbreed to produce fertile offspring; because the offspring produced are sterile, the two populations cannot exchange genes through them, so reproductive isolation exists and the two populations are separate species, despite being able to mate.

Allopatric & sympatric speciation

❓ A population of freshwater fish becomes divided when a new sandbar forms across the middle of a lake, leaving two smaller lakes on either side. Explain how this could lead to the formation of two new species.
The sandbar forms a geographical barrier, causing reproductive isolation between the two fish populations, since they can no longer meet to breed; mutation continues to produce variation independently in each population; the two lakes present different selection pressures, so different alleles are favoured in each; allele frequencies diverge between the two populations over many generations; eventually the two populations are unable to interbreed to produce fertile offspring, even if the sandbar were later removed, so they are now separate species.
❓ Two populations of the same insect species live in the same field but come to feed and breed only on two different plant species that flower at different times. Explain how this could result in sympatric speciation, naming the isolating mechanism involved.
The isolating mechanism is temporal (seasonal) isolation — because the two plant species flower at different times, the two insect populations are never breeding-ready at the same time and so cannot interbreed, despite sharing the same location; from this point, mutation and different selection pressures act independently on each population, allele frequencies diverge, and the populations eventually become unable to interbreed to produce fertile offspring, forming two new species.
❓ A new plant arises with double the normal number of chromosome sets compared with its parent species, following an error in meiosis. Explain why this plant is immediately reproductively isolated from its parent species.
This is an example of polyploidy; the polyploid individual's chromosomes cannot pair correctly at meiosis with those of the parent species, so it cannot produce fertile offspring with parent-species individuals; it can, however, reproduce with other individuals sharing the same new chromosome number, or asexually, meaning a new species can arise in a single generation without the multi-generation steps of typical allopatric or sympatric speciation.

Genetic drift

❓ Explain genetic drift, and explain why it is important only in small populations.
In a small, isolated population, inbreeding is more likely; by chance, only some individuals happen to survive and reproduce, so only their alleles are passed on, purely by chance rather than because of any selective advantage; this can cause an allele's frequency to change randomly, and an allele can even become fixed or lost from the population entirely. The effect is important only in small populations because chance fluctuations are averaged out in large populations, but have a proportionally much larger effect when there are few individuals.
❓ A small population of birds is blown to a remote island and founds a new population. Several generations later, one allele that was rare on the mainland is now very common on the island, despite conferring no obvious survival advantage. Suggest an explanation.
This is most likely the result of a founder effect, a form of genetic drift: the small number of founding individuals happened, by chance, to carry a higher-than-average frequency of that allele; because the founding population was small, this chance sample was not representative of the mainland population's allele frequencies, and this random effect has persisted and been amplified across subsequent generations.
❓ Distinguish between natural selection and genetic drift as explanations for a change in allele frequency.
Natural selection changes allele frequency because the allele affects an individual's survival or reproduction, and is directional, producing adaptation to the environment. Genetic drift changes allele frequency purely by chance, is random, does not produce adaptation, and has an effect that is inversely related to population size.
