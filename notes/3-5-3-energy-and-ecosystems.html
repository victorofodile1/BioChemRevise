Energy and Ecosystems

Section 1 · spec content point

🌱 Producers, biomass and how it's measured

Everything in this topic starts here: a plant fixes carbon into organic compounds, keeps most of it for itself, and what's left over becomes the biomass that every other measurement on this page is built from.

📖

What the specification says: "In any ecosystem, plants synthesise organic compounds from atmospheric, or aquatic, carbon dioxide. Most of the sugars synthesised by plants are used by the plant as respiratory substrates. The rest are used to make other groups of biological molecules. These biological molecules form the biomass of the plants. Biomass can be measured in terms of mass of carbon or dry mass of tissue per given area. The chemical energy store in dry biomass can be estimated using calorimetry."

1
2
3

A plant grabs carbon dioxide from the air or water and turns it into sugar. Most of that sugar gets "burned" straight away to give the plant energy — like using fuel to run an engine. Whatever sugar is left over gets built into the plant's actual body — its leaves, stem, roots — and that body-stuff is called biomass. To find out how much biomass a plant has made, scientists dry it out completely (so water doesn't confuse the measurement) and weigh it, or burn a bit of it in a special machine to see how much energy is stored inside.

  • Biomass = the actual "body material" of living things, built from carbon
  • Dry mass = the weight left once every last drop of water has been removed
  • Calorimetry = burning a sample to measure exactly how much energy is locked inside it

Plants photosynthesise, turning carbon dioxide into organic compounds such as sugars. Most of that sugar is used as a respiratory substrate — respired to release the ATP the plant needs to live. Whatever sugar isn't respired gets converted into other biological molecules — proteins, lipids, cellulose and so on — and together these make up the plant's biomass. Because biomass is really just carbon-based material, it can be measured either as the mass of carbon present, or more practically as dry mass — the mass once all water has been removed, since fresh mass is unreliable (water content varies for reasons that have nothing to do with how much living material is actually there). The chemical energy locked up in that dry biomass can then be measured using calorimetry — burning a weighed sample and using the heat released to calculate the energy stored per gram.

The route from carbon dioxide to a measurable number

Trace the chain in order, because this exact sequence is the kind of thing a "complete the sentence" question tests directly: organic compounds (built by photosynthesis from CO₂) → most are used as respiratory substrates → the remainder is converted into other biological molecules that form biomass → biomass is measured as mass of carbon, or as dry mass of tissue, per given area → the chemical energy store in that dry biomass is estimated using calorimetry.

The dry-mass method, and why it beats fresh mass

  1. Weigh a sample of the plant material, then place it in an oven at a moderate, controlled temperature.
  2. Reweigh at intervals as it dries. Once successive weighings come out the same, all the water has been driven off and the sample is at a constant mass.
  3. That constant mass is the dry mass — a genuine measure of the amount of organic material present.

Why not just weigh it fresh? Fresh (wet) mass includes water, and water content varies hugely between samples and from day to day, for reasons that have nothing to do with how much actual living material is there. Dry mass strips that variability out, so it reflects biological material alone — though the trade-off is that the sample is destroyed in the process, so the same individual can't be tracked over time.

Calorimetry — measuring the energy, not just the mass

A known dry mass of the sample is completely burned in oxygen inside a calorimeter. The heat released raises the temperature of a known volume of water, and that temperature rise is used to calculate the chemical energy released per gram of tissue — which can then be scaled up to an area and a time to give units such as kJ m⁻² year⁻¹.

▶ 🚩 Trap — calorimeter vs colorimeter

These two instruments are one letter apart and measure completely different things: a calorimeter measures energy released by burning a sample — the instrument used here. A colorimeter measures colour/absorbance, and belongs to a different part of the course entirely. Mixing the two up is a well-known, easily avoided way to lose an otherwise-safe mark.

📌

Generous mark-scheme wording: in the "complete the sentence" style of question testing this chain, alternatives such as cells, tissues, organisms and even NPP are often accepted in place of "carbon" at the relevant gap — the sequence matters more than hitting one single word.

Section 2 · spec content point

🔋 Gross and net primary production — GPP and NPP

This is the calculation hub of the whole spec point. Two definitions, one equation — and almost every mark on this page traces back to knowing exactly what GPP and NPP each represent.

📖

What the specification says: "Gross primary production (GPP) is the chemical energy store in plant biomass, in a given area or volume, in a given time. Net primary production (NPP) is the chemical energy store in plant biomass after respiratory losses to the environment have been taken into account, ie NPP = GPP − R. This net primary production is available for plant growth and reproduction. It is also available to other trophic levels in the ecosystem, such as herbivores and decomposers."

1
2
3

Imagine a plant "earns" energy from sunlight through photosynthesis — that total amount earned is GPP. But the plant has to spend some of that energy just to stay alive (breathing/respiring) — think of that like paying bills. Whatever's left over after paying the bills is NPP, and that's the plant's real "savings" — energy it can actually use to grow, make seeds, or pass on to anything that eats it.

  • GPP = total energy earned by photosynthesis
  • R = energy spent on respiration (the "bills")
  • NPP = GPP minus R — what's actually left over

GPP is the total chemical energy a plant captures through photosynthesis, in a given area over a given time. Plants don't keep all of that — they respire a large chunk of it away as heat, just to power their own life processes. NPP is what's left once that respiratory loss (R) has been subtracted: NPP = GPP − R. This remaining energy is what's actually available for the plant to grow and reproduce with, and it's also the only energy that's available to anything further up the food chain — herbivores eating the plant, or decomposers breaking it down, can only ever access NPP, never the full GPP.

TermDefinition — learn exactlyNotes
GPPThe chemical energy store in plant biomass, in a given area or volume, in a given time — the total energy fixed by photosynthesisTypically only around 1–3% of the light energy falling on an area ends up as GPP
RRespiratory losses to the environmentMostly lost as heat
NPPNPP = GPP − R — the chemical energy store in plant biomass after respiratory losses have been accounted forAvailable for plant growth and reproduction, and to other trophic levels (herbivores, decomposers)

"Chemical energy store minus respiratory losses" is, on its own, enough to define NPP fully — that phrase covers both the GPP idea and the subtraction of R in one line. Units are energy (or mass) per unit area per unit time — typically kJ m⁻² year⁻¹ — and you should be ready to derive these units from given axes or data rather than simply recalling them.

Worked example — calculating NPP from GPP and R

A crop has GPP = 30,000 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹. Respiratory losses R = 21,000 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹.

  1. Write down the equation: NPP = GPP − R
  2. Substitute the values: NPP = 30,000 − 21,000
  3. NPP = 9,000 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹
▶ 🚩 The most common national error on this equation

Many students believe NPP simply increases whenever "there are more plants photosynthesising," without ever mentioning respiratory losses — that reasoning is incomplete and isn't credited. NPP only rises if GPP rises relative to R; if respiration rises by the same amount, NPP stays flat. Equally common: adding faecal loss into this equation. Faecal loss has no place in NPP = GPP − R at all — it only ever appears in the separate consumer equation covered in Section 3.

▶ Interpreting NPP data given as a trend

Questions that show NPP changing over time or across sites want the explanation given as a paired statement — the trend, linked explicitly to a cause. For example: "NPP is constant because GPP and respiration are (roughly) equal," or "NPP is low because less light causes less photosynthesis, so GPP is lower." A bare description of the trend on its own, with no linked cause, is treated as incomplete and earns nothing.

Section 3 · spec content point

🐇 Net production of consumers — N = I − (F + R)

The consumer equivalent of NPP. Once energy has passed to an animal, it's still lost in the same broad way — some is never absorbed, some is burned for life processes — and what's left builds the animal's own biomass.

📖

What the specification says: "The net production of consumers (N) can be calculated as: N = I − (F + R), where I is the chemical energy store in ingested food, F represents the chemical energy lost to the environment in faeces and urine, and R is the respiratory loss to the environment."

1
2
3

Think of an animal's food like pocket money. I is everything it "earns" by eating. But some of that gets pooed or weed out without being used (F), and some gets spent keeping the animal alive and moving (R) — like paying bills. Whatever's left after those two types of "spending" is N — the animal's actual savings, which becomes its own growing body.

An animal ingests food, and the chemical energy in that food is I. Not all of it is used, though: some passes straight through undigested and leaves the body as faeces, or is lost as urine — together this is F. Of what's absorbed, a further chunk is used for respiration to power movement, growth and staying warm — this is R. What's left after both of those losses, N, is what actually becomes new biomass — growth, or in some cases reproduction.

N = I − (F + R), where I is the chemical energy store in ingested food, F is the chemical energy lost to the environment in faeces and urine combined, and R is respiratory loss. Read the specification's own wording carefully here: F is not faeces alone — it also includes urine, even though the two remain conceptually distinct processes (egestion of undigested material versus excretion of metabolic waste).

Worked example — calculating N for a consumer

A herbivore ingests I = 4,200 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹. It loses F = 2,700 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹ in faeces and urine, and R = 1,200 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹ in respiration.

  1. Write down the equation: N = I − (F + R)
  2. Add the losses first: F + R = 2,700 + 1,200 = 3,900
  3. Substitute: N = 4,200 − 3,900
  4. N = 300 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹
▶ 🚩 The brackets are not decorative

F and R must be added together before being subtracted from I. Subtracting only R, or only F, is a common arithmetic slip that quietly produces a completely wrong final value, even when the working looks otherwise sensible. Always compute (F + R) as its own step before the final subtraction.

Section 4 · spec content point

📊 Primary and secondary productivity

A short definition that's easy to skate past — but "production" and "productivity" are not interchangeable words, and mixing them up is a precision trap examiners specifically watch for.

📖

What the specification says: "Primary and secondary productivity is the rate of primary or secondary production, respectively. It is measured as biomass in a given area in a given time eg kJ ha⁻¹ year⁻¹."

1
2
3

"Production" is the total amount of stuff made — like the total distance you've walked. "Productivity" is how fast it's being made — like your walking speed. Two fields could end up with the same amount of plant matter (the same "production"), but one field might have got there twice as fast — it has higher "productivity."

Productivity is always a rate — biomass (or energy) produced per given area, per given unit of time. "Primary productivity" describes the rate at which producers (plants) build biomass; "secondary productivity" describes the rate at which consumers do. It's easy to blur "production" (a quantity, like NPP or N) with "productivity" (a rate at which that quantity accumulates) — but examiners treat these as genuinely distinct terms.

Primary and secondary productivity is the rate of primary or secondary production respectively — biomass (or its chemical energy equivalent) generated in a given area, in a given time, expressed as a rate such as kJ ha⁻¹ year⁻¹ or kJ m⁻² year⁻¹. This is a deliberately precise distinction: NPP and N (Sections 2 and 3) are themselves already rates once given proper units, but the specification separately names "productivity" as the general term for this rate concept, applicable to both producers (primary) and consumers (secondary).

📐

Maths-skills link: this is one of the places students are expected to derive the correct unit from context (recognise and use appropriate units, MS 0.1) rather than being handed it — read the axes or the data given, and check the unit genuinely represents "amount per area per time," not just "amount."

Section 5 · spec content point

🚜 Farming practices that increase energy-transfer efficiency

Humans can't change the ~10% rule, but they can change how much of a food chain's energy budget is diverted elsewhere before it ever reaches us — this section is about the two named ways farming does exactly that.

📖

What the specification says: "Students should be able to appreciate the ways in which production is affected by farming practices designed to increase the efficiency of energy transfer by: simplifying food webs to reduce energy losses to non-human food chains; reducing respiratory losses within a human food chain."

1
2
3

Two ways farmers keep more food energy for humans. First: stop other creatures stealing the crop or grass before we can eat it — weeds, pests and diseases are all "thieves" taking a share of the energy. Second: don't let farm animals waste energy — the less they run around or stay cold, the more of their food energy turns into meat instead of being burned off.

Simplifying food webs means removing anything that would otherwise divert a crop or livestock's NPP into a non-human food chain — pests, weeds and disease all compete for that energy, so killing or excluding them means more energy reaches humans instead. Reducing respiratory losses means cutting down how much energy livestock burn on things other than growth — movement, staying warm, or simply living longer than necessary — so more of their food energy converts into biomass/meat instead of being respired away as heat.

Simplifying food webs

Any organism that eats the crop or competes with it diverts energy into a non-human food chain — removing it means more of the crop's NPP reaches the human food chain instead.

  • Pesticides kill insect pests that would otherwise eat the crop.
  • Herbicides kill weeds competing with the crop for light, water and mineral ions, so more captured light energy is converted into crop biomass rather than weed biomass.
  • Fungicides reduce disease-related losses; fencing and pest exclusion keep larger herbivores away from the crop or livestock.
  • Biological control achieves the same outcome using a natural predator or pathogen instead of a chemical.

Reducing respiratory losses in livestock

  • Early slaughter — animals are killed while still growing, before more energy is spent simply maintaining an already-mature body.
  • Controlled/concentrate diet — a higher proportion of the food is digestible and absorbed, so less is lost undigested in faeces.
  • Restricted movement — less energy is spent moving, so less is respired away.
  • Sheltering/heating — less energy is spent generating body heat to counteract a cold environment.
  • Genetic selection for productivity — breeding for animals that naturally convert a higher proportion of ingested energy into biomass.
▶ 🚩 A named practice with no energetic consequence scores nothing

Every one of the practices above only earns credit when it's explicitly paired with its energetic effect. "Restricting movement" on its own is a bare, uncredited fact; "restricting movement, so less energy is respired/lost as heat, meaning more remains available for growth" is the version that actually scores. Never list farming practices without stating, each time, what that practice does to the energy budget.

⚖️

The welfare/cost counter-argument, for evaluate-style questions: intensive rearing raises genuine animal welfare concerns, increases disease risk (and so antibiotic use) in crowded conditions, and carries its own hidden energy costs — heating, feed transport — that can offset some of the efficiency gained. A balanced evaluation weighs the efficiency benefit against these costs rather than treating intensification as a free win.

Section 6 · applying the content

🧮 Energy flow, percentage efficiency & ecological pyramids

This is where every equation above gets put to work: the same three loss categories repeat at every single transfer up a food chain, the percentage-efficiency formula turns that into a number, and pyramids are just three different ways of drawing the same shrinking budget.

Where the energy actually leaks out

At every transfer — sun to producer, producer to primary consumer, consumer to consumer — energy is lost in the same three ways: as heat from respiration, as matter that is simply never eaten (roots, woody tissue, uneaten prey parts), and as matter that is eaten but not assimilated — egested as faeces. (Faeces is not the same as excretion — don't use the two words interchangeably.) Only a fraction of each level's energy passes on to the next, typically around 10% between consumer levels, and roughly 1–3% between the sun and producers.

The same three loss categories — heat from respiration, matter never eaten, and matter eaten but egested — repeat at every single transfer up the chain.

Why transfer is so inefficient — the full explanation

At the producer level, capture is inherently inefficient before respiration is even considered: some light misses the leaf entirely, is reflected or transmitted through it, or is simply the wrong wavelength for chlorophyll to absorb — and even where light is absorbed, photosynthesis itself typically converts only around 1–3% of it into GPP. At consumer levels efficiency rises to roughly 10%, but the losses shift form: some of what's eaten isn't digestible (cellulose, for instance) and is egested as faeces; some energy is lost in urine; and a large share is simply respired away as heat, especially in warm-blooded (homeothermic) animals that spend energy maintaining body temperature. Carnivores tend to use a higher proportion of their food than herbivores, because animal tissue is more digestible than plant tissue (far less indigestible fibre), so less is lost undigested.

The percentage-efficiency formula

🔢

% efficiency of transfer = (energy or biomass available to the next trophic level ÷ energy or biomass available at the level before) × 100. This ~10% figure is not a fixed law — it's a calculated value that varies between food chains and must always be worked out from real data.

Worked example — a standard one-stage calculation

NPP of producers = 20,000 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹. Energy available to primary consumers = 2,000 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹.

  1. Write the formula: % efficiency = (available to next level ÷ available at level before) × 100
  2. Substitute: % efficiency = (2,000 ÷ 20,000) × 100
  3. % efficiency = 0.1 × 100 = 10%

The hardest skill on this spec point — "percentage of a percentage"

The trickiest recurring question type asks what percentage of the original GPP a consumer ends up with, when that consumer's intake is itself already a percentage of NPP. The two most common errors are arithmetic slips of exactly a power of ten, and losing track of which stage of the energy budget a given number actually belongs to (GPP vs NPP vs N vs I).

How to tackle it: write out the full chain of given values and label each one (GPP, R, NPP, I, F, N) before calculating anything. Work out the percentage-efficiency formula at each individual transfer separately, rather than trying to combine transfers in your head. Then sense-check your final answer against the "roughly 10% per level" rule — an answer implying 60% efficiency between trophic levels almost always means a step has been mis-applied.

Worked example — a two-stage "percentage of a percentage" question

A woodland's GPP = 40,000 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹. Producer respiration R = 28,000 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹. The primary consumers' net production N = 1,200 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹. Find (a) the NPP available to primary consumers, and (b) what percentage of the original GPP ends up as primary consumer net production.

  1. Find NPP first: NPP = GPP − R = 40,000 − 28,000 = 12,000 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹
  2. Efficiency of the NPP → N transfer: (1,200 ÷ 12,000) × 100 = 10% — label this stage clearly; it is not yet the answer to part (b)
  3. For part (b), go back to the original GPP, not the intermediate NPP: (1,200 ÷ 40,000) × 100 = 3%
  4. Sense-check: 3% is lower than the "~10% per level" rule because it spans two transfers (GPP→NPP and NPP→N) compressed into a single calculation — a smaller final percentage is exactly what should be expected here, not a warning sign.
▶ 🚩 Dividing by the wrong "level before" changes the question you're answering

Dividing N by NPP answers "what's the efficiency of this one transfer?" Dividing N by the original GPP answers a completely different question: "what percentage of the original GPP survives all the way to this level?" Read what the question is actually asking, and label your working clearly enough that it's obvious which one you calculated.

Ecological pyramids — why energy is always upright, but numbers and biomass aren't

Because energy is always lost at every transfer, a pyramid of energy is always a true, upright pyramid — no exceptions. Pyramids of numbers or biomass are not guaranteed to be upright: a single large producer, such as one oak tree, can support a huge number of small consumers, giving an inverted pyramid of numbers even though the same food chain's biomass pyramid stays upright (one tree still outweighs all the insects feeding on it combined).

Widths are not to scale. A single oak tree supporting huge numbers of tiny aphids gives an irregular numbers pyramid — but the energy pyramid for the same chain is upright regardless, because energy is always lost at every transfer irrespective of organism size.

▶ Why this spec point is ranked hard

This is second-year-only content, and the difficulty doesn't come from conceptually hard biology — it comes from two given equations that must be rearranged and applied to unfamiliar numbers almost every time they're tested, rather than simply recited, combined with a genuinely high terminology-precision risk (faeces vs excretion, calorimeter vs colorimeter, production vs productivity, GPP vs NPP). Nearly every mark on this page is a calculation mark or a precisely-worded definition mark, which is exactly why showing full working and using exact terminology both matter so much here.

Section 8 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

This spec point is a natural building block for a 25-mark essay on food production, because energy-transfer efficiency is the most direct, quantifiable lever biologists have on yield.

▶ Example paragraph — "Using biological knowledge to increase food production"

NPP = GPP − R is the energy available to the next trophic level, and only around 10% of it typically transfers onward at each further step — which is exactly why eating plant crops directly, with fewer trophic levels between sunlight and plate, is more energy-efficient than eating livestock. Farming practices raise the proportion of that energy humans actually capture in two distinct ways: simplifying food webs — pesticides, herbicides and fencing removing competitors and pests so more of a crop's NPP reaches the human food chain rather than a non-human one — and reducing respiratory losses within the human food chain itself, through early slaughter, restricted movement, controlled diet and sheltering/heating livestock, so less of their food energy is burned off as heat before it becomes usable biomass. This is the most directly quantifiable of the biological levers on food production, because every named practice can be traced straight back to one of the two given equations.

Essay technique: this paragraph works well as an opening paragraph for a food-production essay, since it's the most concrete and calculation-backed of the biological mechanisms available — later paragraphs on nutrient supply or selective breeding can then be framed as further routes to the same underlying goal. Whichever angle the essay takes, always pair a named farming practice with its energetic consequence — a bare list of practices reads as description, not explanation, and scores accordingly.

Where 3.5.3 reappears later in the course

  • 3.5.2 Respiration — the R term in both NPP = GPP − R and N = I − (F + R) is the respiration studied in full detail there.
  • 3.5.4 Nutrient cycles — energy flows through an ecosystem once and is lost, while nutrients are recycled: a direct, examinable contrast worth stating explicitly if a question invites it.
  • 3.7.4 Populations in ecosystems — carrying capacity and intraspecific competition explain why crop and livestock density must be actively managed for maximum sustainable yield.
🧠

Memory hook — "three leaks, one flow." At every transfer — producer to consumer, consumer to consumer — energy leaks out three ways: respired as heat, never eaten, and eaten but not assimilated (egested in faeces — never call this excretion). What's left over is roughly a tenth of what came in, which is why energy pyramids are always true pyramids and why food chains rarely run past four or five links. And whenever a number appears in a question, ask first: is this GPP, NPP, I, F, R or N — before doing anything else with it.

Self-check before moving on

  • I can state GPP and NPP in precise wording, and apply NPP = GPP − R and N = I − (F + R) to given data, showing full working with F + R calculated before subtraction.
  • I can name the three itemised categories of energy loss between trophic levels — respiratory heat, uneaten material, and egested/undigested material — and I never call egestion "excretion."
  • I can apply the percentage-efficiency formula to a worked example, including a two-stage "percentage of a percentage" question, and sense-check my answer against the ~10% rule.
  • I can explain how farming practices increase energy-transfer efficiency by simplifying food webs and reducing respiratory losses, each paired with its energetic consequence — and I can weigh this against welfare/cost counter-arguments.
  • I can explain why a pyramid of energy is always upright, but pyramids of numbers or biomass are not (eg one large tree supporting many small insects).
  • I never confuse the calorimeter (measures energy released) with the colorimeter (measures colour/absorbance), and I never confuse "production" (a quantity) with "productivity" (a rate).

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Producers & biomass

  • Chain: organic compounds → most respired → rest builds biomass → measured as dry mass or mass of carbon → energy estimated by calorimetry.
  • Dry-mass method: weigh, oven-dry, reweigh to constant mass — fresh mass is unreliable because water content varies independently of biological material.
  • Calorimeter (energy) ≠ colorimeter (colour) — a one-letter trap.

2 · GPP & NPP

  • NPP = GPP − R. "Chemical energy store minus respiratory losses" scores the definition alone.
  • NPP is available for plant growth/reproduction, and to herbivores/decomposers — consumers never access GPP directly.
  • Never say NPP rises just because "more photosynthesis" — must reference respiration. Never add faeces into this equation.
  • Units: kJ m⁻² year⁻¹ (or similar) — be ready to derive them from the data given.

3 · Net production of consumers

  • N = I − (F + R). Add F and R together first — brackets are not optional.
  • F = faeces and urine combined, in this equation specifically.

4 · Productivity

  • Productivity = a rate (biomass per area per time, eg kJ ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹) — not the same word as "production."

5 · Farming & energy efficiency

  • Simplify food webs: pesticides/herbicides/fungicides/fencing/biological control — remove competitors so more NPP reaches humans.
  • Reduce respiratory losses: early slaughter, controlled diet, restricted movement, sheltering, genetic selection — always paired with the energetic effect.
  • Naming a practice with no energetic consequence scores nothing. Welfare/cost counter-argument: disease risk, antibiotic use, hidden energy costs of heating/transport.

Three leaks, one flow. Every transfer loses energy as heat (respiration), as matter never eaten, and as matter eaten but egested. ~10% survives each consumer transfer, ~1–3% survives sun→producer. Energy pyramids are always upright; numbers/biomass pyramids aren't. Always label GPP/NPP/I/F/R/N before calculating.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Producers & biomass

❓ Complete the chain: plants synthesise organic compounds from carbon dioxide; most sugars are used as ___; the rest form the plant's ___, measured as ___ per given area, with energy estimated using ___.
Respiratory substrates; biomass; dry mass (or mass of carbon); calorimetry.
❓ Describe and justify the method used to measure the dry mass of a plant sample.
Weigh the sample, then heat it in an oven, reweighing at intervals; once successive weighings are the same, the mass is constant and all water has evaporated — this is the dry mass. Fresh mass is unreliable because water content varies independently of the amount of biological material present, so dry mass gives a more accurate measure.
❓ A student measures the energy content of a dried plant sample using a colorimeter. Identify and correct the error.
A colorimeter measures colour/absorbance, not energy. The correct instrument is a calorimeter, which burns a known dry mass of sample and uses the resulting temperature rise of a known volume of water to calculate the chemical energy released.

GPP & NPP

❓ Define gross primary production and net primary production, and give the equation linking them.
GPP is the chemical energy store in plant biomass, in a given area or volume, in a given time — the total energy fixed by photosynthesis. NPP is the chemical energy store in plant biomass after respiratory losses have been taken into account: NPP = GPP − R.
❓ A field's GPP is 25,000 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹ and its respiratory losses are 17,500 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹. Calculate the NPP, showing your working.
NPP = GPP − R = 25,000 − 17,500 = 7,500 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹.
❓ A graph shows NPP staying constant across a growing season despite GPP rising steadily. Explain this pattern.
NPP remains constant because respiratory losses (R) are rising at the same rate as GPP, so GPP and respiration remain approximately equal, leaving GPP − R unchanged even though GPP itself is increasing.

Net production of consumers

❓ Give the equation for the net production of consumers, and define each term.
N = I − (F + R), where I is the chemical energy store in ingested food, F is the chemical energy lost to the environment in faeces and urine, and R is respiratory loss.
❓ A secondary consumer ingests 6,000 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹, loses 3,800 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹ in faeces and urine, and respires 1,600 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹. Calculate its net production.
N = I − (F + R) = 6,000 − (3,800 + 1,600) = 6,000 − 5,400 = 600 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹.
❓ A student calculates N by subtracting only R from I, ignoring F. Explain why this is incorrect.
F and R must be added together before being subtracted from I, because both faecal/urinary loss and respiratory loss reduce the energy available for growth — omitting F overstates the net production figure.

Productivity

❓ Explain the difference between "production" and "productivity" in the context of an ecosystem.
Production (eg NPP or N) is a quantity of biomass or energy generated. Productivity is the rate at which that production occurs — biomass in a given area, in a given time, eg kJ ha⁻¹ year⁻¹ — so two ecosystems could have equal production but different productivity if one reaches that amount faster.
❓ State an appropriate unit for primary productivity and justify it.
kJ m⁻² year⁻¹ (or kJ ha⁻¹ year⁻¹) — because productivity is a rate, the unit must include both an area term (per unit area) and a time term (per unit time), not energy or mass alone.

Farming & energy efficiency

❓ Explain how the use of herbicides can increase the efficiency of energy transfer to a human food chain.
Herbicides kill weeds that would otherwise compete with the crop for light, water and mineral ions; removing this competition means more of the light energy captured on that land is converted into crop biomass rather than weed biomass, so more NPP is available to the human food chain.
❓ Explain two ways in which farmers reduce respiratory losses in livestock to increase yield.
Restricting the movement of livestock reduces the energy respired for muscular activity, so more of their food energy remains as biomass. Keeping livestock sheltered or heated reduces the energy respired to maintain body temperature, again leaving more energy available for growth.
❓ Evaluate the claim that intensive livestock farming has no drawbacks, given that it increases energy-transfer efficiency.
While restricting movement and controlling diet do increase the proportion of food energy converted to biomass, intensive rearing raises animal welfare concerns, increases the risk of disease spreading in crowded conditions (and so antibiotic use), and has its own energy costs — heating and feed transport — which may offset some of the efficiency gained.
