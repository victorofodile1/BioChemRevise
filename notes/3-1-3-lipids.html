Lipids

⚠️

The three habits that protect the most marks on this spec point: always put the C=C double bond that makes a fatty acid unsaturated between carbon atoms in the hydrocarbon chain — never just "in the fatty acid," and never confuse it with the separate C=O bond in the carboxyl group. Always say phosphate group, never "phosphorus," for what replaces a fatty acid in a phospholipid. And keep a lipid droplet (increases surface area for lipase) functionally separate from a micelle (increases fatty-acid solubility and delivery) — merging the two is one of the most common ways marks are lost on this topic.

Section 1 · spec content point

💧 Two groups of lipid, and what they all share

Before either molecule is drawn out, learn what every lipid has in common — AQA is just as happy to test any one of these three shared facts in isolation as it is to test the two named groups.

📖

What the specification says: "Triglycerides and phospholipids are two groups of lipid."

1
2
3

Lipids are a big family of oily molecules that just don't mix with water — like the layer of oil that floats on top of vinegar in salad dressing. There are two branches of the family you need to know: triglycerides (fats and oils, used for energy) and phospholipids (the molecules that build the walls of cells). Both are built by joining smaller pieces together and squeezing out water as they join — but because there isn't one single repeated "lego brick" shape involved, scientists don't call a lipid a polymer, even though the joining process looks similar.

  • Insoluble = doesn't dissolve (lipids don't dissolve in water)
  • Organic solvent = a liquid like ethanol that fats do dissolve in
  • Condensation = joining two molecules together while releasing water
  • Polymer = a long chain built from one repeated building block

Every lipid you meet on this spec point is either a triglyceride or a phospholipid, and both share the same three background facts. First, every lipid is built from carbon, hydrogen and oxygen atoms — phospholipids simply have phosphorus added on top of that. Second, every lipid is insoluble in water but will dissolve in organic solvents such as ethanol; that single property is the entire reason the emulsion test (Section 8) works at all. Third, although both types are built by condensation — the same kind of reaction that builds a polypeptide or a polysaccharide — neither one counts as a polymer, because there's no single repeating monomer being strung together.

  • Elements: all lipids contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen; phospholipids additionally contain phosphorus.
  • Solubility: all lipids are insoluble in water, but soluble in organic solvents such as ethanol — this single fact underpins the entire emulsion test (Section 8).
  • Not polymers: lipids are macromolecules built by condensation, just like a polypeptide or a polysaccharide — but there's no single repeating monomer, so the monomer/polymer definition from 3.1.1 simply doesn't apply.
▶ 🚩 Trap — calling a triglyceride a "polymer"

Never use a triglyceride as a monomer–polymer example in a 3.1.1-style question. The reasoning carries straight over from the previous spec point: condensation without a single repeating monomer does not make something a polymer, no matter how many smaller units were joined together to build it.

📌

Hold these three facts in mind as background for the rest of the topic — almost every later section on this page builds on top of "insoluble in water," "contains C, H, O (+P for phospholipids)," and "not a polymer."

Section 2 · spec content point

🧬 Triglyceride formation — one glycerol, three fatty acids

This "describe how a triglyceride is formed" question is worth three marks awarded in a fixed pattern — miss the number three anywhere in your answer and you lose a mark, however good the rest of the sentence sounds.

📖

What the specification says: "Triglycerides are formed by the condensation of one molecule of glycerol and three molecules of fatty acid."

1
2
3

Picture glycerol as a three-pronged fork, and each fatty acid as a long noodle. Tying one noodle onto one prong squeezes out a drop of water and forms one knot. Tie all three noodles onto all three prongs, and you've used up 3 drops of water and tied 3 knots — and that finished fork-with-noodles is a triglyceride.

  • Glycerol = the small three-pronged "fork" molecule
  • Fatty acid = one long "noodle" that ties onto a prong
  • Knot = the ester bond formed at each join

A triglyceride is built from one glycerol molecule and three fatty acid molecules joining together. Every time a fatty acid attaches to glycerol, a molecule of water is released — a condensation reaction — and a new ester bond forms at the join. Because three fatty acids attach, you end up with three ester bonds and three released water molecules in total. On a labelled diagram, the three-carbon molecule at the centre is glycerol, each join is an ester bond, and the acidic end of each fatty acid chain is the carboxyl group.

  1. One glycerol molecule condenses with three fatty acid molecules.
  2. This is a condensation reaction, with the removal of three molecules of water.
  3. Three ester bonds are formed — one between glycerol and each fatty acid.
📌

Marking habit: the number three has to appear explicitly for both the fatty acids and the water molecules — "some fatty acids" or "water is removed" without the number loses that mark. Full credit is also available from a suitably labelled diagram or a balanced equation, so sentences aren't the only route to the marks.

Each of glycerol's three –OH groups condenses with the –COOH head of a fatty acid, forming an ester bond and releasing one water molecule every time — three fatty acids in, three ester bonds and three water molecules out.

Naming the parts on a labelled diagram

  • The three-carbon backbone → glycerol
  • The bond between glycerol and a fatty acid → ester bond
  • The functional group at the end of a fatty acid → carboxyl group (accept "carboxylic acid")
📌

The pattern worth remembering: number of water molecules removed = number of ester bonds formed = number of fatty acids attached. Work out any one of the three and you've worked out all of them — including for an unusual molecule with more than three fatty acids attached, where you'd simply count the ester bonds instead.

Section 3 · spec content point

🔗 The ester bond — naming it precisely

This is a short, one- or two-mark spec point, but it's a precision trap: the exact same reaction that builds carbohydrates and proteins builds lipids too, and mixing up the bond name is one of the most common ways to lose an easy mark.

📖

What the specification says: "A condensation reaction between glycerol and a fatty acid (RCOOH) forms an ester bond."

1
2
3

Think of the ester bond as a specific type of knot — the name of the knot depends on what you're tying together. Tie glycerol to a fatty acid, and it's called an ester knot. Tie sugar molecules together instead, and it's a completely different knot called a glycosidic bond — so never mix the two names up, whichever molecule the question is actually about.

The bond joining glycerol to a fatty acid has one specific name: an ester bond. It forms in exactly the same type of reaction — condensation, with the loss of a water molecule — that builds every other bond on this part of the course; what makes it "ester" rather than, say, "glycosidic" (used for sugars) or "peptide" (used for amino acids) is simply which two groups are joining: an –OH group from glycerol and the –COOH group from a fatty acid. This precision matters in application questions too: a fat substitute used in some foods isn't broken down by the fat-digesting enzyme lipase, because its shape doesn't let a proper ester bond form, so it can't fit into the enzyme's active site.

Naming and describing the bond

Name the bond: ester bond. Describe how it forms: a condensation reaction (accept "loss of water") occurs, between glycerol and a fatty acid. A labelled diagram showing the –OH and –COOH groups joining, with water leaving, earns the same credit as a full sentence.

▶ 🚩 Trap — ester vs glycosidic

Never write "ester bond" when a question is actually about a polymer (3.1.1) or a carbohydrate — there, the correct answer is glycosidic. The reverse trap costs marks just as often: never write "glycosidic" for a lipid question. If you commit to the wrong bond name across a whole answer, you're typically only penalised once rather than losing every mark that mentions it — so it's still worth naming a bond confidently rather than leaving it blank.

Application: why doesn't lipase break down a fat substitute?

Some manufactured fat substitutes (built from a different alcohol than glycerol) resist digestion. The reasoning always runs the same way:

  • The fat substitute is a different / wrong shape — not complementary — so the bond it forms with a fatty acid is different, meaning no true ester bond forms.
  • Lipase is therefore unable to fit / bind to its active site — no enzyme–substrate complex forms, so hydrolysis can't happen.
📌

This is a specificity argument, structurally identical to any other enzyme-and-substrate reasoning you'll use across the course: wrong shape → can't bind → no complex forms → no reaction. Anchoring the answer in shape and complementarity, rather than vaguely "it's not a fat," is what separates a full-mark answer from a partial one.

Section 4 · spec content point

〰️ Saturated vs unsaturated fatty acids

This is the single most error-prone idea in the whole spec point. The double bond that matters has an exact address — between two carbon atoms along the hydrocarbon tail — and it is not the same as the C=O double bond found at the acidic end of every fatty acid.

📖

What the specification says: "The R-group of a fatty acid may be saturated or unsaturated."

1
2
3

Imagine the fatty acid's tail as a length of rope made of carbon links. A saturated tail is a straight, tightly-packable rope — lots of these ropes can stack up neatly side by side, like solid butter. An unsaturated tail has a kink or bend somewhere along it (caused by a double bond), so the ropes can't stack up as neatly — like the loose, runny texture of vegetable oil. The important trick: that kink-causing bend has to be somewhere along the rope itself, not right at the very end where the rope ties onto glycerol.

Every fatty acid has a long hydrocarbon "tail," and that tail can be saturated or unsaturated. Saturated means every carbon–carbon bond in the tail is a single bond — the tail is "full up" (saturated) with hydrogen atoms, so it's straight and packs closely against its neighbours. Unsaturated means at least one of those carbon–carbon bonds is a double bond instead, which puts a kink in the tail so it can't pack as tightly. That packing difference is exactly why saturated fats tend to be solid at room temperature and unsaturated fats tend to be liquid oils. The double bond that makes a chain unsaturated has to sit between two carbon atoms somewhere along the tail — it's easy to muddle this with the different double bond (C=O) found at the acidic end of every fatty acid, but that one has nothing to do with saturation.

  • Saturated = single bonds only / no double bonds between the carbons in the hydrocarbon chain (accept "maximum number of H atoms bound").
  • Unsaturated = has at least one double bond between carbon atoms in the hydrocarbon chain (accept "alkene"; naming the C=C bond specifically can be enough on its own).
Both tails hang from the same carboxyl group (–COOH), which is where the fatty acid joins glycerol. The defining double bond sits between carbons further along the hydrocarbon tail — never inside the carboxyl group itself.
▶ 🚩 The single most common error in the whole topic

Saying "no C=C bonds" for saturated disqualifies that one mark on its own if it's the whole answer — and saying a fatty acid "contains C=O bonds" is neutral, not evidence of saturation, since every fatty acid has that bond in its carboxyl group. The double bond that actually matters must be located between carbon atoms in the hydrocarbon chain, not in the carboxyl group — mixing these two up is the single most common error across this entire spec point.

Identifying the unsaturated fatty acid from a diagram

State the type of R group (unsaturated / hydrocarbon), then give the explanation: a double bond between carbons (accept "C=C" for "double bond"). Also credited: "some carbons with only one hydrogen"; "not saturated with hydrogen"; or simply pointing to which labelled fatty acid in a diagram it is.

Application: why does the degree of saturation matter?

Saturated fatty acids pack closely together and are solid at room temperature; unsaturated fatty acids have kinks at their double bonds, so pack less closely and are liquid oils. This structural fact regularly gets turned into a population-level comparison — for example, a population with more saturated fatty acids has more energy readily available (useful for growth in warmer conditions), while a population with more unsaturated, liquid fatty acids may show more lipase activity (useful for growth in cooler conditions, since the fat stays liquid and accessible to the enzyme).

📊

Accompanying data-skill mark: if standard deviations for two populations don't overlap, the difference in their means is unlikely to be due to chance — in other words, it's statistically significant.

Section 5 · spec content point

🧫 Phospholipids — one substitution that changes everything

Swap just one fatty acid on a triglyceride for a phosphate-containing group, and a pure energy-storage molecule becomes the basic structural unit of every biological membrane.

📖

What the specification says: "In phospholipids, one of the fatty acids of a triglyceride is substituted by a phosphate-containing group."

1
2
3

Take the "fork and three noodles" picture of a triglyceride from Section 2, and swap just one noodle for a completely different attachment — a water-loving "head." Now the molecule has two oily tails, which still hate water, and one head that loves water, all on the same molecule. That's a phospholipid — and it's exactly why phospholipids can form the walls (membranes) around every cell, with their heads pointing out towards the watery surroundings on both sides and their tails huddling away from the water in the middle.

  • Phosphate group = the water-loving "head" that replaces one fatty acid
  • Hydrophilic = water-loving
  • Hydrophobic = water-hating

A phospholipid starts out built like a triglyceride, but with one twist: instead of three fatty-acid tails, one of those tails is swapped out for a phosphate-containing group. That leaves a phospholipid with two fatty-acid tails plus one phosphate group, rather than three fatty acids. This single substitution changes the molecule's chemistry completely: the phosphate end is attracted to water (hydrophilic), while the two remaining fatty-acid tails still repel water (hydrophobic) — which is exactly why phospholipids behave so differently to triglycerides once they're placed in water. In terms of elements, a phospholipid contains carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and phosphorus — but, unlike a protein or a nucleic acid, it does not contain nitrogen.

  • In a phospholipid, one fatty acid is replaced by a phosphate group.
  • So it has two fatty acids plus a phosphate group, instead of three fatty acids.
  • Accept Pi / PO₄³⁻ / a simple "P" symbol on a labelled diagram, and minor errors in the formula — but ignore any reference to saturation here, since it isn't relevant to this particular mark.
▶ 🚩 The second most common error in the topic

Never write "phosphorus" or "phosphorus group" for the substituent — it is specifically a phosphate group (phosphorus bonded to oxygen atoms), not the bare element. This precise-wording slip is the second most common error across the whole spec point, right behind the C=C-location error in Section 4.

One phospholipid (left) next to a cross-section of the bilayer it forms spontaneously in water (right): hydrophilic phosphate heads face outward into water on both sides, hydrophobic fatty-acid tails point inward. This is the basis of the fluid-mosaic membrane structure reused throughout Topic 3.2.

Which elements does a phospholipid contain?

Carbon, hydrogen and oxygen (as in any lipid), plus phosphorus — but not nitrogen. This is the row students most often get wrong on an elements tick-grid: nitrogen belongs to amino acids and nucleotides, not lipids.

Section 6 · spec content point

⚖️ Different properties from different structures

This is the applied heart of the topic — a 5-mark "compare and contrast" that punishes isolated single-molecule facts. Every point has to explicitly name both molecules, not describe one and leave the comparison to be inferred.

📖

What the specification says: "The different properties of triglycerides and phospholipids related to their different structures."

1
2
3

Triglycerides and phospholipids are cousins built from almost the same ingredients — both have a glycerol backbone joined to fatty acids by ester bonds — but one small ingredient swap gives them totally different personalities. A triglyceride is all oily tail with no water-loving part, so in water it just balls up into a droplet of fat — brilliant for storing energy, useless as a wall. A phospholipid has one water-loving head and two oily tails, so in water it automatically arranges itself into a sandwich (a bilayer) with the heads on the outside — and that sandwich becomes the wall around every cell.

Triglycerides and phospholipids share a surprising amount: both are built with ester bonds onto a glycerol backbone, both can have saturated or unsaturated fatty-acid tails, both are insoluble in water, and both contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen (phospholipids add phosphorus on top). The difference that changes everything is the tail count: a triglyceride keeps all three fatty acids, so it's hydrophobic all over and just forms an oily droplet in water; a phospholipid swaps one tail for a phosphate head, so it has both a water-loving end and water-hating ends, and automatically arranges itself into a monolayer, micelle or bilayer in water instead of a droplet. Functionally, a triglyceride's tightly packed C–H bonds make it an excellent, compact energy store, plus insulation, protection and buoyancy; a phospholipid's bilayer becomes the basic structure of every membrane, letting lipid-soluble substances diffuse straight through while keeping water-soluble substances out.

▶ 🚩 Trap — writing two separate facts instead of one comparison

Writing each statement as two disconnected facts, rather than one linked comparative sentence, loses the mark — inferred comparisons aren't credited. Every statement needs to be explicitly comparative ("…whereas a phospholipid…") or otherwise clearly linked by you; some points can instead be shown on adjacent, annotated diagrams rather than in prose. Both molecules are also worth remembering as non-polymers: built by condensation, but with no repeating monomer, so the 3.1.1 rule from Section 1 applies to both.

Explaining the function of each

TriglycerideHigh ratio of energy-storing C–H bonds to carbon atoms → an excellent energy store, releasing more energy per gram than carbohydrate. Why is a lipid-soluble molecule absorbed by simple diffusion?

  • Rate of uptake is proportional to concentration / doesn't level off — a pattern that shows diffusion, not a carrier-mediated process, is happening.
  • The lipid-soluble molecule can diffuse through / is soluble in the phospholipid bilayer itself.
📌

The converse question: why is a particular molecule not lipid-soluble? Because it's hydrophilic / polar / too large. "It is not lipid soluble" on its own just restates the question and earns nothing — the reason has to be given.

Section 7 · spec content point (applying Section 6)

🔄 Applying it: lipid digestion and absorption

Same specification content point as Section 6 — this is the fuller, more mechanistic synoptic pathway that puts "different properties from different structures" to work, from the gut lumen to the inside of an ileum epithelial cell.

📖

What the specification says: "The different properties of triglycerides and phospholipids related to their different structures." (Same content point as Section 6 — this half applies it to the digestion-and-absorption pathway, which AQA tests as a longer synoptic question built on exactly this structural knowledge.)

1
2
3

Think of digesting fat like breaking up an oil slick so it can be delivered door to door. Bile salts act like dish soap, breaking a big fat blob into loads of tiny droplets — more droplets means more surface for the fat-digesting enzyme (lipase) to chop through. The chopped-up pieces then hitch a ride inside tiny transport bubbles called micelles, which ferry them along to the gut lining. Once there, the fatty acids slip straight through the cell's oily wall by simple diffusion — no special door needed, because both are oily. Inside the cell, the fats get rebuilt, packaged up together with protein into a delivery parcel called a chylomicron, and shipped out the other side of the cell.

Bile salts break large lipid masses into small droplets — emulsification — which massively increases the surface area available for the enzyme lipase, so hydrolysis of the ester bonds in triglycerides happens faster. The fatty acids and monoglycerides this produces are packaged, along with bile salts, into structures called micelles, which keep the fatty acids soluble in the watery gut contents and carry them to the ileum's epithelial cells. Because fatty acids and monoglycerides are themselves lipid-soluble, they can then cross the cell membrane by simple diffusion, with no transport protein needed. Once inside the epithelial cells, triglycerides are re-formed and combined with protein to make chylomicrons, which are packaged into vesicles and leave the cell by exocytosis.

  1. Bile salts emulsify lipids into tiny droplets — this increases the surface area available for lipase to act on.
  2. Faster hydrolysis of triglycerides follows: lipase hydrolyses the ester bonds between fatty acid and glycerol.
  3. Micelles form, containing bile salts together with fatty acids / monoglycerides.
  4. Micelles make the fatty acids more soluble in water, and carry (or maintain a high local concentration of) them at the epithelial cell lining of the ileum.
  5. Fatty acids / monoglycerides are absorbed into the epithelial cells by simple diffusion.
  6. Triglycerides are re-formed inside the cells and combined with protein to form chylomicrons.
  7. Vesicles carrying the chylomicrons move to the cell membrane and are released by exocytosis.
Lipid digestion and absorption, start to finish. Droplets and micelles do different jobs — droplets increase surface area for lipase; micelles increase fatty-acid solubility and delivery — and mixing the two up is a very common way to lose marks here.
▶ 🚩 Trap — droplets vs micelles, and the wrong transport mechanism

Never write "micelles increase surface area" — that role belongs to the droplets, formed earlier in the pathway. Absorption described as facilitated diffusion, or as movement through any membrane protein, is also rejected — fatty acids and monoglycerides cross by simple diffusion, because they are lipid-soluble and can pass straight through the phospholipid bilayer. "Small enough" on its own isn't an accepted explanation either — solubility in the membrane is the reason, not size.

🔎

A vesicle-packaged Golgi apparatus modifies and finishes packaging the re-formed triglycerides with protein into chylomicrons, ready for exocytosis — this is a useful synoptic link if a question also asks about cell organelles or protein processing.

Section 8 · spec content point

🧪 The emulsion test

Only two marks, but sequence-critical — reversing the order of reagents, or heating the mixture, loses the method mark even if the rest of the answer is correct.

📖

What the specification says: "The emulsion test for lipids." (This is the required practical skill (AT f) for this spec point.)

1
2
3

Add a splash of alcohol to a fat sample and it dissolves right in — alcohol and fat get along fine. But pour that alcohol-fat mixture into water, and the fat suddenly can't stay dissolved — it bursts out as billions of tiny droplets, turning the water cloudy white, like a mini snowstorm inside the test tube. That cloudiness is your positive result. Do the steps in the wrong order — water first — and the trick just doesn't work.

The emulsion test works because lipids dissolve in ethanol but not in water. You add ethanol to your sample and shake, which dissolves any lipid present; then you add water. As soon as the dissolved lipid meets the water, it comes out of solution as thousands of tiny droplets, and those droplets scatter light — which is what makes the mixture look white and milky. That milky colour is the positive result. Getting the order backwards (water before ethanol), or heating the mixture, stops the test working properly and loses the method mark, even if you eventually see cloudiness.

Method

  • Add ethanol / alcohol, then add water and shake / mix (or: add ethanol and shake, then pour into / add water).
  • A white / milky emulsion shows that lipid is present.

Accept "alcohol" or a named alcohol; accept "milky." Accept "add Sudan III and mix" as an alternative reagent, with a red top layer as the positive result. Ignore "cloudy" and a second shake as extra, non-essential detail.

▶ 🚩 Trap — sequence, heating, and wording

The sequence must be correct — ethanol first, then water. Heating the emulsion test disqualifies the first mark entirely. The result is specifically an emulsion, never a "precipitate" — that word describes a different kind of reaction and is rejected here. Watch spelling too: "ethanal" and "ethonal" are both wrong words, not just typos of "ethanol."

Testing a solid food sample

  1. Crush / grind the sample.
  2. Add ethanol / alcohol.
  3. Then add water / add to water — forms an emulsion / goes white or cloudy.

Water must be added after ethanol for that third mark. Writing "carry out the emulsion test" without describing the actual method isn't accepted — you have to describe the steps.

🎯 Common mistakes & precise wording

This spec point is rated medium difficulty across every measure — memorisation load, application depth, misconception frequency and wording precision — and almost all of the marks lost trace back to a handful of precisely-worded distinctions, not genuine conceptual difficulty.

▶ Where marks are actually won and lost

Three precisely-worded distinctions — not conceptual difficulty — account for almost all of the marks lost on this spec point: the C=C-must-be-between-carbons trap, "phosphorus" instead of phosphate, and the droplet-vs-micelle confusion in digestion and absorption. A separate, common examiner observation is that triglyceride-specific questions get answered with generic "lipid" language — for example, wrongly giving a protein-processing answer about the Golgi apparatus for a question actually about triglycerides. The fix for all of these is the same habit: notice exactly which noun the question uses (lipid vs triglyceride vs phospholipid vs droplet vs micelle), and answer that specific thing.

▶ Command words used on this spec point

Describe wants the sequence of events (triglyceride formation, the emulsion test, digestion and absorption), not why it matters. Explain wants a property linked to its consequence (e.g. hydrophilic head → bilayer forms). Compare means every statement must reference both molecules throughout — isolated single-molecule facts score zero. Identify means pointing to the specific feature (e.g. the C=C double bond) rather than describing generally. Name wants an exact technical term — "ester," never "glycosidic," for a lipid question.

Section 10 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

3.1.3 almost always opens a longer essay-style answer rather than concluding one — it supplies the structural foundation that later paragraphs then build on.

Two essay-style titles are built directly on this spec point: "The importance of membranes in cells and organisms" and "The importance of bonds and bonding in organisms." This content also opens paragraphs on hormone-specificity questions, since a lipid-soluble (steroid) hormone can cross the phospholipid bilayer directly to bind an intracellular receptor, unlike a protein hormone that has to act at the cell surface.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around membranes

A phospholipid's hydrophilic phosphate head and hydrophobic fatty-acid tails mean that, in a watery environment, phospholipids spontaneously arrange themselves into a bilayer — the basic structure underlying every biological membrane, with the tails turned inward, away from water on both sides. That bilayer forms a selectively permeable barrier: lipid-soluble substances can diffuse straight through it, while the hydrophobic core blocks most water-soluble substances from crossing without a dedicated protein channel or carrier.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around bonding

Both triglycerides and phospholipids are held together by ester bonds, formed by condensation between glycerol's –OH groups and a fatty acid's –COOH group. The number and saturation of those fatty-acid tails then explains two very different biological roles from a shared bonding pattern: a fully saturated, three-tailed triglyceride packs tightly into a dense, high-energy fuel store, while a phospholipid's single phosphate substitution turns the same basic chemistry into a self-assembling, semi-fluid membrane.

Whichever theme the title concerns, treat 3.1.3 as the "structural foundation" paragraph: state the process precisely — naming the ester bond, or the hydrophilic/hydrophobic split, depending on what's being asked — then add one sentence that links it explicitly back to the essay's actual theme, rather than leaving the examiner to make that connection for you.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Two groups of lipid

  • All lipids: C, H, O (+ P for phospholipids); insoluble in water, soluble in organic solvents like ethanol.
  • Never a polymer — condensation, but no single repeating monomer.

2 · Triglyceride formation

  • 1 glycerol + 3 fatty acids → condensation → 3 ester bonds + 3 H₂O released. The number 3 must appear explicitly.
  • Glycerol = 3-carbon backbone; bond = ester bond; fatty acid's acidic end = carboxyl group.

3 · The ester bond

  • Name it "ester," never "glycosidic" (carbohydrates) — and vice versa.
  • Forms by condensation between glycerol's –OH and a fatty acid's –COOH.
  • Fat substitute question: wrong shape → no ester bond forms → lipase can't bind → no complex.

4 · Saturated & unsaturated

  • Saturated = no C=C between carbons. Unsaturated = ≥1 C=C between carbon atoms in the chain — never the carboxyl group's C=O.
  • Saturated packs closely → solid; unsaturated has a kink → liquid oil.
  • Non-overlapping standard deviations = significant difference.

5 · Phospholipids

  • One fatty acid replaced by a phosphate group — never say "phosphorus."
  • Two fatty-acid tails (hydrophobic) + one phosphate head (hydrophilic).
  • Contains C, H, O, P — not nitrogen.

6 · Properties from structure

  • Shared: ester bonds, glycerol, sat/unsat tails, insoluble in water, C/H/O.
  • Different: 3 tails vs 2 tails+phosphate; hydrophobic vs amphipathic; droplet vs monolayer/micelle/bilayer.
  • Every comparison point must be one linked, comparative sentence naming both molecules.

7 · Digestion & absorption

  • Bile salts emulsify → droplets ↑ surface area for lipase → faster hydrolysis of ester bonds.
  • Micelles (bile salts + fatty acids/monoglycerides) increase solubility, deliver to ileum cells.
  • Fatty acids/monoglycerides absorbed by simple diffusion → re-formed as triglycerides → chylomicrons → exocytosis.

8 · The emulsion test

  • Ethanol first, then water, shake → white/milky emulsion = positive.
  • Never heat; result is an emulsion, never a "precipitate."
  • Solid sample: crush/grind first. Combined test: max 4/5 if only 2 of 3 substances shown.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

1 · Two groups of lipid

❓ State two features shared by all lipids, other than the elements they contain.
All lipids are insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents such as ethanol; and all lipids are built by condensation reactions but are not classed as polymers, because there is no single repeating monomer.
❓ A student writes "a triglyceride is a polymer made from glycerol and fatty acid monomers." Identify and correct the error.
A triglyceride is not a polymer. Although it is built by condensation, glycerol and fatty acid are two different molecules, not one single repeating monomer, so the monomer–polymer definition does not apply.
❓ Explain why phospholipids, but not triglycerides, are described as containing phosphorus-based groups.
All lipids contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, but only phospholipids additionally contain a phosphate-containing group, which replaces one of the three fatty acids found in a triglyceride.

2 · Triglyceride formation

❓ Describe how a triglyceride is formed from its component molecules.
One glycerol molecule condenses with three fatty acid molecules; this is a condensation reaction, with the removal of three molecules of water; three ester bonds are formed, one between glycerol and each fatty acid.
❓ On a labelled diagram of a triglyceride, name the three-carbon backbone, the bond formed at each join, and the functional group at the end of each fatty acid.
The three-carbon backbone is glycerol; the bond formed at each join is an ester bond; the functional group at the end of each fatty acid is the carboxyl group (accept "carboxylic acid").
❓ A molecule is found to have five fatty acids attached to a modified glycerol-like backbone. State how many water molecules would be released during its formation, and explain your reasoning.
Five water molecules would be released, because the number of water molecules removed equals the number of ester bonds formed, which equals the number of fatty acids attached.

3 · The ester bond

❓ Name the bond formed between glycerol and a fatty acid, and describe how it forms.
Ester bond. It forms by a condensation reaction (loss of water) between glycerol and a fatty acid.
❓ A manufactured fat substitute is not broken down by lipase. Explain why.
The fat substitute has a different / wrong shape and is not complementary to the enzyme's active site, so the bond between the substitute and a fatty acid is different and no true ester bond forms; lipase is therefore unable to bind to it, so no enzyme–substrate complex is formed.
❓ A student answers a lipid question by naming the bond between glycerol and a fatty acid as "glycosidic." Explain why this is incorrect and give the correct term.
Glycosidic bonds join sugar molecules together in carbohydrates, not lipids; the correct term for the bond between glycerol and a fatty acid is an ester bond.

4 · Saturated & unsaturated fatty acids

❓ Distinguish between a saturated and an unsaturated fatty acid.
A saturated fatty acid has single bonds only, with no double bonds between the carbons in its hydrocarbon chain; an unsaturated fatty acid has at least one double bond between carbon atoms in its hydrocarbon chain.
❓ Two fatty acid diagrams are shown, one straight and one with a kink. Identify which is unsaturated and explain how you know.
The kinked chain is unsaturated; the kink is caused by a double bond (C=C) between two carbon atoms in the hydrocarbon chain, which prevents the chain packing as closely as a straight, saturated chain.
❓ Population A has a higher proportion of saturated fatty acids than Population B. Suggest one physiological consequence of this difference and explain your reasoning.
Population A's fatty acids pack more closely together and remain solid at a wider range of temperatures, providing more concentrated, readily available energy; Population B's more unsaturated, kinked fatty acids stay liquid at lower temperatures, which may support enzyme (e.g. lipase) activity in cooler conditions.

5 · Phospholipids

❓ Describe how a phospholipid differs from a triglyceride.
In a phospholipid, one of the three fatty acids present in a triglyceride is replaced by a phosphate-containing group, so the molecule has two fatty acids plus a phosphate group instead of three fatty acids.
❓ A student writes that a phospholipid "contains phosphorus instead of a fatty acid." Explain why this wording would not gain full credit.
The substituent is specifically a phosphate group (phosphorus bonded to oxygen atoms), not the bare element phosphorus; "phosphorus" alone is not accepted as the name of the replacing group.
❓ State which elements are present in a phospholipid, and name one element found in most other biological macromolecules that is absent.
A phospholipid contains carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and phosphorus; nitrogen, found in proteins and nucleic acids, is absent.

6 · Properties from structure

❓ Compare and contrast the structure of a triglyceride and a phospholipid.
Both are formed from a glycerol backbone joined by ester bonds and both may contain saturated or unsaturated fatty acids, but a triglyceride has three fatty acids and is hydrophobic throughout, whereas a phospholipid has two fatty acids plus a hydrophilic phosphate group, giving it both hydrophilic and hydrophobic regions.
❓ Explain why phospholipids, but not triglycerides, spontaneously form a bilayer in water.
A phospholipid has a hydrophilic phosphate head and hydrophobic fatty-acid tails, so in water the heads orientate towards the water and the tails away from it on both sides, forming a bilayer; a triglyceride is hydrophobic throughout with no hydrophilic region, so it simply forms an oil droplet instead.
❓ Explain why a lipid-soluble hormone is absorbed into a cell by simple diffusion rather than via a membrane protein.
The hormone is soluble in the hydrophobic phospholipid bilayer, so it can diffuse directly through the membrane down its concentration gradient without needing a carrier or channel protein; the rate of uptake being proportional to concentration, rather than levelling off, is evidence that diffusion, not a carrier-mediated process, is occurring.

7 · Digestion & absorption

❓ Describe how a triglyceride is digested and absorbed in the small intestine.
Bile salts emulsify the lipid into droplets, increasing the surface area for lipase to act on, so triglycerides are hydrolysed faster; lipase hydrolyses the ester bonds between fatty acid and glycerol. Micelles, containing bile salts and fatty acids/monoglycerides, make the fatty acids more soluble in water and carry them to the ileum epithelial cells. Fatty acids and monoglycerides are absorbed by simple diffusion; inside the cells, triglycerides are re-formed and combined with protein to form chylomicrons, which move to the cell membrane in vesicles and are released by exocytosis.
❓ A student states that "micelles increase the surface area available for lipase." Explain why this is incorrect.
Increasing surface area for lipase is the role of the lipid droplets formed by emulsification with bile salts, not the micelles; micelles form later in the pathway and instead increase the solubility of fatty acids in water and deliver them to the epithelial cells of the ileum.
❓ Explain why fatty acids and monoglycerides cross the ileum epithelial cell membrane without needing a transport protein.
Fatty acids and monoglycerides are lipid-soluble, so they can diffuse directly through the hydrophobic phospholipid bilayer of the cell membrane by simple diffusion, down their concentration gradient, without requiring a carrier or channel protein.

8 · The emulsion test

❓ Describe the emulsion test for lipids, including the correct sequence of steps.
Add ethanol (or another alcohol) to the sample and shake, then add water (or pour the ethanol mixture into water); a white or milky emulsion forming shows that lipid is present. The sequence must be correct — ethanol before water — and the mixture must not be heated.
❓ A student heats their sample during the emulsion test and describes the result as "a white precipitate." Identify two errors in this method or description.
The sample should not be heated during the test — heating disqualifies the method mark; and the correct term for the positive result is an emulsion, not a precipitate.
❓ Describe how you would test an unknown solid food sample for the presence of lipid, non-reducing sugar and protein in the same investigation.
For lipid: crush/grind the sample, add ethanol, then add water and shake — a white/milky emulsion is positive. For non-reducing sugar: if Benedict's test on the unheated sample stays blue, boil a fresh sample with acid, neutralise with alkali, then heat with Benedict's reagent — a red/orange precipitate is positive. For protein: add biuret reagent — a purple/violet/mauve/lilac colour is positive. All three tests must be carried out and reported, or the maximum mark available is capped.
