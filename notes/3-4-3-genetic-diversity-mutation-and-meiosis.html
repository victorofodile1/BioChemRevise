Genetic Diversity, Mutation and Meiosis

How brand-new alleles arise by mutation, and how meiosis reshuffles the alleles that already exist into new combinations — the two nuclear divisions, independent segregation, crossing over, and the random-fertilisation maths that ties it all together.

⚠️

The one distinction that costs the most marks on this whole spec point: only mutation ever produces a genuinely new allele. Independent segregation, crossing over and random fertilisation only ever produce new combinations of alleles that already existed — mixing this up is treated as a seriously costly error. A close second: it is meiosis I, not meiosis II, that halves the chromosome number — meiosis II only separates sister chromatids, exactly like mitosis, and changes the chromosome number not at all.

Section 1 · spec content point

🧬 Gene mutation and the frameshift effect

Two ways a single mistake in the DNA can happen, and one crucial reason they behave completely differently — this section is where "define and explain" questions live, and where a huge amount rides on precise, complete wording.

📖

What the specification says: "Gene mutations involve a change in the base sequence of chromosomes. They can arise spontaneously during DNA replication and include base deletion and base substitution. Due to the degenerate nature of the genetic code, not all base substitutions cause a change in the sequence of encoded amino acids. Mutagenic agents can increase the rate of gene mutation."

1
2
3

DNA is like a recipe written in three-letter words with no spaces between them. If you swap just one letter for another (a substitution), you usually only wreck one word — and sometimes not even that, because several different three-letter "spellings" mean exactly the same thing. But if you delete a letter, or sneak an extra one in, every single word after that point shifts along by one space — the whole rest of the recipe turns to gibberish. That's called a frameshift, and it's why deletions and insertions are so much more damaging than substitutions. A mutagen — like UV light or a chemical in cigarette smoke — doesn't choose which typo happens; it just makes typos happen more often overall.

  • Mutagen = something that raises the chance of a mistake happening, without deciding what the mistake is
  • Frameshift = everything after the mistake reads wrong, because it's all shifted along by one

A gene mutation is a change to the base sequence of DNA, and it can happen spontaneously — most often as a copying error during DNA replication. There are two kinds to keep separate. A substitution swaps one base for a different one, so it can only ever affect the one triplet (codon) it sits in — and because the genetic code is degenerate (several different triplets can code for the same amino acid), it sometimes changes nothing at all. A deletion or insertion removes or adds a single base, which shifts every triplet after that point along by one — a frameshift — so the amino acid sequence downstream of the mutation is completely different, not just one amino acid. A mutagenic agent, such as ionising radiation or chemicals in tobacco smoke, only increases the rate at which mutations happen; it never decides which mutation occurs — mutations stay random.

Defining a gene mutation, and why it might do nothing at all

A gene mutation is a change in the base (nucleotide) sequence of the DNA making up a chromosome. By definition, that change produces a new allele of the gene involved. Mutations happen spontaneously, most often as random errors during DNA replication.

A full answer explaining why a mutation might have no effect needs to draw on genuinely separate reasons, not restatements of the same idea:

  • The genetic code is degenerate — several different triplets can code for the same amino acid — so a base substitution sometimes swaps a triplet for a different one that still specifies the identical amino acid.
  • The mutation might fall within an intron, a non-coding section of the gene that gets removed before translation, so it never reaches the amino acid sequence at all.
  • Even where an amino acid does change, that single substitution might have no effect on the protein's tertiary structure — the fold, and therefore the function, stays the same.
  • If the new allele produced is recessive, it won't be expressed in a heterozygous phenotype.

Occasionally a mutation is positively useful: a changed amino acid can alter a protein's properties in a way that improves it — its stability, or its affinity for a substrate, for instance — which can increase an organism's chances of survival or reproductive success. This is the raw material that natural selection ultimately acts on (developed further in 3.4.4).

Substitution vs deletion/insertion — the frameshift effect

TypeWhat happensEffect on the polypeptide
SubstitutionOne base is replaced by another.Affects only the one triplet it falls in, so at most one amino acid changes — often none, since the code is degenerate.
Deletion or insertionA base is removed, or an extra base is added.Causes a frameshift — every triplet after the mutation is shifted along, so the amino acid sequence from that point on is very different.
Generic base letters are used deliberately — the specification does not require recall of specific codons or the amino acids they code for. What matters is the reading-frame consequence: one base swapped changes at most one triplet; one base removed or added shifts every triplet downstream.
🚩

A strong "define and explain" answer needs to draw at least one point from each of three separate ideas — the definition itself, a reason a mutation may have no effect, and a reason it may have a positive effect — rather than several points from just one of those three. A named type of mutation is an acceptable way to describe "a change," and "polypeptide," "amino acid sequence" and "protein" can all be used interchangeably.

Identifying the mutation type from a changed amino acid sequence

If you're given two versions of the same polypeptide's amino acid sequence and asked what type of mutation produced the difference, count how many amino acids changed. A single altered amino acid points to a substitution — only one triplet is affected, and even that might not be enough to matter, since the code is degenerate. Multiple amino acids changing from one point onwards points to a deletion or insertion, since a frameshift alters every triplet — and so every amino acid — downstream of the change.

Mutagenic agents — raising the rate, never choosing the mutation

A mutagenic agent (mutagen) is any factor that increases the rate at which mutations occur — examples include ionising radiation such as X-rays, UV and gamma rays, and chemicals such as those found in tobacco smoke.

🚩

Mutations themselves are spontaneous and random — a mutagen never "causes" or "directs" one specific mutation to happen. It only makes mutations, in general, more likely across the genome. This is the exact same causation trap examiner reports flag for antibiotic resistance later in 3.4.4: the environment does not decide which mutation occurs.

Tracing a mutation's consequence all the way to the phenotype

Build this as one unbroken chain, not a jump straight from "DNA" to "no protein": altering the base sequence changes the sequence of amino acids coded for in the primary structure of the polypeptide; that altered primary structure can change how the polypeptide folds, altering its tertiary structure; and an altered tertiary structure can stop the protein working — for an enzyme, its active site may no longer be complementary to its substrate, while for a receptor it's the binding site that's affected, since receptors don't have an active site at all.

🚩

The idea of sequence is essential — "makes different amino acids" on its own, with no reference to sequence, is not a complete answer. Don't just name the mutation type ("a deletion occurred") when a question asks for the effect of the mutation. And keep active site (enzymes) and binding site (receptors) strictly separate — this mix-up shows up even in otherwise strong answers.

Section 2 · spec content point

🧩 Chromosome mutations — non-disjunction

A different scale of mistake entirely: not a change within a gene, but whole chromosomes failing to separate correctly. Short to state, but heavily tested on precise cause-and-consequence wording.

📖

What the specification says: "Mutations in the number of chromosomes can arise spontaneously by chromosome non-disjunction during meiosis."

1
2
3

Imagine dealing a deck of cards evenly between two players, one matching pair of cards at a time. Non-disjunction is like accidentally handing one player both cards of a pair, and the other player neither — one hand ends up one card too many, the other one card too few. In biology, if that "too many" hand ends up being a baby's set of chromosomes, it can cause a condition like Down's syndrome.

  • Gamete = a sex cell (sperm or egg)
  • Zygote = the cell formed when two gametes fuse at fertilisation

Normally, meiosis shares chromosomes out evenly — one of each matching pair into each gamete. Non-disjunction is when that sharing-out goes wrong: a pair fails to separate, so one gamete ends up with both chromosomes of that pair and another gamete ends up with neither. The result is two gametes with the wrong chromosome number — one has an extra chromosome, one is missing one. If a gamete like that is fertilised, every cell in the resulting embryo carries that same abnormal chromosome number — an extra chromosome 21, for example, causes Down's syndrome.

Non-disjunction is the name for chromosomes failing to separate properly during meiosis. Instead of moving apart to opposite poles as usual, a homologous pair (or, if it happens at the second division, a pair of sister chromatids) stays together and both copies end up in the same daughter cell.

🚩

This is specifically a meiosis event on this spec point — leave mitosis out of the explanation entirely. Reference to the first or second meiotic division is enough to indicate "meiosis"; "move to one side" or "move to one pole" is an acceptable way to describe chromosomes not separating.

The consequence is a pair of gametes with unequal chromosome numbers, not absent ones: one gamete gains a chromosome too many, and the other loses one it should have had.

🚩

A commonly reported misconception is that non-disjunction leaves a daughter cell with no chromosomes at all. The correct idea is unequal numbers — one gamete with one chromosome too many, one with one too few.

If a gamete produced by non-disjunction is fertilised, the resulting zygote carries an abnormal chromosome number in every one of its cells — the standard example is an extra copy of chromosome 21, causing Down's syndrome. If a whole chromosome set fails to separate, rather than just one pair, the result is polyploidy instead: the zygote gains one or more entire extra sets of chromosomes.

Section 3 · spec content point

🔬 Meiosis — the two nuclear divisions

One diploid cell, two divisions, four haploid cells — and every one of the four genetically different from the others. This is the backbone diagram of the whole spec point; get the chromosome-and-chromatid bookkeeping exactly right at each stage.

📖

What the specification says: "Meiosis produces daughter cells that are genetically different from each other. The process of meiosis only in sufficient detail to show how: two nuclear divisions result usually in the formation of four haploid daughter cells from a single diploid parent cell; genetically different daughter cells result from the independent segregation of homologous chromosomes; crossing over between homologous chromosomes results in further genetic variation among daughter cells." (This section covers the outcome and the two divisions themselves; Section 4 covers the independent segregation and crossing over mechanisms named in the same content point.)

1
2
3

Imagine four pairs of shoes, one shoe from mum and one from dad in every pair. Round one: sort them so each new box gets only one shoe from each pair — you've now halved the number of shoes per box. Round two: cut each remaining shoe in half lengthways, and share the two halves into two more new boxes. You end up with four boxes, each holding half a shoe from every original pair — and because of how the sorting happened, no two boxes match.

  • Diploid = has chromosomes in matching pairs (one full set from each parent)
  • Haploid = has only one chromosome from each pair (half the full set)

Meiosis is cell division that makes gametes, and it happens in two rounds. Round one (meiosis I) separates matching (homologous) chromosome pairs, cutting the chromosome number in half straight away. Round two (meiosis II) then separates the two identical copies (sister chromatids) of each chromosome, much like an ordinary cell division (mitosis) — it doesn't halve the number again. The end result is four cells, each with half the original chromosome number, and every one of the four genetically different from the others.

Meiosis I — the reduction division

Homologous chromosomes pair up (synapsis) and line up together at the equator; whole homologous chromosomes are then pulled to opposite poles, so each of the two resulting cells ends up with only one member of each homologous pair. This is what actually halves the chromosome number, which is why meiosis I is sometimes called the reduction division. Crucially, each chromosome still consists of two sister chromatids at this point — nothing has separated those yet.

Meiosis II — just like mitosis

Each of the two cells produced by meiosis I now carries out a second division, independently and at the same time. This time it's the sister chromatids of each chromosome that are pulled apart to opposite poles — mechanically, this is exactly the same event as an ordinary mitotic division. Because the chromosome number was already halved in meiosis I, meiosis II does not change the chromosome number again — it only separates chromatids.

⚠️

The single most consistently examined boundary on this spec point: "homologous chromosomes separate" is meiosis-I-only vocabulary and scores nothing if written for meiosis II; "sister chromatids separate" alone is an incomplete description of meiosis I (it is homologous chromosomes, each still carrying 2 chromatids, that separate at meiosis I — sister chromatids only separate at meiosis II, exactly as in mitosis).

The result: four haploid, genetically different daughter cells

Two nuclear divisions in a row produce four haploid daughter cells from a single diploid parent cell. All four are genetically different from each other — not just different from the parent cell — because of the independent segregation and crossing over covered in Section 4.

Meiosis I separates the homologous pairs (red vs blue; amber vs teal) into Cell A and Cell B — each chromosome shown here as a double bar is still two sister chromatids joined at a centromere. Meiosis II then separates those chromatids (single bars), giving four haploid cells, all genetically different from one another.

Spotting meiosis in an unfamiliar life cycle

If you're shown an unfamiliar life cycle and asked to identify which division is meiotic, don't rely on recognising named stages. Instead, track the chromosome number all the way through the cycle and find the single point where it halves — that division is meiosis, wherever it happens to sit and whatever the organism is.

Drawing chromosome content correctly after each division

After meiosis I: two cells, each with half the chromosome number, but every chromosome still made of two chromatids. After meiosis II: four cells, still with half the chromosome number, but every chromosome is now a single chromatid.

🚩

The commonest diagram error nationally is drawing single-chromatid chromosomes straight after the first division — chromosomes are still 2-chromatid structures immediately after meiosis I, right up until meiosis II actually separates them.

Section 4 · spec content point

🔀 Independent segregation and crossing over

The two mechanisms inside meiosis that shuffle alleles into new combinations — one shuffles whole chromosomes between gametes, the other shuffles alleles within a single chromosome. Neither ever invents a new allele.

📖

What the specification says: "Meiosis produces daughter cells that are genetically different from each other. The process of meiosis only in sufficient detail to show how: two nuclear divisions result usually in the formation of four haploid daughter cells from a single diploid parent cell; genetically different daughter cells result from the independent segregation of homologous chromosomes; crossing over between homologous chromosomes results in further genetic variation among daughter cells." (Same content point as Section 3 — this half covers the two named shuffling mechanisms.)

1
2
3

Independent segregation is like flipping a separate coin for every pair of socks in a drawer — heads, the left sock goes in bag A; tails, it goes in bag B — and doing this separately for every single pair, so you end up with a random mix of sock-pairs in each bag.

Crossing over is like swapping one page between two nearly identical notebooks lying open side by side. Afterwards, each notebook is a new mix of the two originals — but no new page, no new letter or word, was ever invented. It was just moved.

Two separate shuffling tricks happen during meiosis I. First, each matching chromosome pair lines up randomly at the equator, independently of every other pair — so which parent's copy ends up in a given daughter cell is basically down to chance (independent segregation). Second, chromosomes swap chunks with their paired partner while they're lined up together — the touching strands break and rejoin at a point called a chiasma, trading alleles between them (crossing over). Neither trick invents a new allele — they just remix the alleles that were already there.

Independent segregation

During metaphase I, each homologous pair lines up at the equator independently of every other pair — the orientation of one pair has no bearing on how any other pair orientates. Because that orientation is random, which member of each pair ends up in a given daughter cell is also random, producing many different combinations of maternal and paternal chromosomes among the resulting gametes.

🚩

Use the specification's own term — independent segregation (or independent assortment) — rather than a vaguer word like "shuffling," which isn't credited. Always state explicitly that it is homologous pairs whose orientation is independent, not just "chromosomes" in general.

Crossing over

  1. While homologous chromosomes are paired up, their non-sister chromatids — one chromatid from each of the two homologous chromosomes, not two chromatids of the same chromosome — twist around one another.
  2. The tension this creates makes sections of the chromatids break, and each broken section rejoins onto the chromatid it's now overlapping with, at a point called a chiasma.
  3. Because the two swapped sections come from different homologous chromosomes, this exchanges alleles between them.
  4. The result is new combinations of alleles on the same chromosome — one that started out entirely maternal, for instance, can end up carrying a mix of maternal and paternal alleles.
Only the two touching, non-sister chromatids (middle pair) exchange material at the chiasma — the outer sister chromatids (AB and ab, left and right) are untouched. Ab and aB are new combinations of alleles that already existed; no new allele (A, a, B or b) is created by this process.
⚠️

The single most costly mix-up on this spec point: crossing over and independent segregation produce new combinations of existing alleles, not new alleles — only mutation makes a genuinely new allele. Getting this the wrong way round is treated as a particularly costly error. Also keep the mechanism itself precise: it is non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes that cross over, never sister chromatids of the same chromosome, and it is chromatid sections breaking and rejoining, not chromosome segments physically "snapping off" under tension.

✅

Four things worth being able to say correctly, every time: "Meiosis I separates homologous chromosomes and halves the chromosome number; meiosis II separates sister chromatids, like mitosis." · "Crossing over occurs between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes, exchanging alleles at a chiasma." · "Crossing over produces new combinations of alleles, not new alleles — only mutation makes new alleles." · "Use the term 'independent segregation', never 'shuffling'."

Section 5 · bringing it together

🧮 Random fertilisation and the maths skill

The third source of new allele combinations, and the one maths skill named directly in the specification — worth learning as its own small, self-contained routine.

After meiosis, random fertilisation is the random fusion of any one male gamete with any one female gamete. Because any gamete can fuse with any other, and each gamete produced by meiosis is already genetically different from every other gamete (thanks to independent segregation and crossing over), random fertilisation multiplies the variation meiosis has already generated.

The two formulas

QuantityFormula
Different chromosome combinations possible in a gamete, from independent segregation alone (ignoring crossing over)2ⁿ
Different chromosome combinations possible in the zygote, after random fertilisation of two such gametes(2ⁿ)² = 2²ⁿ (i.e. 2ⁿ × 2ⁿ)

Worked example: in humans, n = 23, so 2²³ ≈ 8.4 million chromosome combinations are possible per gamete, and (2²³)² ≈ 7 × 10¹³ combinations are possible in the zygote — and that's before crossing over is even taken into account.

🚩

n is the number of homologous pairs, not the total number of chromosomes — always check whether a question has given you n or 2n before starting the calculation; this is the single most common source of numerical error on this maths skill. It's worth showing the value of n itself as part of your working, not just a final answer, so a slipped power still earns partial credit.

🧭

How to avoid the commonest error: underline or circle whatever the question gives you first. If it's 2n (the total diploid number), halve it to get n before raising 2 to that power.

Putting all four sources of variation together

Crossing over makes each individual chromosome a new combination of alleles; independent segregation then produces 2ⁿ combinations of those chromosomes in a gamete; and random fertilisation squares that number again in the zygote. Add mutation, which is the only one of the four that supplies genuinely new alleles in the first place, and the genetic diversity available to a sexually reproducing population becomes, for practical purposes, limitless — the raw material natural selection then acts on.

✅

If a question asks where genetic diversity in a population comes from, a complete answer names all four mechanisms distinctly — mutation, independent segregation, crossing over, and random fertilisation — rather than treating them as one blurred process.

Section 6 · exam technique

🎯 Common mistakes & precise wording

The individual facts on this spec point are short, but they're examined relentlessly on exact terminology — these are the slips that come up again and again.

Vague or incorrect versionWhat to say instead

"Crossing over / independent segregation creates new alleles"They create new combinations of existing alleles — only mutation creates a genuinely new allele

▶ Where the difficulty on this spec point actually comes from

The content itself is a modest memorisation load — four content sub-points and one formula pair — but the misconception rate and terminology precision required are both high. A large share of marks are lost not from not knowing the biology, but from confusing which mechanism is responsible for which effect: attributing the chromosome-number halving to the wrong division, describing crossing over between the wrong type of chromatid, or confusing "new alleles" with "new combinations of existing alleles." Applying the "chromosome number halves" rule to an unfamiliar life cycle is the hardest single application skill here, since it demands reading an unfamiliar diagram rather than recalling a definition.

Section 7 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

This content supports more than one essay theme — the trick each time is closing the paragraph with a sentence that ties the mechanism back to whatever the title is actually about, rather than repeating a generic summary.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around movement

In meiosis I, homologous chromosomes pair and are pulled to opposite poles by independent segregation; in meiosis II, sister chromatids separate. Crossing over sees non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes twist around each other, break and rejoin at a chiasma, exchanging alleles. These are further examples of directed chromosome movement, this time producing genetically different daughter cells — extending the "movement of chromosomes" theme from mitosis to show how controlled intracellular movement generates the variation that natural selection, and ultimately survival, depends on.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around mutation as the ultimate source of variation

Mutation produces new alleles; independent segregation, crossing over and random fertilisation during meiosis then produce new combinations of those existing alleles — mutation is the ultimate source, and meiosis multiplies its effects combinatorially. Chromosome mutations, such as non-disjunction during meiosis, give gametes with an abnormal chromosome number, producing conditions such as Down's syndrome after fertilisation. Mutation is important as the single source of all new genetic variation in a population — without it, meiosis would only ever reshuffle a fixed, unchanging set of alleles.

Whichever essay theme this content supports, keep the mutation/meiosis distinction explicit — state which mechanism is doing the work (a new allele vs a new combination) every time you use this material, since blurring the two is the single most cited error connected to this spec point.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Gene mutation & the frameshift effect

  • A gene mutation = a change in the base sequence of DNA, producing a new allele; happens spontaneously, usually during DNA replication.
  • Substitution changes only one triplet, so at most one amino acid changes — often none, because the code is degenerate.
  • Deletion/insertion causes a frameshift: every triplet from that point on is altered, so the whole downstream amino acid sequence changes.
  • A mutagenic agent (e.g. ionising radiation, chemicals in tobacco smoke) only increases the rate of mutation — never causes or directs a specific mutation.
  • Full causal chain: altered base sequence → altered amino acid sequence (primary structure) → altered tertiary structure → protein no longer functions (active site, or binding site for a receptor, no longer complementary).

2 · Chromosome mutations — non-disjunction

  • Non-disjunction = chromosomes failing to separate properly during meiosis, so both members of a pair end up in the same cell.
  • Produces gametes with unequal chromosome numbers — one too many, one too few — never a gamete with zero chromosomes.
  • After fertilisation, the zygote has an abnormal chromosome number in every cell, e.g. trisomy 21 (extra chromosome 21) causing Down's syndrome.
  • If a whole chromosome set fails to separate, the result is polyploidy instead.

3 · Meiosis — the two divisions

  • Two nuclear divisions produce four haploid daughter cells from one diploid parent cell — all four genetically different from each other.
  • Meiosis I: homologous chromosomes pair up and separate — this halves the chromosome number (the reduction division); each chromosome still has 2 chromatids.
  • Meiosis II: sister chromatids separate, exactly like mitosis — no further change in chromosome number.
  • To spot meiosis in an unfamiliar life cycle: find the point where the chromosome number halves.
  • Draw the chromatids: chromosomes are still 2-chromatid straight after meiosis I, only single-chromatid after meiosis II.

4 · Independent segregation & crossing over

  • Independent segregation: each homologous pair orientates randomly at the equator, independently of every other pair — produces many combinations of maternal/paternal chromosomes. Use this exact term, not "shuffling."
  • Crossing over: non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes twist together, break and rejoin at a chiasma, exchanging alleles.
  • Both produce new COMBINATIONS of existing alleles — never new alleles. Only mutation makes a new allele; mixing this up is flagged as a costly error.
  • Number of gamete chromosome combinations from independent segregation alone = 2ⁿ, where n = number of homologous pairs (squared again by random fertilisation).

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Gene mutation & the frameshift effect

❓ Define a gene mutation, and give two distinct reasons why a base substitution might have no effect on the phenotype.
A gene mutation is a change in the base/nucleotide sequence of the DNA/chromosomes, resulting in the formation of a new allele. It may have no effect because the genetic code is degenerate, so more than one triplet can code for the same amino acid and the amino acid sequence may not change; or because even if an amino acid does change, this has no effect on the tertiary structure of the protein.
❓ A student compares two versions of the same polypeptide and finds that only one amino acid differs between them. What type of mutation most likely produced this, and why?
A base substitution — because it affects only the single triplet in which it occurs, so at most one amino acid changes, and often none, since the code is degenerate. An insertion or deletion would instead cause a frameshift, altering every amino acid coded for from that point onwards, not just one.
❓ Explain why a mutagenic agent cannot be said to "cause" a particular mutation to occur.
Mutations are spontaneous, random events; a mutagenic agent such as ionising radiation or a chemical in tobacco smoke only increases the rate at which mutations occur across the genome — it does not direct or determine which specific mutation happens.

Chromosome mutations — non-disjunction

❓ Name and explain a mutation in which chromosomes fail to separate correctly during meiosis, and state its consequence for a resulting zygote.
Non-disjunction: during meiosis, a homologous pair (or a pair of sister chromatids) fails to separate, so both move into the same cell instead of into different cells; this produces gametes with one chromosome too many and one chromosome too few. After fertilisation, the zygote has an abnormal chromosome number in every cell — for example, an extra chromosome 21 causes Down's syndrome.
❓ A student states that non-disjunction leaves one of the resulting cells with no chromosomes at all. Correct this statement.
This is incorrect — non-disjunction produces gametes with unequal, not absent, chromosome numbers: one gamete gains one extra chromosome, and the other loses one, but neither ends up with zero.

Meiosis — the two divisions

❓ Describe the outcome of meiosis, and state which of the two divisions is responsible for halving the chromosome number.
Meiosis consists of two nuclear divisions producing four haploid daughter cells from a single diploid parent cell, all genetically different from each other. Meiosis I halves the chromosome number, because homologous chromosomes pair up and are then separated into different cells; meiosis II separates the sister chromatids of each chromosome, exactly as in mitosis, and does not change the chromosome number further.
❓ A diagram of an unfamiliar organism's life cycle shows a diploid cell producing cells with half the chromosome number at one particular stage. Explain how you would identify which division this is, without needing to know the names used in the diagram.
Look for the single point in the cycle where the chromosome number halves — that division is meiosis, regardless of what stage it is labelled or where in the life cycle it occurs; every other division that keeps the chromosome number the same is mitosis.
❓ State what a diagram of a cell immediately after the first meiotic division should show about each chromosome, and explain the commonest error made here.
Each of the two resulting cells should have half the original chromosome number, but every chromosome should still be shown as two sister chromatids joined at a centromere — only meiosis II separates the chromatids. The commonest error is drawing single-chromatid chromosomes straight after meiosis I, before they have actually been separated.

Independent segregation & crossing over

❓ Explain how independent segregation of homologous chromosomes during meiosis I contributes to genetic variation.
Each homologous pair lines up at the equator in a random orientation, independently of how every other pair orientates; this means which member of each pair (maternal or paternal) passes into a given daughter cell is random, so many different combinations of whole maternal and paternal chromosomes are produced in the resulting gametes.
❓ Explain the process of crossing over, and state precisely what type of chromatid is involved.
Homologous chromosomes pair up and their non-sister chromatids (not sister chromatids of the same chromosome) twist around each other; tension causes sections of the chromatids to break and rejoin with the chromatid of the other homologous chromosome, at a point called a chiasma; this exchanges alleles between the homologous chromosomes, producing new combinations of alleles on the same chromosome.
❓ A student writes that crossing over creates new alleles. Explain why this is incorrect, and state what crossing over actually produces.
This is incorrect — crossing over does not create any new alleles; the alleles being exchanged (e.g. A, a, B and b) were already present on the homologous chromosomes before the chiasma formed. Crossing over produces new combinations of existing alleles on the same chromosome; only mutation creates a genuinely new allele.
