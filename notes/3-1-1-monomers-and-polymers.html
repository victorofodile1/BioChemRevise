Monomers and Polymers

The six ideas every biological-molecules topic is built on: what a monomer and a polymer actually are, the three families of monomer named on the spec, and how condensation and hydrolysis join and break them apart. Short on paper, but it resurfaces in almost every other topic in the course.

⚠️

The single habit that protects the most marks on this spec point: never write "building blocks" for a monomer — the credited definition is a smaller, repeating unit or molecule that larger molecules are made from. And never reach for a triglyceride or phospholipid as your monomer→polymer example: both are made by condensation, but neither is a polymer, because neither is built from a repeating monomer. Get those two habits right and almost every mark on this page is yours.

Section 1 · spec content point

🧬 Why every living thing shares the same chemistry

This idea almost never gets a question to itself — but it's the reason condensation and hydrolysis matter everywhere else in the course, and it quietly supplies the synoptic evidence base for evolution.

📖

What the specification says: "The variety of life, both past and present, is extensive, but the biochemical basis of life is similar for all living things."

1
2
3

Imagine every living thing on Earth — a mushroom, an elephant, the bacteria living in your gut — being built out of exactly the same set of chemical "ingredients," just mixed and shaped in different ways. That's basically true. Because everything shares the same ingredients and the same genetic instruction-language, scientists can compare two species' instructions (their DNA) and work out how closely related they are: the more alike the instructions, the more recently the two species split from a shared ancestor.

Mini-glossary: common ancestor — a species that two different, later species both evolved from, further back in time.

All living things — animals, plants, fungi, bacteria, even viruses — are built from the same handful of chemical families, made and broken down by the same two reactions. That shared chemistry is used as evidence for evolution: species whose DNA (or protein) sequences are more alike shared a common ancestor more recently than species whose sequences are very different from one another.

Millions of different species exist, yet zoom into any of their cells and you find the same small set of carbon-based chemical families doing the work — carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids — all built and broken down by exactly the same two reactions covered later on this page: condensation and hydrolysis. Every organism on Earth, right down to viruses, also shares the same genetic code. This shared chemistry is not treated as a standalone fact to recall; it is used as synoptic evidence for evolution.

The logic to apply whenever this idea resurfaces: the more similar the DNA base sequence — or the amino-acid sequence of a shared protein, such as cytochrome c — between two species, the more closely related those species are, and the more recently they shared a common ancestor. Close similarity signals a recent split; large differences signal a distant one.

  • Carried forward directly into 3.4.4 Genetic diversity and 3.4.5 Species and taxonomy, where comparing base or amino-acid sequences is literally how relatedness between species gets measured.
  • Also underpins 3.8 Gene technologies — a shared genetic code across species is what makes technologies such as recombinant DNA possible in the first place.
📌

Treat this section as background, not a recall target on its own. If it appears in a question, it will be dressed up as an evolution, classification or gene-technology question — the mark comes from applying the "more similar sequence = more recent common ancestor" rule, not from reciting this section verbatim.

Section 2 · spec content point

🔹 Monomers

One two-part definition carries almost every mark on this section — learn it precisely enough that you could recite it half asleep, without ever reaching for the word "building block."

📖

What the specification says: "Monomers are the smaller units from which larger molecules are made."

1
2
3

Think of a monomer as a single Lego brick. On its own it's small and not that interesting — but click loads of the same-shaped bricks together and you get something much bigger and more impressive: a tower, a wall, a whole model. In biology, that "whole model" built from repeating bricks is called a polymer.

Mini-glossary: unit — one single, complete piece; repeating — the same type of piece used again and again to build something bigger.

A monomer is a small, repeating unit that larger molecules — polymers — are built from. Two things always need to be in your answer: that it's a smaller, repeating unit, and that bigger molecules are made from it. Don't call it an "atom," an "element," or a "building block" — despite sounding correct, none of those earn credit.

A monomer is a smaller, repeating unit or molecule from which larger molecules — polymers — are made. Two separate ideas have to be present for this to count as a complete definition: (i) it is a smaller, repeating unit, and (ii) larger molecules are built from it. Miss either half and the definition is incomplete, even if the wording otherwise sounds right.

▶ 🚩 Trap — words that sound right but score nothing

Calling a monomer an "atom" or an "element" confuses the level of biology being described — a monomer is a molecule or unit, not a single atom. And "building block," while intuitively close, is specifically not credited: it doesn't capture that the unit is smaller and repeating, or that larger molecules are specifically made from it. Write out the full two-part definition instead.

Beyond the definition itself, examiners like to test whether you can recognise a monomer inside an unfamiliar diagram or passage — typically by giving several labelled structures alongside a list of descriptive clues, and asking you to match each clue to the correct structure. Clues might point to a monomer indirectly: a description of where it's found (an amino acid in an enzyme's active site), what it forms when two of it condense together (two α-glucose molecules forming maltose), or what test it gives a positive result for (an amino acid giving a positive biuret test, because biuret detects the peptide bonds in a protein). The skill being tested isn't memorising exact clues — it's holding the four monomer families from Section 4 clearly enough in your head to reason out an unfamiliar one.

📌

Marking habit worth building now: work through every clue in a matching-style question methodically, on its own evidence, rather than guessing by elimination. The same answer can be correct for more than one clue, or not needed at all — ruling an option out too early is one of the most common ways marks are lost here, even when the underlying knowledge is solid.

Section 3 · spec content point

🔗 Polymers

Being made by condensation is not the same as being a polymer — this single distinction is where most of the marks on this spec point are actually lost.

📖

What the specification says: "Polymers are molecules made from a large number of monomers joined together."

1
2
3

A polymer is what you get once you've clicked together loads and loads of the same Lego brick — not just two. Two bricks stuck together doesn't really count as "a tower" yet. Here's the twist: something can be built the "condensation way" — squeezing out water as it's glued together — without actually being a proper repeating tower, like gluing one red brick to one blue brick just the once. That's not a polymer, even though it was made in the same way.

Mini-glossary: dimer — a molecule made of just two monomers joined together, too small to count as a polymer; polymerisation — the process of forming a polymer.

A polymer is a molecule built from a large number of monomers joined together — "large number" is the key phrase, since two joined monomers (a dimer) isn't enough to count. To spot a polymer in a diagram, look for a pattern that clearly repeats, not just two linked units on their own.

Here's the trap: being made by condensation doesn't automatically make something a polymer. DNA and proteins are both polymers because they're built from many repeating monomers. ATP and phospholipids are also made by condensation — but neither is a polymer, because neither is built from a repeating chain of the same monomer.

A polymer is a molecule made from a large number of monomers joined together, usually by condensation reactions; the process of forming one is called polymerisation. The phrase "large number" is doing real work here — a molecule built from just two monomers (a dimer) does not count as a polymer.

If you're shown a diagram and asked what evidence shows a molecule is a polymer, point to a repeating pattern — repeating units, monomers, or molecules. Showing two linked units on their own is not evidence of a polymer; you have to demonstrate that the pattern actually repeats.

⚠️

The trap examiners set again and again: "formed by condensation" is a much broader category than "is a polymer." A named enzyme (many amino acids) and DNA (many nucleotides) genuinely are polymers. But ATP — a single nucleotide derivative with three phosphate groups, not a repeating chain — is not a polymer, despite being assembled by condensation. A phospholipid is not a polymer either: it's held together by ester bonds, but there's no repeating monomer running through it. Reason each molecule through the same test every time: does it contain a large number of the same repeating monomer, or not?

Section 4 · spec content point

📋 Examples of monomers — the three named families

Every carbohydrate and protein question in this course eventually comes back to this one table — learn it so it's automatic, and never let a lipid sneak into it.

📖

What the specification says: "Monosaccharides, amino acids and nucleotides are examples of monomers."

1
2
3

Picture three different toy sets, each with its own connector piece. Amino-acid pieces click together with a "peptide" connector to build proteins. Nucleotide pieces click together with a "phosphodiester" connector to build DNA. And two slightly different glucose pieces — α and β — both click together with a "glycosidic" connector, but the α version builds a coiled-up energy store (starch), while the β version builds long, straight fibres (cellulose) — like the difference between a coiled phone cord and a straight ruler.

Mini-glossary: isomer — two molecules with exactly the same atoms, just arranged slightly differently; biuret test — a colour-change test that shows protein is present, turning the sample purple/lilac.

There are really only three families of monomer on this spec point. Amino acids build proteins, using peptide bonds. Nucleotides build DNA and RNA, using phosphodiester bonds. And glucose builds carbohydrates — but which type of glucose matters: α-glucose makes starch and glycogen using glycosidic bonds, while β-glucose makes cellulose, also using glycosidic bonds, but in a way that keeps the chain straight instead of coiled. If a question doesn't say exactly which carbohydrate is meant, "monosaccharide" and "polysaccharide" are safe general terms.

Two things to never write here: a triglyceride as an example (it isn't a polymer — see Section 3), or "ester bond" as the bond inside a named polymer.

Amino acidPolypeptide, protein, enzyme, antibodyPeptidePositive biuret test — turns purple/lilac
NucleotidePolynucleotide, DNA, RNAPhosphodiesterPhosphate group of one nucleotide bonds to the sugar of the next

Fallback rule: if a question doesn't name a specific carbohydrate, "monosaccharide/glucose" for the monomer and "polysaccharide" for the polymer are accepted answers.

⚠️

Two answers are always rejected here. A triglyceride is never acceptable as a monomer–polymer example — it's built by condensation, but as Section 3 explains, it isn't a polymer at all. And "ester bond" is never accepted for the "name the bond within this polymer" mark, because lipids aren't polymers in the first place — stick to peptide, glycosidic and phosphodiester bonds for this table.

α-glucose and β-glucose are isomers of each other — the only structural difference is which way the –OH group on carbon 1 points. That single flip is the entire reason starch coils and branches while cellulose runs in long, straight, unbranched chains (the mechanical consequences of this are covered in more depth in 3.1.2 Carbohydrates).

▶ Identifying a monomer from a passage, without extra detail creeping in

If a passage describes a polymer built from a monomer containing an amine group, a carboxyl group and a variable side group, the answer wanted is simply "amino acid" (or "amino acids") — nothing more. Adding extra, unrequested detail to an otherwise correct short-answer response can cause the mark to be withheld, so keep answers like this to exactly what's asked.

Section 5 · spec content point

💧 Condensation reactions

Every named bond in this course — peptide, glycosidic, ester, phosphodiester — is formed in exactly this way, so this three-part definition is worth learning as a single, fixed unit.

📖

What the specification says: "A condensation reaction joins two molecules together with the formation of a chemical bond and involves the elimination of a molecule of water."

1
2
3

Condensation is like gluing two Lego bricks together — except the "glue" here works by squeezing out a tiny drop of water as the two pieces click into place. Every single joint in every biological polymer in this course — proteins, DNA, starch, cellulose, even fats — is made this exact way, by squeezing out water.

Mini-glossary: eliminate / release — to produce and let go of; here, the water molecule is a leftover product, not something that gets used up.

A condensation reaction glues two monomers together: a new chemical bond forms between them, and a water molecule is released as a byproduct. All four named bonds in this course — peptide (between amino acids), glycosidic (between monosaccharides), ester (between glycerol and a fatty acid), and phosphodiester (between nucleotides) — are formed exactly this way.

If a question asks you to work out a formula after several monomers join together, remember that each new bond removes one water molecule — so n monomers joining together lose (n − 1) waters in total.

A condensation reaction joins monomers together, forms a chemical bond between them, and releases (eliminates) a molecule of water. All three ideas need to appear in a full answer — stating only that "water is lost," without also naming the bond that forms, is an incomplete answer.

Condensation joins monomers and releases water; hydrolysis breaks the same bond using water. Every named bond in this course — peptide, glycosidic, ester, phosphodiester — forms and breaks in exactly this way.

Describing how each specific bond is formed

BondWhat to write
PeptideCondensation between amino acids: an –NH₂ (amine) group on one joins a –COOH (carboxyl) group on the other, forming a peptide bond and releasing water, and building up a specific sequence of amino acids.
GlycosidicCondensation between two monosaccharides forms a glycosidic bond, releasing a molecule of water.
EsterCondensation (loss of water) between glycerol and a fatty acid.
PhosphodiesterNucleotides join as phosphodiester bonds form between the phosphate group of one nucleotide and the sugar of the next — catalysed by DNA polymerase during replication, or RNA polymerase during transcription.
▶ 🚩 Trap — misdescribing what DNA/RNA polymerase actually does

Never describe DNA polymerase or RNA polymerase as "forming hydrogen bonds" — that isn't their role in condensation. They catalyse the formation of the covalent phosphodiester bond that links adjacent nucleotides together in the sugar–phosphate backbone.

Working out a molecular formula after condensation

Every condensation reaction removes exactly one water molecule — so joining n monomers together removes (n − 1) water molecules in total.

Worked example: two molecules of α-glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆ each) condense to form maltose. Add the atoms together first: C₁₂H₂₄O₁₂. Then subtract one H₂O for the single glycosidic bond formed: C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁.

🧮

This kind of arithmetic question rewards care, not extra biology knowledge — count the monomers, subtract (n − 1) waters, and double-check your addition before committing to a final formula.

🧪

Where this sits relative to practical skills: no required practical is tied directly to 3.1.1 itself — but every biochemical test you go on to practise (Benedict's, iodine, biuret, the emulsion test, in 3.1.2–3.1.4) works precisely because it detects the product of a specific condensation or hydrolysis reaction. Revisiting this page before those practicals pays off directly.

Section 6 · spec content point

💦 Hydrolysis reactions

Hydrolysis is condensation run in reverse — same bonds, opposite direction, and a water molecule used up instead of released.

📖

What the specification says: "A hydrolysis reaction breaks a chemical bond between two molecules and involves the use of a water molecule."

1
2
3

If condensation is gluing two Lego bricks together by squeezing water out, hydrolysis is the opposite: adding a drop of water back in to pull the two bricks apart again. Your digestive system runs almost entirely on hydrolysis — it's how a meal made of huge molecules gets broken down into pieces small enough to actually absorb.

Mini-glossary: hydro- — a word part meaning "water" (as in hydrolysis: splitting something apart using water).

Hydrolysis is condensation in reverse: instead of releasing water to form a bond, a water molecule is used to break a bond apart. Both parts matter in an answer — which bond breaks, and that water is used to break it.

You'll meet this idea inside digestion contexts especially: peptidases break proteins down into amino acids in stages; triglycerides are hydrolysed in the gut into fatty acids and glycerol; starch is hydrolysed into the smaller, more soluble sugar maltose; and ATP is hydrolysed to release energy for processes like active transport. Whatever the substance, the pattern is identical — name the bond, and say water is used.

A hydrolysis reaction breaks a chemical bond between monomers, using a water molecule to do it. Both ideas need to be present: a bond is broken, and water is used up in the process. If a bond has already been named in the question stem, simply repeating the word "hydrolysis" earns nothing further — the marks are for describing the mechanism, so swap in the correct bond for the context: glycosidic for a carbohydrate, ester for a lipid, peptide for a protein, phosphodiester for a nucleic acid.

A worked example: digesting a protein

Protein digestion is a good model for how hydrolysis gets tested in context. Enzymes called peptidases hydrolyse peptide bonds in stages: endopeptidases break a polypeptide into smaller chains by hydrolysing bonds in the middle of the molecule; exopeptidases then remove amino acids one at a time from the ends of those chains; dipeptidases finish the job, hydrolysing the last remaining dipeptides into single, free amino acids.

Recurring digestion and transport contexts

  • A triglyceride hydrolysed during digestion in the small intestine yields fatty acids and glycerol, which are then carried across the gut lining inside structures called micelles.
  • Starch hydrolysed into maltose produces a smaller, more soluble product, which lowers (makes more negative) the water potential of the surrounding solution — relevant wherever digestion connects to osmosis.
  • Hydrolysis of ATP releases the energy that powers processes such as active transport.

Whatever the context, the underlying answer stays the same: name the bond that's broken, and state that water is used.

Bringing condensation and hydrolysis together

A strong synoptic answer on this spec point states both reactions precisely, then illustrates them with two different monomer–polymer pairs, naming the bond in at least one. For example: "A condensation reaction joins monomers together, forming a chemical bond and releasing a molecule of water; a hydrolysis reaction is the exact reverse, breaking that bond using a molecule of water. Amino acids are the monomers that join by peptide bonds to form a polypeptide, and nucleotides are the monomers that join by phosphodiester bonds to form DNA."

Acceptable monomer–polymer pairs to reach for: amino acid → polypeptide/protein/enzyme/antibody; nucleotide → polynucleotide/DNA/RNA; α-glucose → starch/glycogen; β-glucose → cellulose.

⚠️

Never substitute a triglyceride into an answer like this — it isn't a polymer (Section 3), so it cannot illustrate a monomer-to-polymer relationship at all, however tempting it feels as a "condensation" example.

Section 7 · exam technique

🎯 Common mistakes & precise wording

3.1.1 is short — six ideas — but it's tested constantly, because condensation and hydrolysis reappear inside almost every other biological-molecules and metabolism topic. It isn't conceptually hard; the marks that get lost, get lost to imprecise wording.

▶ Why this "easy" spec point still costs marks nationally

At this level, most questions are word-perfect 1-mark recall: define a term, or identify a monomer on a matching grid — entirely accessible once the four core definitions are memorised exactly. Where it actually bites is the "is it a polymer?" tick-grid trap (ATP and phospholipids are made by condensation but are not polymers), and the outright ban on "building blocks" or a triglyceride as a monomer–polymer example. These two traps — not conceptual difficulty — account for the overwhelming majority of marks lost on this page nationally. Don't file 3.1.1 as "easy, move on": drill the four definitions to word-perfect recall, then specifically rehearse why each trap fails the "many repeating monomers" test, not just that it does.

▶ How to approach a matching-grid or tick-grid question

These questions deliberately span carbohydrates, lipids and proteins in one go — forcing you to recall content from later spec points (3.1.2–3.1.4) through a 3.1.1 lens. Work every row or clue on its own evidence, in order, rather than eliminating options as you go: the same answer can be reused for more than one row, or not needed at all, so early elimination is one of the most common ways marks are lost here even when the underlying knowledge is solid. When asked to name the general reaction that joins monomers into a polymer, "condensation" and "polymerisation" are both accepted.

Section 8 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

This content is short enough to feel minor, but it's one of the most reusable paragraphs in the whole specification — treat it as the mechanism paragraph that almost every other essay eventually needs to cash in.

Two essay titles are built directly on this spec point: "The importance of condensation and hydrolysis reactions in organisms" and "The importance of polymers and their monomers in organisms." It also frequently supplies the opening paragraph for essays on a broader title — most often something like "the importance of nitrogen-containing substances in biological systems" — and for essays on chemical bonding across the whole course.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around nitrogen-containing substances

Amino acids and nucleotides are both nitrogen-containing monomers, joined by condensation reactions that release water and form new covalent bonds — peptide bonds between amino acids to build proteins, and phosphodiester bonds between nucleotides to build nucleic acids. Both polymers can later be broken back down by the reverse reaction, hydrolysis, which uses a water molecule to release the monomers again. This shared mechanism underlies nearly every nitrogen-containing molecule discussed elsewhere in an essay on this theme.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around chemical bonding generally

Almost every polymer in a living organism is assembled and disassembled using the same underlying chemistry: a condensation reaction forms a chemical bond between two monomers and releases a molecule of water, while hydrolysis reverses this exactly, breaking the bond using a water molecule. Monosaccharides, amino acids and nucleotides are examples of monomers joined this way, forming the glycosidic, peptide and phosphodiester bonds that hold together the polysaccharides, proteins and nucleic acids almost every later process in this essay depends on.

Essay technique: whenever a later paragraph in a longer answer needs a bond formed or broken — ATP resynthesis, peptide bonds in translation, glycosidic bonds in starch, phosphodiester bonds in DNA replication — name it explicitly as a condensation or hydrolysis reaction. Examiners credit the explicit link back to this mechanism, not just the correct biology sitting there in isolation.

🧠

Memory hook. Con-DENSE-ation packs monomers together and pushes water out; hydro-LYSIS pulls water in to split things apart. If you remember which way the water is moving, you always know which reaction you're looking at — and every named bond in this course (peptide, glycosidic, ester, phosphodiester) is formed and broken this exact same way.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Common biochemical basis of life

  • All life shares the same carbon-based chemistry and the same genetic code.
  • Used as synoptic evidence for evolution — never tested as a standalone fact.
  • More similar DNA base sequence (or protein sequence, e.g. cytochrome c) = more recent common ancestor.

2 · Monomers

  • Monomer = a smaller, repeating unit or molecule from which larger molecules/polymers are made. Both halves needed.
  • Never write "atoms," "elements," or "building blocks" — all rejected.
  • Matching-grid questions: work every clue on its own evidence, don't guess by elimination.

3 · Polymers

  • Polymer = a molecule made from a large number of monomers joined together (usually by condensation).
  • A dimer (two units) is NOT a polymer — you need a repeating pattern.
  • Made by condensation ≠ is a polymer: ATP and phospholipids are made by condensation but aren't polymers.

4 · Examples of monomers

  • Amino acid → peptide bond → polypeptide/protein.
  • Nucleotide → phosphodiester bond → polynucleotide/DNA/RNA.
  • α-glucose → glycosidic bond → starch/glycogen (iodine test, blue-black).
  • β-glucose → glycosidic bond → cellulose (straight, unbranched chains).
  • Never use a triglyceride as an example; never say "ester bond" for the named-polymer-bond mark.

5 · Condensation reactions

  • Joins monomers, forms a chemical bond, releases water — all three needed for the mark.
  • Peptide (amino acids), glycosidic (monosaccharides), ester (glycerol + fatty acid), phosphodiester (nucleotides) all form this way.
  • n monomers joining removes (n − 1) water molecules — check maltose: 2 × C₆H₁₂O₆ − H₂O = C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁.

6 · Hydrolysis reactions

  • Breaks a bond between monomers, uses a water molecule — both needed.
  • Exact reverse of condensation — same bonds, opposite direction.
  • Common contexts: peptidases digesting protein, triglycerides in the ileum/micelles, starch → maltose, ATP hydrolysis for active transport.
🧠

Water in = hydrolysis, water out = condensation. Never write "building blocks," never use a triglyceride as a monomer/polymer example, and always name the bond alongside the water.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Common biochemical basis of life

❓ Explain how a strong similarity between the DNA base sequence of two species can be used as evidence for evolution.
The more similar the DNA base sequence (or the amino-acid sequence of a shared protein, such as cytochrome c) between two species, the more closely related those species are, and the more recently they shared a common ancestor; this is indirect evidence for evolution.
❓ Suggest why the shared biochemistry of all living organisms is unlikely to appear as a stand-alone exam question.
It is introductory, synoptic content — it is assessed by applying it as evidence within questions on evolution, genetic diversity or classification, rather than being tested as an isolated recall fact.

Monomers

❓ What is a monomer?
A monomer is a smaller, repeating unit or molecule from which larger molecules (polymers) are made. Reject: atoms, elements, or "building blocks" for this definition — both halves (smaller/repeating unit, and larger molecules made from it) are required.
❓ A student writes "a monomer is a building block of a larger molecule." Explain why this would not gain full credit, and rewrite it so that it would.
"Building block" does not state that the unit is smaller and repeating, nor precisely that larger molecules/polymers are made from it — both halves are required. Correct wording: "a monomer is a smaller, repeating unit or molecule from which larger molecules/polymers are made."
❓ In a set of labelled molecules A–D, one gives a positive biuret test and is found in an enzyme's active site. Identify this monomer.
An amino acid.

Polymers

❓ What is a polymer?
A molecule made from a large number of monomers joined together (usually by condensation reactions); the process of forming one is called polymerisation. Polymers must contain many monomers — a dimer does not count.
❓ A diagram shows only two repeating units joined together. Explain why this is insufficient evidence that the molecule shown is a polymer.
Two units do not demonstrate a repeating pattern; evidence for a polymer requires showing that the unit repeats multiple times, not just that two are joined.
❓ ATP and a phospholipid are both formed by condensation reactions. Explain why neither is classified as a polymer.
ATP is a single nucleotide derivative, not a repeating chain of monomers; a phospholipid is held together by ester bonds but is not built from a repeating monomer. Both fail the requirement that a polymer must contain a large number of the same repeating monomer.

Examples of monomers

❓ Complete: the monomer of DNA is ___, joined by ___ bonds.
A nucleotide; joined by phosphodiester bonds, formed between the phosphate group of one nucleotide and the sugar of the next.
❓ A passage describes a polymer built from a monomer containing an amine group, a carboxyl group and a variable R group. Identify the monomer and the bond it forms.
An amino acid; joined to other amino acids by peptide bonds to form a polypeptide.
❓ Explain why a triglyceride would never be accepted as an example of a monomer–polymer pair.
A triglyceride is not a polymer: it is a single molecule of glycerol joined to three fatty acids by ester bonds, not a large number of repeating monomers, so it cannot illustrate the monomer-to-polymer relationship.

Condensation reactions

❓ Describe a condensation reaction.
A condensation reaction joins monomers together, forming a chemical bond, and releases (eliminates) a molecule of water. All three ideas — monomers joining, a bond forming, and water being released — are required.
❓ Two molecules of α-glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) condense to form maltose. Calculate the molecular formula of maltose.
2 × C₆H₁₂O₆ = C₁₂H₂₄O₁₂; subtract one H₂O for the single glycosidic bond formed → C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁.
❓ Describe how a peptide bond is formed.
A condensation reaction occurs between two amino acids: an amine (–NH₂) group on one joins with a carboxyl (–COOH) group on the other, forming a peptide bond and releasing a molecule of water; repeating this builds up a specific sequence of amino acids.

Hydrolysis reactions

❓ Describe a hydrolysis reaction.
A hydrolysis reaction breaks a chemical bond between monomers, using a water molecule; both the bond breaking and the water being used must be stated.
❓ Describe the roles of endopeptidases, exopeptidases and dipeptidases in the hydrolysis of a protein.
Endopeptidases hydrolyse peptide bonds within the polypeptide chain, breaking it into smaller peptide chains; exopeptidases hydrolyse peptide bonds at the ends of these chains, removing terminal amino acids one at a time; dipeptidases hydrolyse the remaining dipeptides into single amino acids.
❓ Describe the chemical reactions involved in converting polymers to monomers and monomers to polymers, using two named examples.
A condensation reaction joins monomers together, forming a chemical bond and releasing a molecule of water; a hydrolysis reaction breaks that bond between monomers, using a molecule of water. For example, amino acids are the monomers joined by peptide bonds to form a polypeptide, and nucleotides are the monomers joined by phosphodiester bonds to form DNA.
