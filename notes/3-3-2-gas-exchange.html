Gas Exchange

How single-celled organisms, insects, fish and dicot leaves all solve the same problem — getting oxygen in and carbon dioxide out fast enough — and how the human gas exchange system does it at scale, from alveolus to ventilation mechanism to lung disease.

Section 1 · spec content point 1, part 1 of 4

🔬 Gas exchange across the body surface of a single-celled organism

Every "explain how X is adapted for gas exchange" question on this spec point, whatever the organism, is really the same four marks wearing different clothing. Single-celled organisms are the simplest case — no specialised structure at all, just the whole cell surface doing the job.

📖

What the specification says: "Adaptations of gas exchange surfaces, shown by gas exchange: across the body surface of a single-celled organism; in the tracheal system of an insect (tracheae, tracheoles and spiracles); across the gills of fish (gill lamellae and filaments including the counter-current principle); by the leaves of dicotyledonous plants (mesophyll and stomata)." This section covers the first named organism, the single-celled organism; sections 2–4 cover the other three.

1
2
3

Imagine two water balloons — one tiny, one huge. The tiny one has loads of skin compared to how much water is inside it; the huge one has much less skin per drop of water. That "skin compared to insides" ratio is what a cell has too. A single-celled organism is so small that its outer skin (its membrane) is plenty big enough, on its own, to let oxygen soak in and carbon dioxide leak out — no lungs, no gills, no special parts needed at all.

  • Diffusion = particles spreading out from where there's a lot of them to where there's less, until it's evened out
  • Surface area to volume ratio (SA:V) = how much outside skin an organism has compared to how much "inside stuff" it has to serve
  • Cell-surface membrane = the thin skin around every cell

Because a single-celled organism is so small, it has a large surface area relative to its volume — plenty of membrane per unit of cell that needs supplying. Oxygen simply diffuses in across the cell-surface membrane, which is the only structure gas has to cross, and carbon dioxide made by respiration diffuses straight back out the same way. Because the cell is so small, no point inside it is ever far from that outer surface, so the diffusion pathway is short. If the organism has a cell wall outside its membrane, that wall doesn't get in diffusion's way at all — it's not counted as an extra barrier. The name for the process matters: it's diffusion (or "simple diffusion"), never facilitated diffusion — there's no carrier protein involved, gases just move directly across the phospholipid bilayer.

Why size determines whether a specialised surface is needed

As an organism gets larger, its surface area to volume ratio falls — surface area increases with the square of a linear dimension, volume with the cube, so volume grows faster than surface area as size increases (the same underlying principle covered under SA:V in 3.3.1). A small organism therefore has a large amount of surface per unit of volume to be supplied, while a large one does not; this is why larger organisms need specialised, dedicated gas exchange surfaces and single-celled ones don't.

Gas exchange in a single-celled organism

  • Being small gives the organism a large surface area to volume ratio.
  • Oxygen is absorbed by diffusion across the whole body surface, which is covered only by the cell-surface membrane — that membrane is the gas exchange surface.
  • Carbon dioxide produced by respiration diffuses out across the same membrane, down its own concentration gradient.
  • The diffusion pathway is short because no part of the cell is ever far from the surface.
  • Where a cell wall is present (e.g. in some single-celled algae), it is no additional barrier to the diffusion of gases.
📌

Name the process precisely: diffusion, or "simple diffusion" — never facilitated diffusion. Gases move directly across the phospholipid bilayer; no carrier protein is needed for this.

The four-feature skeleton — worth learning here, before the other three organisms

Almost every adaptation question anywhere on this spec point, for any of the four named organisms, is testing the same underlying idea in different clothing:

  1. A large surface area — always name the actual structure providing it.
  2. A short diffusion pathway — always name the thin thing responsible.
  3. A steep diffusion gradient, maintained — by ventilation of the external medium and/or circulation of an internal one.
  4. A permeable surface.

If the organism is terrestrial, a fifth idea belongs alongside these four: the water-loss compromise, covered in Section 5.

▶ 🚩 Trap — stopping at "large surface area"

An answer that gives "large surface area" and then stops has missed every mark that follows it. A strong answer always gives at least three separate features from the four-feature skeleton above — not the same idea in different words repeated three times.

Section 2 · spec content point 1, part 2 of 4

🐛 Gas exchange in the tracheal system of an insect

An insect's whole gas exchange strategy is to skip blood entirely — air is delivered directly to the tissues that need it, through a branching system of tubes running straight through the body.

📖

What the specification says: "Adaptations of gas exchange surfaces, shown by gas exchange: across the body surface of a single-celled organism; in the tracheal system of an insect (tracheae, tracheoles and spiracles); across the gills of fish (gill lamellae and filaments including the counter-current principle); by the leaves of dicotyledonous plants (mesophyll and stomata)." This section covers the tracheal system of an insect.

1
2
3

Picture a network of drinking straws running from little doors on the outside of an insect's body, all the way down into every single muscle. Air just walks straight in through the doors, along the straws, and reaches the muscles directly — there's no blood involved anywhere in the journey, because the air is already exactly where it needs to be.

  • Spiracle = the little door on the outside, with a valve that can open and shut
  • Trachea = the main straw, held open by stiff rings
  • Tracheole = the tiniest branches of the straw, going right up to individual cells

Air enters an insect through spiracles — openings in the body wall, each with a valve that can close — and travels down tracheae, tubes held permanently open by strengthened rings so they never collapse. Tracheae branch into tracheoles: very numerous, highly branched, thin-walled tubes that carry air right up to individual respiring cells. Gas simply diffuses along the tracheoles into the cells — and because this is diffusion through air rather than through water, it's much faster. At no point does blood or a respiratory pigment get involved; the air goes directly to the tissue.

The pathway, structure by structure

  • Spiracles — openings in the body wall, each controlled by a valve that can close, mainly to limit water loss (see Section 5) rather than to aid gas exchange itself.
  • Tracheae — tubes held permanently open by strengthened rings, so they stay air-filled even under pressure.
  • Tracheoles — highly branched, thin-walled tubes that carry air directly to, and sometimes into, individual respiring cells.

Naming all three structures — spiracles, tracheae, tracheoles — anywhere in an answer earns credit on its own. Air moves in bulk down the trachea, then diffuses along the tracheoles into the tissue; there is no blood or haemoglobin involved at any point in this pathway.

Air moves in bulk down the trachea, then diffuses along the highly branched tracheoles straight into respiring cells — there is no blood or haemoglobin anywhere in this pathway.
📌

Building a full-marks answer: tracheoles are highly branched, giving a large surface area (accept "network" or "large number" for "highly branched"); their walls are thin, giving a short diffusion distance to cells — or the tracheoles enter cells directly, shortening the distance further; and tracheae are tubes full of air, so diffusion in air is faster than diffusion in water. A water-loss point (Section 5) is needed alongside these structural points to complete a fuller answer.

▶ 🚩 Trap — vague walls, misplaced spiracles, and blood

Don't describe tracheole walls as simply "thin membranes" — name the actual feature (numerous, highly branched, thin-walled tracheoles) and link it explicitly to surface area or diffusion distance. Citing spiracles as an adaptation "for efficient gas exchange" is not credited — spiracles are there to limit water loss, not to aid gas exchange itself. And if an answer mentions blood or a respiratory pigment at all, it is capped at very few marks: insects have no respiratory pigment, and air is delivered directly to the tissues without one.

Section 3 · spec content point 1, part 3 of 4

🐟 Gas exchange across the gills of fish, and the counter-current principle

The counter-current principle is the single most heavily flagged idea on the whole specification — get the wording exactly right, because "close" wording here is treated as wrong wording.

📖

What the specification says: "Adaptations of gas exchange surfaces, shown by gas exchange: across the body surface of a single-celled organism; in the tracheal system of an insect (tracheae, tracheoles and spiracles); across the gills of fish (gill lamellae and filaments including the counter-current principle); by the leaves of dicotyledonous plants (mesophyll and stomata)." This section covers the gills of fish, including the counter-current principle.

1
2
3

Imagine two conveyor belts running right next to each other, but moving in opposite directions — one carrying oxygen-rich water, the other carrying oxygen-poor blood. Because they're always moving past each other rather than alongside each other in the same direction, oxygen keeps being able to hop from the water belt to the blood belt all the way along, right to the very end. If both belts moved the same way, they'd end up "equal" partway along and oxygen would stop hopping across.

  • Filament and lamella = the feathery folds of a gill where the exchange happens
  • Counter-current = flowing in opposite directions

Gill filaments and the secondary lamellae on them give a large surface area; their epithelium is thin and flattened — one or two cell layers — giving a short diffusion pathway between water and blood. What makes gills so efficient is that water and blood flow in opposite directions across the gill (never "blood and oxygen" — oxygen is a dissolved gas, it doesn't flow on its own). Because of this opposite flow, blood entering the gill is always meeting water that still has more oxygen in it than the blood does, all the way along — so the diffusion gradient is kept going along the entire length of the lamella, not just at one end.

Structural features of the gill

  • Filaments and secondary lamellae give the gill a large surface area.
  • A thin, flattened epithelium (one or two cell layers) gives a short diffusion pathway between water and blood.
  • Counter-current flow maintains the diffusion gradient.

The counter-current principle, in exactly three parts

  1. Direction of flow — water and blood flow in opposite directions across the gill.
  2. What this maintains — a diffusion/concentration gradient for oxygen, kept higher in the water than in the blood at every point.
  3. Where — along the whole length of the lamellae/filament/gill, not just at one end.
Water and blood flow in opposite directions (never "blood and oxygen"). Blood entering is always meeting water carrying more oxygen than the blood does, so the diffusion gradient is maintained along the whole length of the lamella.
📎

The numbers, worth quoting if space allows: counter-current flow allows around 80% of the oxygen in the water to be absorbed. Parallel flow would only maintain the gradient across half the lamella, and would only manage to absorb around 50%.

▶ 🚩 The most heavily flagged reject on the whole specification

Never write "blood and oxygen flow in opposite directions." Oxygen is a dissolved gas, not itself a flowing stream — only the two media, water and blood, actually flow. The correct pairing is always "water and blood flow in opposite directions." Also, don't credit "thin cell walls/membranes" for the gill epithelium — say "thin, flattened epithelium, one or two cell layers" instead.

Section 4 · spec content point 1, part 4 of 4

🍃 Gas exchange by the leaves of dicotyledonous plants

A leaf has no ventilation and no circulation at all — every gas simply diffuses, relying entirely on structure to keep the pathway short and the surface large.

📖

What the specification says: "Adaptations of gas exchange surfaces, shown by gas exchange: across the body surface of a single-celled organism; in the tracheal system of an insect (tracheae, tracheoles and spiracles); across the gills of fish (gill lamellae and filaments including the counter-current principle); by the leaves of dicotyledonous plants (mesophyll and stomata)." This section covers the leaves of dicotyledonous plants.

1
2
3

A leaf is a bit like a sponge full of tiny connected tunnels of air. Little doors called stomata let carbon dioxide wander in, and once it's inside, it can travel through all those connected air tunnels to reach almost any cell in the leaf — no pumping needed, it just spreads out on its own.

  • Stoma (plural stomata) = a pore in the leaf surface, opened and closed by two guard cells
  • Mesophyll = the layer of cells inside a leaf that does photosynthesis and respiration

Leaves have many small pores called stomata, spread across the surface, so no cell inside the leaf is ever far from one — keeping the diffusion pathway short. Numerous interconnecting air spaces run all through the spongy mesophyll, letting gases reach mesophyll cells easily once they're inside. Carbon dioxide enters through a stoma (opened by its two guard cells), diffuses through those air spaces, and reaches a photosynthesising cell down a diffusion gradient. A large surface area of mesophyll cells is exposed to the air spaces, and because this is diffusion through gas rather than water, it's fast. The gradients themselves are kept going by respiration in mitochondria and photosynthesis in chloroplasts — a leaf has no dedicated transport system moving these gases at all.

  • Many small pores, stomata, mean no cell is far from a stoma, so the diffusion pathway is short.
  • Numerous interconnecting air spaces throughout the mesophyll let gases readily reach mesophyll cells.
  • Carbon dioxide enters via stomata, opened by guard cells, then diffuses through the air spaces down a diffusion gradient to a photosynthesising cell.
  • A large surface area of mesophyll cells is exposed to those air spaces.
  • Diffusion in the gas phase is faster than diffusion in water.
  • Gradients are maintained by respiration in mitochondria and photosynthesis in chloroplasts — there is no dedicated transport system for these gases in a leaf.
Stomata (pore + 2 guard cells) open onto interconnecting spongy-mesophyll air spaces, so CO₂ can reach any photosynthesising cell by diffusion alone — no leaf structure moves gas the way ventilation or blood flow does in an animal.
▶ 🚩 Trap — "stroma" and vague gradient direction

Never write "stroma" when you mean "stomata" — the stroma is a completely different structure, found inside a chloroplast. And don't leave "along a gradient" unqualified — always make the direction clear, e.g. "down a diffusion gradient."

Section 5 · spec content point 2

💧 Gas exchange vs water loss: the compromise

Everything that makes an exchange surface good at gas exchange — thin, permeable, large area — also makes it good at losing water. Terrestrial insects and xerophytic plants both have to negotiate that same trade-off.

📖

What the specification says: "Structural and functional compromises between the opposing needs for efficient gas exchange and the limitation of water loss shown by terrestrial insects and xerophytic plants."

1
2
3

Imagine wearing a raincoat with a zip on it. Zip it open fully and you get lots of fresh air — but you also get soaked when it rains. Zip it shut and you stay dry — but you get stuffy and can't breathe well. Insects and desert plants have to find a middle ground between "let gas in and out easily" and "don't dry out," and they've each evolved their own tricks to manage both at once.

Efficient gas exchange needs a thin, permeable surface with a large area — but those are exactly the features that let water evaporate away fastest too. Terrestrial insects manage this with a waterproof outer layer, spiracles with valves that can shut, hairs around the spiracles, and by simply being small (a small surface area to volume ratio limits the area losing water in the first place). Closing the spiracles works against getting oxygen in, though, so insects mostly do this while resting. Plants adapted to dry conditions (xerophytes) use hairs or sunken stomata to trap a layer of still, humid air right by the leaf surface, a thick waxy cuticle to make evaporation harder, and sometimes rolled leaves or spine-like leaves for the same reasons.

⚖️

State the conflict first, every time: efficient gas exchange needs a thin, permeable surface with a large area — exactly the features that increase water loss by evaporation. Locating an exchange surface inside the body means the air surrounding it is close to saturated with water vapour, so less water evaporates out of it in the first place.

How terrestrial insects limit water loss

  • A waterproof cuticle/chitin/exoskeleton reduces water loss.
  • Spiracles can close (they have valves), so little or no water is lost through them.
  • Hairs around the spiracles reduce water loss further.
  • A small surface area to volume ratio minimises the total area over which water can be lost.
📌

The compromise itself: closing the spiracles conflicts directly with the insect's need for oxygen — so this happens largely while the insect is at rest, not during activity.

Xerophytic plant adaptations

  • Hairs, or stomata sunk in pits or grooves, 'trap' water vapour close to the leaf surface, decreasing the water potential gradient that would otherwise drive evaporation.
  • A thick, waxy cuticle increases the diffusion distance and reduces evaporation.
  • Rolled or curled leaves trap still, saturated air in the same way.
  • Spines or needles reduce the surface area to volume ratio.

Accept "humid" or "moist air" for "water vapour" (but not "water" or "moisture" alone); a "diffusion gradient" is accepted as equivalent to a "water potential gradient" here.

▶ Why rolled leaves roll that particular way

Stomata are largely confined to the lower epidermis of a leaf, so rolling with the lower epidermis on the inside traps a pocket of still air that becomes saturated with water vapour — no water potential gradient forms between the inside and outside of the leaf, so no water is lost. Rolling the other way round would leave the stomata directly exposed, defeating the point entirely.

▶ Why plants can't simply shrink like insects can

Plants photosynthesise, and photosynthesis needs a large leaf surface area for capturing light as well as for exchanging gases. Reducing surface area to save water is therefore always balanced against that separate need — it can't just be minimised the way an insect's whole body plan can.

▶ 🚩 Trap — using water-potential language for gas exchange itself

A very common wrong turn: explaining the actual movement of gas across an exchange surface — alveoli, gills, leaves, or an unfamiliar organism — in terms of water potential or osmosis. Water-potential language belongs only to water-loss questions (this section); gas movement itself is always described as diffusion down a diffusion/concentration gradient, never as movement down a water potential gradient.

Section 6 · spec content point 3

🌬️ The gross structure of the human gas exchange system

Five named structures, always tested in the same physical order — learn the sequence exactly, because getting the order wrong on a "path of an oxygen molecule" question limits how many marks the rest of the answer can earn.

📖

What the specification says: "The gross structure of the human gas exchange system limited to the alveoli, bronchioles, bronchi, trachea and lungs."

1
2
3

Think of your airway like a tree turned upside down and shrunk right down. Air comes in through one big trunk (the trachea), splits into two big branches (the bronchi, one per lung), then keeps splitting into smaller and smaller twigs (the bronchioles), until it ends at tiny leaves — the alveoli — where the actual swap of oxygen for carbon dioxide happens.

The trachea is a flexible airway held open by rings of cartilage so it doesn't collapse when you breathe in; it's lined with cells that make mucus and cells with cilia that sweep that mucus away. The trachea splits into two bronchi, one to each lung, built similarly but with less cartilage. Bronchi branch further into bronchioles, which have muscle in their walls but no cartilage at all — that muscle lets them narrow or widen to control airflow. Bronchioles end in millions of tiny alveoli, the actual gas exchange surface, which can stretch as you breathe in and spring back to push CO₂-rich air back out. The whole system sits inside the body because air alone isn't dense enough to support and protect it, and being inside also limits water loss.

StructureStructure and function
TracheaFlexible airway supported by rings of cartilage, preventing it from collapsing as air pressure inside falls during breathing in. Walls of muscle, lined with ciliated epithelium and goblet cells.
BronchiTwo divisions of the trachea, one to each lung. Similar structure to the trachea; produce mucus to trap dirt particles, with cilia that move the dirt-laden mucus towards the throat. Cartilage support decreases as the bronchi get smaller.
BronchiolesBranching subdivisions of the bronchi. Walls of muscle lined with epithelial cells — no cartilage; the muscle allows them to constrict, controlling the flow of air in and out of the alveoli.
AlveoliMinute air sacs, 100–300 µm in diameter, at the ends of the bronchioles. Lined with epithelium; collagen and elastic fibres between them let alveoli stretch on breathing in and spring back to expel CO₂-rich air. Roughly 300 million per lung; total surface area around 70 m².
LungsA pair of lobed structures made of highly branched tubules ending in alveoli; located inside the body because air is not dense enough to support and protect them, and because the body would otherwise lose a great deal of water.

The path of an oxygen molecule, atmosphere to blood

Trachea → bronchi → bronchioles, moving down a pressure gradient, then across the alveolar epithelium and the capillary endothelium, moving down a diffusion gradient into the blood.

  1. Trachea, then bronchi, then bronchioles;
  2. down a pressure gradient;
  3. down a diffusion gradient, across the alveolar epithelium;
  4. across the capillary endothelium.

"Squamous epithelial cells" is accepted for epithelium; "capillary wall" is a neutral description of the capillary endothelium.

▶ 🚩 This question is sequence-sensitive

An incorrect order caps the alveolar/capillary marks severely. Learn the order exactly: trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → (pressure gradient, then diffusion gradient) → alveolar epithelium → capillary endothelium. Getting the biology right in the wrong order still loses marks here.

How the trachea and bronchi protect the alveoli

Goblet cells secrete mucus, which traps dirt particles and microorganisms. Ciliated epithelial cells then beat to move that dirt-laden mucus up towards the throat, where it is swallowed — so pathogens and particles never reach, or damage, the delicate alveoli further down the airway.

Section 7 · spec content point 4

🎈 The essential features of the alveolar epithelium

This is the single highest-frequency short question anywhere on 3.3.2 — and the single most commonly dropped mark in the whole topic comes from one wrong word choice.

📖

What the specification says: "The essential features of the alveolar epithelium as a surface over which gas exchange takes place."

1
2
3

Each alveolus is like a tiny, super-thin balloon wrapped in a net of even tinier tubes (capillaries). The balloon's skin and the tube's skin are each only one cell thick, so together gas only has two thin skins to cross to get from air into blood — that's why it happens so fast.

The alveolar epithelium is made of flattened cells, just one layer thick, which reduces the diffusion distance gas has to cross. It's permeable, allowing oxygen and carbon dioxide to diffuse straight through. There are millions of alveoli, with folded walls, surrounded by a huge network of capillaries, giving a very large surface area for exchange. Ventilation constantly brings in oxygen-rich air and removes oxygen-poor air, while circulation constantly replaces oxygen-poor blood with fresh oxygen-poor blood needing to pick up oxygen — together, these keep the diffusion gradient steep. Red blood cells are also slowed down as they pass through the tiny capillaries, and get flattened against the capillary wall, both of which give gas more time and a shorter distance to diffuse across.

Essential features of the alveolar epithelium

  • Flattened cells, or a single layer of cells;
  • which reduces the diffusion distance/pathway;
  • the epithelium is permeable;
  • allowing diffusion of oxygen/carbon dioxide.

"Thin cells" and "one cell thick" are both accepted equivalents for "flattened cells".

The alveolar epithelium and the capillary endothelium are each one cell thick, so the total diffusion pathway is just two cells — never "thin membrane" for either wall.
▶ 🚩 The most commonly dropped mark in the whole topic

Reject "thin cell wall" or "thin membrane" for the alveolar epithelium — write "one cell thick" or "flattened cells" instead. This single wording swap accounts for more lost marks across this topic than any actual gap in understanding.

Explaining how the alveoli are adapted for rapid gas exchange

  • Many alveoli, with folded walls, and many surrounding capillaries, give a large surface area — for fast diffusion.
  • The flattened/squamous epithelium is thin, giving a short diffusion distance/pathway — for fast diffusion.
  • Ventilation and circulation together maintain a diffusion/concentration gradient — for fast diffusion.
  • Two further credited points: red blood cells are slowed as they pass through pulmonary capillaries, allowing more time for diffusion; and red blood cells are flattened against the capillary walls, reducing the distance to alveolar air.

How ventilation and circulation maintain the gradient

Ventilation, within the alveolus itself, brings in air with a higher oxygen concentration and removes air with a lower oxygen concentration — both the idea of air moving and of oxygen concentration are needed for full credit. Circulation is the movement of blood, which replaces blood carrying a high oxygen concentration with blood carrying a low oxygen concentration ready to pick more up; "blood" alone, with no reference to movement or replacement, isn't enough on its own.

📌

"Alveoli provide a large surface area" on its own, without "many" or "folded," reads as a neutral statement rather than a credited point — always attach the reason the surface area is large.

Section 8 · spec content point 5

🫀 Ventilation — the mechanism of breathing

Up to six marks can ride on this one mechanism — the antagonistic pull between two named muscle groups, described precisely enough to show pressure and volume changing in the right order.

📖

What the specification says: "Ventilation and the exchange of gases in the lungs. The mechanism of breathing to include the role of the diaphragm and the antagonistic interaction between the external and internal intercostal muscles in bringing about pressure changes in the thoracic cavity."

1
2
3

Think of your chest like the plunger of a big syringe. Pull the plunger out (that's your diaphragm and rib muscles working) and the space inside gets bigger, so air gets sucked in to fill it. Push the plunger back in (relax those muscles) and the space shrinks, squeezing air back out. Breathing hard — like after running — is like giving that plunger an extra shove on the way back in.

Breathing in: the diaphragm contracts and flattens, and the external intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribcage up and out; this increases the volume of the thoracic cavity, which drops the pressure inside below atmospheric pressure, so air moves in down that pressure gradient. Breathing out, in a normal resting breath, is mostly passive — the diaphragm and external intercostals simply relax, and the stretched elastic lung tissue recoils on its own, shrinking the cavity and pushing air out. Under exercise or effort, forced expiration adds active muscle contraction: the internal intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribcage down harder and decreasing the volume further, forcing more air out faster.

PhaseWhat happens
InspirationDiaphragm muscles contract, and the diaphragm flattens; external intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribcage up and out; volume of the thoracic cavity increases and pressure falls, below atmospheric; air moves down a pressure gradient, entering from the higher atmospheric pressure outside.
Expiration (quiet, at rest)Diaphragm muscles relax and it moves back up into a domed shape; external intercostal muscles relax and the ribcage moves down and in; volume decreases and pressure rises, above atmospheric; air is forced out down the pressure gradient. This is largely a passive process — the elastic recoil of lung tissue, not muscle contraction, is the main cause.
Forced expirationInternal intercostal muscles contract (in addition to the diaphragm and external intercostals relaxing), pulling the ribcage down further; this causes a larger decrease in thoracic volume; air is pushed out down a steeper pressure gradient. Internal intercostals contract only for forced expiration, not quiet breathing.
▶ 🚩 Trap — vocabulary that loses marks in ventilation answers

Reject "chest" as a substitute for "thoracic cavity". Reject "ribs move up and out" given as a standalone point with nothing else attached. Reject "air is sucked in", "by diffusion", or "space increases" as descriptions of ventilation itself — ventilation is a bulk/mass movement of air down a pressure gradient, not diffusion. Don't credit the diaphragm-contraction mark if an answer describes intercostal muscles as causing the diaphragm to flatten — the diaphragm is a separate muscle, controlled independently.

Pulmonary ventilation rate

Pulmonary ventilation rate = tidal volume × breathing rate, with units (dm³ min⁻¹) = (dm³) × (min⁻¹).

  • Tidal volume = the volume of air taken in at each breath at rest, around 0.5 dm³ — must carry the context "in one breath," not "volume" on its own.
  • Breathing rate = the number of breaths per minute, normally 12–20 in a healthy adult.

On a spirometer trace, a falling baseline over time means the volume of air in the lung system is decreasing — oxygen is being used up in respiration, and carbon dioxide is being absorbed by the soda lime in the apparatus.

Section 9 · exam skills

🫁 Lung disease, risk factors & data interpretation

These questions reward the ability to follow one causal chain all the way through, and to give a genuinely balanced evaluation rather than a one-sided answer.

🧭

Follow the same chain, every time: structural change → effect on surface area, diffusion distance or elasticity → effect on the rate of diffusion or ventilation → effect on the body (less oxygen reaching the blood → less aerobic respiration → less ATP).

Working through emphysema as the model example

  • Alveoli break down, collapse or rupture, or alveolar walls thicken — reducing surface area;
  • so there is less diffusion;
  • elastin/elastic tissue is lost — the lungs stop recoiling properly and become permanently over-inflated;
  • this reduces the flow rate, so less air is expelled and the diffusion gradient is reduced — air isn't replenished;
  • less oxygen enters the blood and tissues, so less aerobic respiration occurs, meaning less ATP is produced (some tissue may switch to anaerobic respiration, producing lactate instead).
📌

The loss-of-elasticity point and the reduced-flow-rate point are two separate, distinct marks — don't credit elasticity twice for both. "Less surface area for diffusion" alone can be worth two separate marking points.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "energy produced," and vague damage

Never write "less energy produced" — write "less ATP produced" or "less energy released" instead. "Alveoli damaged," on its own with no further detail, is treated as a neutral statement rather than a credited point.

Correlation versus causation — a named skill on this spec point

A correlation is when a change in one variable is reflected by a change in another — but a correlation alone never establishes a causal link, since a third factor might be responsible, or the association could simply be coincidence. Moving from correlation to genuine cause requires forming a hypothesis, designing and running experiments to test it, and establishing a causal link with a theory that explains it.

📝

A safe way to phrase this: "The data show a positive correlation between X and Y, but do not show that X causes Y, because other factors (e.g. …) were not controlled."

Judging statistical significance

Using standard deviations or error bars: if the SDs (or 2×SD) don't overlap, the difference is statistically significant; if they do overlap, the difference could be down to chance — either way, always say what that means for the specific variable named in the question, not just "the results are significant" left unqualified. A balanced evaluation of a pollution or smoking claim needs points on both sides — for example, a significantly higher pollutant concentration with no overlap in 2×SD supports a claim, while no evidence that the measured level is actually dangerous, or no significant difference found elsewhere, argues against it. An answer that only argues one side is capped at around half the available marks.

Risk factors and statutory restrictions

Risk factors include smoking (around 90% of COPD sufferers are or were heavy smokers), air pollution, genetics, chest infections, and occupation. Restrictions worth citing include smoking bans in enclosed public places and workplaces and in cars carrying children, the tobacco advertising ban, plain packaging, a minimum sale age, air-quality legislation, and asbestos bans — each works by reducing exposure to a risk factor, which in turn reduces the incidence of disease. Some diseases, such as cancers linked to exposure, can develop 20–30 years after exposure, so a fall in incidence can lag well behind the introduction of a restriction.

Section 11 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

3.3.2 is an excellent source for a mid-essay paragraph in any diffusion- or exchange-themed 25-mark title — the trick is opening with the shared skeleton, then naming each surface's specific feature, rather than describing "diffusion happens" in general terms.

▶ Example paragraph — for a title on "the importance of diffusion in organisms"

Every specialised exchange surface shares the same underlying features: a large surface area, a thin or short diffusion pathway, permeability, and movement of the external and/or internal medium to maintain the diffusion gradient. In the alveoli, flattened epithelial cells give a short diffusion pathway, while ventilation and circulation together maintain the diffusion gradient for oxygen and carbon dioxide. In insects, tracheae and tracheoles rely on diffusion of gases through air — faster than through water — delivered directly to respiring tissues. In fish, counter-current flow in the gills maintains a diffusion gradient along the whole length of the lamellae. Gas exchange surfaces across these very different organisms are, underneath, structural adaptations built specifically to maximise the rate of diffusion.

▶ Example paragraph — for a title on structural adaptation more broadly

The counter-current arrangement of blood and water flow across a fish gill is a clear example of a structure organised purely to maintain a concentration gradient: because water and blood flow in opposite directions, blood entering the gill always meets water carrying more oxygen than the blood itself, so the gradient — and therefore the rate of diffusion — is sustained along the whole length of the lamella rather than only at one end. The same underlying logic, applied differently, explains why ventilation continuously refreshes the air at the alveolar surface and why circulation continuously refreshes the blood passing it.

Whichever essay title this content supports, treat it as a self-contained "mechanism" paragraph: open with the shared four-feature list, run through the named surfaces in the spec's own order (single-celled organism, insect, fish, leaf), naming a specific structural feature each time, and close by linking explicitly back to whatever the title is actually asking.

5-Minute Cram

The highest-yield bullets from each spec-content section — read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Single-celled organisms & SA:V

  • Small size → large surface area to volume ratio → whole cell-surface membrane acts as the exchange surface.
  • No part of the cell is far from the surface → short diffusion pathway. Cell wall, if present, is no barrier.
  • Name the process as diffusion/simple diffusion — never facilitated diffusion.
  • Four-feature skeleton for any surface: large SA, short pathway, steep gradient maintained, permeable.

2 · Insect tracheal system

  • Spiracles (valves, close mainly for water loss) → tracheae (rings keep them open, air-filled) → tracheoles (highly branched, thin-walled, reach cells directly).
  • Naming all three structures earns a mark on its own.
  • Diffusion in air is faster than in water; no blood or haemoglobin anywhere in the pathway.
  • Mentioning blood at all caps the answer at very few marks.

3 · Fish gills & counter-current

  • Filaments + secondary lamellae = large SA; thin flattened epithelium (1–2 cells) = short pathway.
  • Counter-current: water and blood flow in opposite directions (never "blood and oxygen").
  • Gradient maintained along the whole length of the lamellae, not just at one end.
  • Counter-current ≈ 80% O₂ absorbed; parallel flow ≈ 50%.

4 · Dicot leaves

  • Many stomata (pore + 2 guard cells) → no cell far from a stoma → short pathway.
  • Interconnecting spongy-mesophyll air spaces let gas reach any cell; large SA of mesophyll cells.
  • Gas-phase diffusion is faster than in water; gradients kept by respiration + photosynthesis, no dedicated transport system.
  • Never "stroma" for stomata.

5 · Gas exchange vs water loss

  • Efficient gas exchange needs thin/permeable/large area = more water loss — that's the compromise.
  • Insects: waterproof cuticle, spiracles close (valves), hairs, small SA:V; closing spiracles mostly happens at rest.
  • Xerophytes: hairs/sunken stomata trap water vapour, thick waxy cuticle, rolled leaves, spines/needles.
  • Water-potential language belongs here only — never for gas movement across a surface.

6 · Human gross structure

  • Order: trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → (pressure gradient) → alveolar epithelium → (diffusion gradient) → capillary endothelium.
  • Trachea/bronchi = cartilage rings + cilia + goblet cells; bronchioles = muscle only, no cartilage.
  • ~300 million alveoli per lung, ~70 m² total surface area.
  • Lungs internal: air not dense enough to support/protect them, and it limits water loss.

7 · Alveolar epithelium

  • Flattened cells/single layer → short diffusion distance; permeable → allows diffusion of O₂/CO₂.
  • Never "thin cell wall/membrane" — write "one cell thick" or "flattened cells".
  • Many folded alveoli + many capillaries = large SA; ventilation + circulation maintain the gradient.
  • RBCs slowed and flattened against capillary walls = more time and shorter distance to diffuse.

8 · Ventilation mechanism

  • Inspiration: diaphragm + external intercostals contract → volume↑ pressure↓ (below atm) → air in.
  • Quiet expiration: muscles relax, largely passive (elastic recoil) → volume↓ pressure↑ → air out.
  • Forced expiration: internal intercostals contract too → bigger volume decrease → air pushed out faster.
  • PVR = tidal volume × breathing rate (dm³ min⁻¹); reject "chest", "sucked in", "by diffusion".

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Single-celled organisms & SA:V

❓ Explain why a single-celled organism can rely on diffusion across its whole body surface for gas exchange, while a much larger multicellular organism cannot.
A single-celled organism is small, so it has a large surface area to volume ratio; oxygen diffuses in and carbon dioxide diffuses out across the cell-surface membrane, and no part of the cell is ever far from that surface, so the diffusion pathway is short. A larger organism has a smaller surface area to volume ratio, so diffusion across its outer surface alone cannot supply its inner cells fast enough, meaning it needs a specialised gas exchange surface instead.
❓ A student states that gases enter a single-celled organism by facilitated diffusion, because the organism has a cell wall. Evaluate this statement.
The statement is incorrect. Gases move by simple diffusion directly across the phospholipid bilayer of the cell-surface membrane; no carrier protein is involved, so facilitated diffusion is not the correct term. A cell wall, where present, is not an additional barrier to gas diffusion and does not change the process involved.
❓ Give three separate features that any specialised gas exchange surface needs, and explain why each one increases the rate of gas exchange.
A large surface area, which increases the rate of exchange; a short diffusion pathway (a thin, or flattened, structure), so materials cross rapidly; and a permeable surface, allowing gases to cross freely. A fourth feature — movement of the external and/or internal medium to maintain a diffusion gradient — is also credited where relevant.

Insect tracheal system

❓ Explain how the tracheal system of an insect is adapted for efficient gas exchange.
Spiracles, tracheae and tracheoles are the named structures; oxygen diffuses in through the spiracles and along the tracheae and tracheoles. Tracheoles are highly branched, giving a large surface area; their walls are thin, and they can enter cells directly, giving a short diffusion distance. Tracheae are tubes full of air, and diffusion in air is faster than diffusion in water.
❓ A student's answer to a question on insect gas exchange includes the sentence "oxygen is carried in the blood to the tissues." Explain why this loses marks, and what should be written instead.
Insects have no respiratory pigment, and their tracheal system delivers air directly to respiring tissues without any involvement of blood; any reference to blood caps the mark available for that part of the answer. The correct description is that air moves in bulk down the tracheae and then diffuses along the tracheoles directly into the tissues.
❓ Explain why citing the spiracles as an adaptation "for efficient gas exchange" is not creditworthy.
Spiracles are valve-like openings whose main role is to limit water loss, by being able to close — not to improve the efficiency of gas exchange itself. The gas-exchange credit instead comes from naming the tracheae and tracheoles and linking their structure to surface area or diffusion distance.

Fish gills & counter-current

❓ Explain the counter-current principle in a fish gill, and state why it allows a greater percentage of dissolved oxygen to be extracted from the water than a parallel-flow arrangement would.
Water and blood flow in opposite directions across the gill; this means blood entering the gill is always meeting water with a higher oxygen concentration than the blood, so a diffusion gradient is maintained along the whole length of the lamellae, not just at one end. Counter-current flow allows around 80% of the oxygen in the water to be absorbed, compared with only around 50% for parallel flow, where the gradient could only be maintained across half the lamella before equilibrium was reached.
❓ A student writes: "blood and oxygen flow in opposite directions across the gill." Rewrite this using the correct, precise wording.
Water and blood flow in opposite directions across the gill. Oxygen is a dissolved gas and does not itself flow as a stream; only the two media, water and blood, actually flow.
❓ Explain why fish gills are located on the outside of the body, whereas mammalian lungs are located inside.
Water has a lower oxygen concentration than air, so an external gill system maximises the surface area in contact with water and minimises the diffusion distance between water and blood. Water is also denser than air, so it can physically support the delicate gill structures — unlike air, which cannot support or protect an equivalent structure, so lungs are housed internally instead.

Dicot leaves

❓ Describe how a molecule of carbon dioxide travels from the atmosphere to a photosynthesising cell inside a dicot leaf.
Carbon dioxide enters the leaf via a stoma, which is opened by its two guard cells; it then diffuses through the interconnecting air spaces of the spongy mesophyll, down a diffusion gradient, until it reaches a photosynthesising mesophyll cell.
❓ Explain why the palisade mesophyll layer being only one or a few cells thick benefits the leaf, distinguishing this from a gas-exchange adaptation.
A single, or few, layer(s) of palisade cells allows light to pass through the tissue and reach chloroplasts efficiently, maximising light absorption for photosynthesis — this is a light-capture adaptation, separate from (though it also indirectly shortens) the gas diffusion pathway credited under the four-feature skeleton.
❓ A student writes "carbon dioxide moves along a gradient into the leaf." Explain what needs to be added for this to gain full credit.
The direction of the gradient must be stated explicitly — "down a diffusion gradient" — since "along a gradient" alone does not specify direction and is not creditable on its own.

Gas exchange vs water loss

❓ Explain three ways in which a terrestrial insect is adapted to limit water loss while still permitting gas exchange to occur.
A waterproof cuticle/chitin exoskeleton reduces water loss across the body surface; spiracles have valves that can close, so little or no water is lost through them; and a small surface area to volume ratio minimises the total area over which water can evaporate. The compromise is that closing the spiracles also restricts oxygen entry, so this happens largely while the insect is at rest.
❓ Explain how a rolled xerophytic leaf reduces water loss, referring to water potential in your answer.
Rolling the leaf with the lower epidermis, where most stomata are located, on the inside traps a pocket of still air that becomes saturated with water vapour. This removes the water potential gradient between the inside and outside of the leaf, so no net water loss occurs by diffusion out through the stomata.
❓ A student explains gas movement across the alveolar epithelium using the term "water potential gradient." Explain why this is inappropriate, and state the correct term.
Water-potential language describes the movement of water by osmosis, and should be reserved for water-loss questions involving terrestrial insects or xerophytic plants. Movement of a gas such as oxygen or carbon dioxide across an exchange surface is always described as diffusion down a diffusion, or concentration, gradient.

Human gross structure

❓ Describe the path taken by a molecule of oxygen from the atmosphere to the blood, in the correct order.
The oxygen molecule passes through the trachea, then the bronchi, then the bronchioles, moving down a pressure gradient; it then crosses the alveolar epithelium and the capillary endothelium, moving down a diffusion gradient, to enter the blood.
❓ Give two structural features of the trachea and explain the function of each.
Rings of cartilage support the trachea, preventing it from collapsing when air pressure inside falls during inspiration; and ciliated epithelium, together with mucus-secreting goblet cells, traps dirt particles and microorganisms and sweeps them up towards the throat to be swallowed, protecting the lower airway.
❓ Explain how the cells lining the trachea and bronchi protect the alveoli from damage.
Goblet cells secrete mucus, which traps dirt particles and microorganisms; ciliated epithelial cells then beat to move this dirt-laden mucus up towards the throat, where it is swallowed, so that pathogens and particles do not reach and damage the delicate alveoli.

Alveolar epithelium

❓ Give the essential features of the alveolar epithelium that allow it to function as a gas exchange surface.
The epithelium is made of flattened cells, forming a single layer, which reduces the diffusion distance; it is permeable, allowing diffusion of oxygen and carbon dioxide across it.
❓ Explain, in as much detail as you can, how the alveoli are adapted for rapid gas exchange.
There are many alveoli with folded walls, and many surrounding capillaries, giving a large surface area for fast diffusion. The flattened, squamous epithelium is thin, giving a short diffusion distance for fast diffusion. Ventilation and circulation together maintain a diffusion/concentration gradient, again for fast diffusion. Red blood cells are also slowed as they pass through the pulmonary capillaries, allowing more time for diffusion, and are flattened against the capillary walls, reducing the distance to alveolar air.
❓ A student writes "the alveoli have a thin membrane, so gas exchange is fast." Rewrite this using precise, full-credit wording.
The alveolar epithelium is one cell thick (flattened cells), which reduces the diffusion distance/pathway, so gas exchange occurs rapidly.

Ventilation mechanism

❓ Describe how air is moved into the lungs during a normal, quiet breath.
The diaphragm muscles contract and the diaphragm flattens; the external intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribcage up and out; this increases the volume of the thoracic cavity and decreases the pressure inside it, below atmospheric pressure; air then moves in, down the pressure gradient, from the higher atmospheric pressure outside.
❓ Describe the changes that occur during forced expiration, and explain how this differs from quiet expiration.
During forced expiration, the internal intercostal muscles contract, while the diaphragm and external intercostal muscles relax; this causes a greater decrease in the volume of the thoracic cavity than in quiet breathing, forcing air out down a steeper pressure gradient. In quiet expiration, by contrast, the process is largely passive — the elastic recoil of the lung tissue, rather than muscle contraction, is the main cause of air being forced out.
