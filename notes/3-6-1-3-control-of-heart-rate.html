Control of Heart Rate

How the heart keeps its own beat — the myogenic conduction pathway (SAN → AVN → bundle of His → Purkyne fibres) that sets the rhythm from inside — and how chemoreceptors and baroreceptors, working through the medulla and the sympathetic and parasympathetic nerves, adjust that rhythm to match the body's changing needs.

⚠️

The single habit that protects the most marks on this spec point: this whole topic is marked as one named, ordered chain — receptor and its location → sensory neurone → the medulla (the cardiovascular/cardiac centre) → the correct autonomic branch, named → the SAN. "Impulses go to the SAN" on its own is only ever half an answer: always say which branch carries them, and in which direction they travel. And never write "signals," "messages" or "electronic" where the mark scheme wants impulses — these are electrical impulses within cardiac muscle or nerve impulses along a named neurone, never anything vaguer.

Section 1 · spec content point

💓 The myogenic heart & the intrinsic conduction pathway

The heart sets its own rhythm from inside its own muscle, with no nervous input needed at all. AQA marks the pathway that spreads that rhythm through the heart as a strict, ordered sequence — get the order right, and name every structure precisely.

📖

What the specification says: "Myogenic stimulation of the heart and transmission of a subsequent wave of electrical activity. The roles of the sinoatrial node (SAN), atrioventricular node (AVN) and Purkyne tissue in the bundle of His."

1
2
3

Your heart doesn't wait for a signal from your brain before it beats — it has its own built-in "spark plug" that fires off a wave of electricity all by itself, over and over, without any outside help. That's why a heart can carry on beating for a short while even after it's been removed from the body. The wave then travels through the heart in a set order, a bit like a Mexican wave passing through a crowd: it starts at the top, pauses briefly in the middle so the top half can finish emptying into the bottom, then races down the middle wall and spreads back up from the bottom, squeezing the lower chambers from the bottom up so blood gets pushed out through the top.

  • Myogenic = starting inside the heart muscle itself, not from a nerve
  • SAN (sinoatrial node) = the heart's own "spark plug", also called the pacemaker
  • AVN (atrioventricular node) = the brief "pause point" between the top and bottom chambers
  • Bundle of His / Purkyne fibres = the wiring that carries the spark down the middle wall and back up through the lower chambers

"Myogenic" means the heartbeat begins inside the cardiac muscle itself rather than being triggered by a nerve impulse from the brain — the nervous system only ever adjusts the rate later on, it never starts the beat. The sinoatrial node (SAN), in the wall of the right atrium, is the heart's pacemaker: it fires off a wave of electrical activity on its own, at a steady rhythm. That wave spreads across both atria, making them contract together, but a band of non-conducting tissue stops it reaching the ventricles directly. Instead it has to pass through the atrioventricular node (AVN), which delays it briefly, before sending it down the bundle of His — a strip of conducting fibres running through the septum — to the base of the heart. From there, the Purkyne fibres carry the wave back up through the ventricle walls, so the ventricles contract from the bottom upward.

  1. SAN fires spontaneously — the pacemaker
  2. Wave spreads across both atria — atrial systole
  3. Non-conducting tissue stops the wave reaching the ventricles directly
  4. AVN delays the wave briefly
  5. AVN passes the wave down the bundle of His, through the septum
  6. Purkyne fibres carry the wave from the base up through the ventricle walls — ventricular systole

Why the AVN delay matters

The delay allows the atria to finish contracting and empty their blood into the ventricles before the ventricles begin to contract. Don't just describe it as "a pause" — the mark is for stating its purpose specifically: the delay prevents the atria and ventricles contracting at the same time, so filling is complete before the ventricles squeeze.

Why the ventricles contract from the base upwards

Purkyne fibres carry the wave of excitation from the base of the heart upward through the ventricle walls, so contraction begins at the bottom of the ventricles and moves up — this forces blood upward and out through the arteries that leave at the top of the heart (the pulmonary artery and the aorta).

▶ 🚩 Trap — never say "signals," "messages" or "electronic"

These are electrical impulses travelling within cardiac muscle, not nerve impulses — write "wave of electrical activity" or "impulses," never a vaguer substitute. One mark is available simply for giving the sequence in the correct order — you can start describing it from any point in the cycle, but the events must follow the correct order. Either spelling, "Purkyne" or "Purkinje," is accepted.

📎

This whole spec point is really two separate systems, and it pays to keep them apart in an answer: the heart's own intrinsic wiring described above (which sets the rhythm and needs no nervous input at all), and the autonomic nervous system covered in Sections 2–3 (which only ever adjusts how fast that intrinsic rhythm runs).

Section 2 · spec content point

🧭 Chemoreceptors & baroreceptors

Two separate receptor types keep constant watch on the blood, in the same general area of the body — mixing them up, or leaving out their location, is one of the easiest ways to lose marks here.

📖

What the specification says: "The roles and locations of chemoreceptors and pressure receptors and the roles of the autonomic nervous system and effectors in controlling heart rate." (This section covers what each receptor detects and where it's found — Section 3 covers the autonomic nervous system and effector half of this same content point.)

1
2
3

Picture two different watchmen stationed in the same stretch of blood vessel, each checking for a different kind of trouble. One sniffs the blood for a build-up of a particular chemical — a bit like a smoke alarm. The other feels for how hard the blood is pushing against the vessel wall — a bit like a pressure gauge. Neither watchman does anything about what they find themselves; they just report it onward.

  • Chemoreceptor = the "smoke alarm" watchman, checking a chemical change
  • Baroreceptor (pressure receptor) = the "pressure gauge" watchman, checking a mechanical change
  • Aorta & carotid arteries = the main blood vessels both watchmen are stationed in

Chemoreceptors detect a chemical change in the blood — specifically a rise in carbon dioxide concentration, which shows up as a fall in blood pH (e.g. during exercise, when respiring muscles produce more CO₂). Baroreceptors, also called pressure receptors, detect a mechanical change instead — any change in blood pressure. Both types sit in the same general places: the walls of the aorta and the carotid arteries, with a well-known baroreceptor cluster called the carotid sinus. Neither receptor acts directly on the heart — both simply send impulses onward to the medulla, which decides what to do with the information.

ReceptorDetectsLocation
ChemoreceptorsA rise in blood CO₂ concentration / fall in blood pH (e.g. during exercise)Walls of the aorta and carotid arteries (the aortic and carotid bodies)
Baroreceptors (pressure receptors)A change in blood pressureAorta and carotid arteries, e.g. the carotid sinus

Both receptor types sit in the same general blood vessels and both do the same job in the chain: they send impulses to the medulla — never directly to the SAN. The medulla is always the coordinator sitting in between the receptor and the effector.

▶ 🚩 Trap — don't mix up the two receptors

Chemoreceptors detect a chemical change (CO₂ / pH); baroreceptors detect a mechanical change (pressure). Calling a baroreceptor a chemoreceptor, or vice versa, loses the mark even if the location given is otherwise correct. The A-level context for chemoreceptors here is specifically CO₂ / pH — they are not being tested on detecting oxygen concentration at this spec point.

📌

Marking habit worth building now: naming "a receptor" with no further detail caps the mark — always give the receptor's name, what it detects, and its location together, in the same sentence.

Section 3 · spec content point

⚡ The autonomic nervous system & the full reflex chain

The medulla is the coordinator that turns what the receptors detect into a change in heart rate, via two competing nerves. Learn the whole chain as one continuous sentence, receptor to response — this is the hardest application skill on this spec point.

📖

What the specification says: "The roles and locations of chemoreceptors and pressure receptors and the roles of the autonomic nervous system and effectors in controlling heart rate." (Same content point as Section 2 — this half covers the autonomic nervous system, the effector, and the complete reflex chain.)

1
2
3

Imagine a control room (the medulla) that constantly reads reports coming in from the two watchmen in Section 2. Based on what it hears, the control room can press one of two remote-control buttons connected to the heart's own pacemaker: a "speed up" button, or a "slow down" button — never both at once, and never neither. Pressing "speed up" releases one chemical at the pacemaker; pressing "slow down" releases a different one. Whichever button gets pressed, the heart rate changes, which in turn changes what the watchmen are reporting — so the whole system settles back down again.

  • Medulla (oblongata) = the control room / coordinator
  • Sympathetic nerve = the "speed up" button — releases noradrenaline
  • Parasympathetic (vagus) nerve = the "slow down" button — releases acetylcholine
  • SAN = the pacemaker being remote-controlled

The medulla oblongata — specifically its cardiovascular/cardiac centre — is the coordinator. It receives impulses from the chemoreceptors and baroreceptors, and sends impulses onward to the SAN along two different nerves that pull in opposite directions. The sympathetic nerve is the "accelerator": it releases noradrenaline at the SAN, and heart rate increases. The parasympathetic nerve — better known as the vagus nerve — releases acetylcholine at the SAN instead, and heart rate decreases. Whichever branch is sending more impulses at any moment wins out, and the SAN (the effector) responds by changing its own rate of spontaneous excitation.

BranchNeurotransmitterEffect on heart rate
Sympathetic ("accelerator")NoradrenalineIncreases ↑
Parasympathetic (vagus)AcetylcholineDecreases ↓

The medulla oblongata (the cardiovascular/cardiac centre) is the coordinator: it receives impulses from the receptors via a sensory neurone, and sends impulses onward to the SAN via two nerves — which is exactly why a complete answer must always name which branch is involved.

Sympathetic nerve — the "accelerator"NoradrenalineIncreases the frequency of impulses from the SAN → heart rate rises

✅

The antagonistic pair — never swap these. Sympathetic = noradrenaline = speeds the heart up. Parasympathetic (vagus) = acetylcholine = slows the heart down. Both nerves originate from the cardiac centre in the medulla; the resting heart rate is simply the balance between the two.

Learn this as one continuous sentence, receptor to response — and always give the direction of impulse travel: from the receptor to the medulla, and from the medulla to the SAN, never the reverse.

The full chain, worked both directions

Increase: chemoreceptors, in the walls of the aorta and carotid arteries, detect a rise in blood CO₂ concentration / fall in blood pH (e.g. during exercise) → impulses travel via the sensory neurone to the cardiovascular/cardiac centre in the medulla oblongata → more impulses travel along the sympathetic neurone to the SAN → heart rate increases.

Decrease: baroreceptors (pressure receptors), in the aorta and carotid arteries (e.g. the carotid sinus), detect a rise in blood pressure → fewer sympathetic / more parasympathetic (vagus) impulses travel to the SAN → heart rate decreases.

See it happen: which branch fires?

Exercise — CO₂ rises
Recovery — CO₂/BP normal
Blood pressure rises

Both receptor types report only to the medulla, never to the SAN directly. Pick a scenario above to see which branch dominates.

Exercise as a full negative-feedback loop

During exercise, muscles respire faster, producing more CO₂, which lowers blood pH. Chemoreceptors detect this fall in pH and send more impulses to the cardiac centre in the medulla, which sends more impulses along the sympathetic neurone to the SAN, increasing heart rate — raising blood flow to the lungs and tissues so CO₂ is removed and O₂ delivered faster. Once CO₂ falls back to normal, fewer impulses are sent along the sympathetic pathway (and more along the parasympathetic/vagus pathway), lowering heart rate again — a complete negative-feedback loop, working in both directions.

▶ 🚩 Trap — the most commonly confused pairing

Confusing sympathetic and parasympathetic effects on heart rate is a well-known error, as is muddling the direction of impulse travel (from the receptor to the medulla, and from the medulla to the SAN — never the reverse). A described "receptor" with no name or location caps the mark, and reject "signals" or "messages" wherever "impulses" is wanted — always specify more or fewer impulses, not just "an impulse."

🔎

Whatever the scenario, always trace the effect all the way through to the SAN — describing a general symptom (e.g. "heart rate becomes irregular") without tracing the specific effect on impulses reaching the SAN misses the marks the question is actually testing.

Section 4 · exam skills

🧮 Cardiac output & the pulse-rate investigation

No required practical is numbered directly against this spec point — instead, two specific skills opportunities are named: the cardiac output calculation, and a student-designed investigation into a named variable's effect on human pulse rate.

Calculating cardiac output

Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume, written CO = R × V in AQA's own notation (R = heart rate, V = stroke volume).

  • Stroke volume = the volume of blood pumped out of the left ventricle in one beat.
  • Heart rate = the number of beats per minute.
  • Rearranged: R = CO ÷ V, and V = CO ÷ R.

Practise rearranging the equation in every direction, and watch for the recurring trap of converting between cm³ and dm³ min⁻¹ — AQA often gives one quantity in one unit and expects the answer in the other.

Why a trained athlete has a lower resting heart rate

Training increases the size and muscular strength of the heart (cardiac hypertrophy), which increases stroke volume — more blood leaves the heart with each beat. Because cardiac output stays the same before and after training, the same cardiac output can be maintained at a lower heart rate.

🚩

"A stronger heart" on its own is not enough — the explanation must be linked explicitly to an increase in stroke volume, not left as a vague description of general fitness.

Designing a pulse-rate investigation

  • Identify a clear independent variable (the named factor being changed, e.g. exercise intensity, caffeine dose, temperature, or body position) and dependent variable (pulse rate, measured over a fixed time and converted to beats per minute).
  • Control other variables that could affect pulse rate — time since eating, prior activity, room temperature, and so on.
  • Take repeats and calculate a mean to improve reliability.
  • Consider ethical issues — informed consent, health screening of participants, and the right to stop the investigation at any time.

Interpreting heart-rate data

Data are commonly presented with error bars; use their size and overlap to comment on the variability of the data and whether an apparent difference between two means is likely to be significant — non-overlapping bars suggest a real, reliable difference. A placebo group is included so that any change in the treatment group can be attributed to the variable being tested, rather than to participants' expectation of an effect (a psychological effect of believing they received the treatment).

▶ Where this point sits in difficulty terms

This spec point mixes a well-secured recall load (the four named structures of the intrinsic pathway, in the correct order) with a genuinely harder application skill: reproducing the complete receptor-to-SAN reflex chain, correctly naming the branch involved and the direction of impulse travel every time. A second, related weak spot examiner reports flag is linking these electrical events to the pressure/volume changes of the cardiac cycle (3.3.4.1) — practise stating explicitly which electrical event (SAN firing, the AVN delay, ventricular depolarisation via the Purkyne fibres) causes which pressure change (atrial systole, the isovolumetric phase, ventricular systole).

Section 6 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

This content works well as a supporting paragraph inside a longer essay-style answer on more than one theme — the trick is closing each paragraph with a sentence that ties the mechanism back to whatever the title is actually about, rather than repeating a generic summary.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around negative feedback / homeostasis

Heart rate is a clear example of negative feedback operating on a rapid, second-by-second timescale. A rise in blood CO₂ concentration during exercise is detected by chemoreceptors in the aorta and carotid arteries, which send more impulses to the cardiovascular centre in the medulla; more impulses then travel along the sympathetic neurone to the SAN, increasing heart rate and so increasing the rate at which CO₂ is removed from the blood. Once CO₂ returns to normal, the stimulus to the chemoreceptors falls, and fewer sympathetic impulses are sent — heart rate falls back towards its resting value. This shows the coordinator, here the medulla, continuously comparing the current value of a monitored variable against a set point and adjusting an effector accordingly, exactly as any negative-feedback system does.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around cycles / repeating processes

The heart is myogenic: the SAN initiates each wave of electrical activity, which is delayed at the AVN — allowing the atria to empty before the ventricles contract — before passing down the bundle of His and Purkyne tissue to trigger ventricular contraction from the base upward. This sequence repeats automatically with every cardiac cycle, and its rate is continuously adjusted by chemoreceptors and baroreceptors acting via the medulla and the sympathetic/parasympathetic nerves to the SAN. The SAN effectively restarts the electrical sequence at the beginning of every single beat, which is exactly what makes the conducting system the mechanism that drives the cardiac cycle to repeat continuously, rather than a one-off event.

Whichever essay theme this content is used for, treat it as the "mechanism" paragraph: state the process precisely — naming the SAN, AVN, bundle of His and Purkyne fibres, or the receptor-to-SAN reflex chain, depending on what the title is asking about — then add one sentence linking it explicitly back to that theme.

Memory hook — intrinsic rhythm, extrinsic rate. The conduction pathway SAN → both atria → AVN (delay) → bundle of His → Purkyne fibres (base upwards) sets the heart's own myogenic rhythm and needs no nervous input at all. The autonomic nervous system only adjusts how fast that rhythm runs: sympathetic = noradrenaline = speeds up; parasympathetic (vagus) = acetylcholine = slows down — triggered by chemoreceptors (CO₂/pH) and baroreceptors (pressure) in the aorta and carotid arteries, reporting to the medulla, never directly to the SAN.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Myogenic heart & conduction pathway

❓ Describe the intrinsic conduction pathway of the heart.
The SAN initiates the heartbeat, acting as the pacemaker; it sends a wave of electrical activity across the atria, causing atrial systole; non-conducting tissue between the atria and ventricles prevents the impulses reaching the ventricles directly; the AVN delays the electrical activity; this allows the atria to empty before the ventricles contract; the AVN then sends the wave down the bundle of His and Purkyne fibres; this causes the ventricles to contract from the base upwards — ventricular systole. One mark is available simply for the correct sequence.
❓ Explain why the AVN delays the wave of electrical activity before it reaches the ventricles.
The delay ensures the atria have fully emptied — and the ventricles have finished filling — before ventricular contraction begins; without it, the atria and ventricles would contract at the same time and blood would not be pumped efficiently.
❓ Explain why the ventricles contract from the base upwards rather than from the top down.
Purkyne tissue in the bundle of His carries the wave of excitation down the septum to the base of the heart before it spreads upward through the ventricle walls; contracting from the bottom up forces blood upward into the arteries — the aorta and pulmonary artery — which leave at the top of the heart.

Chemoreceptors & baroreceptors

❓ Name the two types of receptor involved in controlling heart rate, and state what each detects and where each is located.
Chemoreceptors detect a rise in blood CO₂ / a fall in blood pH, and are located in the walls of the aorta and carotid arteries (the aortic and carotid bodies). Pressure receptors (baroreceptors) detect a change in blood pressure, and are located in the aorta and carotid arteries, e.g. the carotid sinus.
❓ A student describes a receptor in the aorta as "detecting when the heart needs to speed up." Rewrite this using precise, full-credit wording.
Chemoreceptors in the walls of the aorta and carotid arteries detect a rise in blood CO₂ concentration (a fall in blood pH); baroreceptors in the same vessels detect a change in blood pressure — neither receptor "decides" to speed the heart up itself, they only send impulses to the medulla.
❓ Explain why chemoreceptors would not be credited for "detecting oxygen concentration" at this spec point.
The chemoreceptors relevant to heart rate control respond to a rise in carbon dioxide concentration (or the resulting fall in pH), not to oxygen concentration directly; a description referring to oxygen instead of CO₂/pH will not be credited.

Autonomic nervous system & reflex chain

❓ Explain how heart rate is increased via a named receptor, through to the effector.
Chemoreceptors, in the walls of the aorta and carotid arteries, detect a rise in blood CO₂ concentration (fall in blood pH); impulses travel via the sensory neurone to the cardiovascular/cardiac centre in the medulla oblongata; more impulses then travel along the sympathetic neurone to the SAN; heart rate increases.
❓ Explain how a rise in blood pressure leads to a decrease in heart rate.
Baroreceptors (pressure receptors), in the aorta and carotid arteries (e.g. the carotid sinus), detect the rise in blood pressure; more impulses travel to the cardiovascular/cardiac centre in the medulla; more impulses then travel along the parasympathetic (vagus) neurone to the SAN; heart rate decreases; this reduces blood pressure back towards normal, completing a negative-feedback loop.
❓ Name the two branches of the autonomic nervous system involved in heart rate control, the neurotransmitter each releases at the SAN, and the effect of each on heart rate.
The sympathetic nerve releases noradrenaline at the SAN, increasing the frequency of impulses from the SAN and so increasing heart rate. The parasympathetic nerve (the vagus nerve) releases acetylcholine at the SAN, decreasing the frequency of impulses from the SAN and so decreasing heart rate. Both nerves originate from the cardiac centre in the medulla oblongata, and the two branches are antagonistic.

Cardiac output & the pulse-rate investigation

❓ A person has a stroke volume of 70 cm³ and a heart rate of 72 beats per minute. Calculate their cardiac output in dm³ min⁻¹.
Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume = 72 × 70 = 5040 cm³ min⁻¹; converting to dm³ min⁻¹ (÷1000) gives 5.04 dm³ min⁻¹.
❓ Explain why a trained athlete typically has a lower resting heart rate than an untrained person, despite having a similar resting cardiac output.
Training increases the size and muscle mass of the heart (cardiac hypertrophy), increasing stroke volume — more blood is pumped out of the left ventricle per beat; because cardiac output (heart rate × stroke volume) remains the same, the same cardiac output can be maintained at a lower heart rate.
❓ A study into the effect of caffeine on pulse rate includes a placebo group. Explain the purpose of this group, and suggest one ethical consideration for the investigation.
The placebo group ensures that any change in pulse rate in the treatment group can be attributed to caffeine itself, rather than to participants' expectation of an effect (a psychological response to believing they had taken caffeine). Ethical consideration: informed consent must be obtained, and participants should be screened for any relevant heart conditions before taking part, with the right to withdraw at any time.
