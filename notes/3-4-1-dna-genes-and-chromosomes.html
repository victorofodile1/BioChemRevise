DNA, Genes and Chromosomes

How DNA is organised into genes and chromosomes, why eukaryotic and prokaryotic DNA look so different down a microscope, and the three rules that make the genetic code work — the vocabulary every later 3.4 and 3.8 spec point assumes you already know precisely.

The single habit that protects the most marks on this spec point: locus is defined in the very first idea of this page, and it is still one of the worst-scored one-mark definitions on the whole specification — well under a fifth of candidates nationally get it right, with wrong answers guessing "where proteins are stored" or "where tRNA is made." Fix both, and you've fixed most of what goes wrong here.

Section 1 · spec content point

🦠 DNA in prokaryotic cells

The simplest packaging job on the whole specification: one loop of DNA with nothing wrapped around it. The facts here are short, but four specific wrong answers about this exact DNA come up again and again.

📖

What the specification says: "In prokaryotic cells, DNA molecules are short, circular and not associated with proteins."

1
2
3

Imagine a single elastic band, joined end to end so it's a loop, floating loose in a room with nothing wrapped around it. That's prokaryotic (bacterial) DNA — no wrapping paper, no spools, just a bare loop drifting free inside the cell. Some bacteria also carry a few extra, much smaller loops nearby — spare elastic bands with useful bonus instructions on them.

  • The big loop = the main bacterial chromosome
  • The small extra loops = plasmids
  • "Naked" DNA = no wrapping (no protein) around it at all

Prokaryotic DNA is short, circular (a closed loop, not two free ends), and not associated with protein — biologists call this "naked" DNA, meaning there are no histones wrapped around it. Because a prokaryotic cell has no nucleus, this loop lies free in the cytoplasm. Many prokaryotic cells also carry one or more small circular plasmids, separate from and much smaller than the main loop, which can be copied and passed on independently. A handful of other structures often turn up alongside this DNA in exam questions: a protective capsule (or glycocalyx) around the outside of the cell wall, a whip-like flagellum for movement, a hair-like pilus used for attaching to surfaces or exchanging DNA, and smaller 70S ribosomes than a eukaryotic cell's.

  • Prokaryotic DNA is short, circular and not associated with proteins — a single closed loop, usually called the (main) bacterial chromosome, lying free in the cytoplasm since there is no nucleus to contain it.
  • Many prokaryotes also carry one or more plasmids: small, separate circular loops of DNA, clearly smaller than the main chromosome, that can be copied and inherited independently of it.

The extended prokaryotic feature list

StructureWhat it is
Capsule / glycocalyxA protective outer layer surrounding the cell wall
Plasmid(s)Small circular DNA loops, separate from the main chromosome
FlagellumA whip-like structure used for movement
PilusA hair-like structure used for attachment or DNA exchange

This kind of recall question is usually capped at two or three marks, so naming any two or three of these features is normally enough for full credit — you don't need the whole list every time.

▶ 🚩 Trap — never write "capsid" for a bacterial capsule

A capsid is the protein coat of a virus — never a bacterial structure. The bacterial equivalent is a capsule (or glycocalyx). The two terms sound alike but are consistently and explicitly treated as different structures belonging to different kinds of particle.

▶ 🚩 Trap — four misconceptions about prokaryotic DNA that recur nationally

Never write that prokaryotic DNA is single-stranded (it is double-stranded, exactly like eukaryotic DNA — it's the shape and protein association that differ, not the number of strands), that it contains uracil (uracil belongs to RNA, not DNA, in either type of cell), that it is "not replicated semi-conservatively" (semi-conservative replication applies to prokaryotic DNA too), or that "viruses are prokaryotes" (viruses are not cells at all, and are not classified as prokaryotic or eukaryotic).

Section 2 · spec content point

🧵 Eukaryotic nuclear DNA & the chromosome

Unlike the bare loop in Section 1, eukaryotic nuclear DNA has a genuine packing problem — it has to fit an enormous amount of DNA into a tiny nucleus without losing track of the sequence. The solution to that problem is the chromosome, and its definition is worth learning word-for-word.

📖

What the specification says: "In the nucleus of eukaryotic cells, DNA molecules are very long, linear and associated with proteins, called histones. Together a DNA molecule and its associated proteins form a chromosome."

1
2
3

Now imagine a piece of thread so long it would never fit in a drawer on its own — that's eukaryotic DNA. To store it, it gets wound around tiny spools (proteins called histones), like thread wound around cotton reels. Reel after reel after reel packed together and coiled up tightly makes one tidy, condensed bundle — and that whole bundle, thread plus spools together, is what's called a chromosome.

  • The thread = the DNA molecule
  • The spools = histone proteins
  • Thread + spools together, packed up = a chromosome

Eukaryotic nuclear DNA is very long and linear (it has two free ends, unlike the closed loop of prokaryotic DNA), and it is associated with proteins called histones. The DNA winds around clusters of histones to form bead-like units called nucleosomes, which then coil further into chromatin, and condense further still into a fully packaged chromosome. A chromosome, precisely, is the DNA molecule together with its associated histone proteins — not the DNA on its own.

Packaging stageWhat's happening
Linear DNA double helixThe raw, unpackaged molecule
NucleosomesDNA wound around histones — "beads on a string"
ChromatinNucleosomes coiled further
ChromosomeFully condensed — DNA + its histones, together
🎯

Define a chromosome — word-perfect: a DNA molecule together with its associated histone proteins. One-mark questions specifically catch students who answer only "a strand of DNA" — the histones are part of the definition itself, not an optional extra.

Eukaryotic nuclear DNA is very long and linear — it has two free ends, in contrast to the closed loop of prokaryotic DNA (Section 1) — and is associated with proteins called histones. Together, a DNA molecule and its associated histones form a chromosome.

Linear DNA winds around histone proteins to form nucleosomes ("beads on a string"), which coil further into supercoiled chromatin, which condenses further still into a chromosome — the structure that becomes visible under a light microscope once stained.
🔬

Why this page underpins the root-tip squash practical: no AQA required practical is tied directly to this spec point, but the mitosis root-tip squash practical only makes sense once "chromosome" means something precise under the microscope — a DNA molecule with its associated histones, condensed enough to become visible once stained. Without that definition, the dark, thread-like structures being counted in a stained root-tip image have no explanation for why they're called chromosomes at all.

Section 3 · spec content point

🔋 Mitochondrial & chloroplast DNA

This section completes a three-way comparison examiners test directly: organelle DNA is structurally identical in kind to the prokaryotic DNA of Section 1 — not to the nuclear DNA of Section 2 — and that similarity is itself a piece of evidence.

📖

What the specification says: "The mitochondria and chloroplasts of eukaryotic cells also contain DNA which, like the DNA of prokaryotes, is short, circular and not associated with protein."

1
2
3

Mitochondria and chloroplasts each carry their own tiny loop of DNA — and it's the bare-loop kind from Section 1, not the wound-round-spools kind from Section 2. That's a big clue about where these organelles originally came from: scientists think they were once free-living bacteria-like cells that got swallowed up and started living inside a bigger cell, keeping their own bacterial-style DNA loop the whole time.

Mitochondria and chloroplasts each contain their own DNA, and it is short, circular and not associated with protein — structurally the same kind of DNA as a prokaryotic cell's, not the long, linear, histone-wrapped DNA found in the nucleus. This is treated as evidence for the endosymbiotic theory: the idea that mitochondria and chloroplasts were once free-living prokaryotic cells that were engulfed by, and began living inside, a larger host cell.

Mitochondrial and chloroplast DNA is short, circular and not associated with protein — like prokaryotic DNA, and unlike the eukaryotic nuclear DNA that shares the same cell.

▶ 🔬 Why this matters — the endosymbiotic theory

Organelle DNA being structurally identical in kind to prokaryotic DNA (short, circular, naked) is cited as evidence supporting the endosymbiotic theory: that mitochondria and chloroplasts originated as free-living prokaryotic cells, engulfed by a larger ancestral cell, which then evolved a mutually dependent relationship rather than being digested.

✍️

Writing the comparison correctly: a "contrast" question always wants genuinely comparative sentences covering both sides together, never two separate lists. For example: "eukaryotic nuclear DNA is very long and linear, and is associated with histone proteins forming a chromosome, whereas prokaryotic DNA — and mitochondrial/chloroplast DNA — is short and circular, and not associated with protein." One-sided facts about only one DNA type, however accurate, score nothing for a comparative point. (Section 8 covers this exam-technique habit in full.)

Section 4 · spec content point

🧩 What a gene is

A gene definition has two halves, and the second one is the part almost everyone forgets to include.

📖

What the specification says: "A gene is a base sequence of DNA that codes for: the amino acid sequence of a polypeptide; a functional RNA (including ribosomal RNA and tRNAs)."

1
2
3

Think of the whole DNA molecule as a giant recipe book. A gene is just one recipe inside it — a stretch of instructions written in DNA "letters" that tells the cell how to build one particular thing. Most recipes build a protein chain. But a few recipes build something else instead — a working piece of RNA, used directly, rather than a protein at all.

A gene is a base sequence of DNA that codes for something functional. Most genes code for the amino acid sequence of a polypeptide — the chain that folds up into a protein. But a gene can instead code for a functional RNA molecule that is used directly, without ever being made into a protein — this includes ribosomal RNA (rRNA), part of a ribosome's structure, and transfer RNA (tRNA), which carries amino acids during translation. Both possibilities are part of what "gene" means — not just the polypeptide half.

A gene is a base sequence of DNA that codes for either of two things:

  • the amino acid sequence of a polypeptide, or
  • a functional RNA — a category that explicitly includes ribosomal RNA and tRNA.
▶ 🚩 Trap — leaving out the functional-RNA half

Answering "codes for a protein" and stopping there is not a full definition of a gene — it captures only the first half. Because genes for rRNA and tRNA don't code for a polypeptide at all, the functional-RNA half has to be present too for a definition to be complete. Also note: "codes for a protein" is a looser way of saying "codes for a polypeptide" — a protein may be made of one or more polypeptide chains, so "polypeptide" or "amino acid sequence" is the more precise term to use.

🔗

Genes coding for rRNA and tRNA rather than a polypeptide is exactly why "gene expression" can't always be summarised as simply "DNA → protein" — a small but examinable minority of genes stop at a functional RNA product instead, a distinction this same page returns to when triplet, codon and anticodon are separated by molecule in Section 6.

Section 5 · spec content point

📍 The locus

This is the single costliest recall mark on this entire spec point — defined in the very first idea students meet here, and still one of the worst-scored one-mark definitions nationally.

📖

What the specification says: "A gene occupies a fixed position, called a locus, on a particular DNA molecule."

1
2
3

If a gene is a recipe in the recipe book (Section 4), a locus is simply that recipe's page number — the fixed spot in the book where it always lives. It's just an address, not the recipe itself. And if two people have slightly different versions of the same recipe on the same page number — say, one version makes the dish spicier — those different versions are called alleles.

A locus is the fixed position of a gene on a particular DNA molecule or chromosome — it names a location, not a thing. An allele is a different version of the base sequence found at that same locus — so locus (a position) and allele (a version found there) are tested as a linked pair, and neither should be confused with an exon (a coding region within a gene, covered in Section 7) — all three are simple recall definitions that get muddled surprisingly often.

🎯

Define a locus — word-perfect: the fixed position of a gene on a (particular) DNA molecule / chromosome.

An allele is a different version of a gene — a variant of the base sequence found at that same locus. Because alleles differ in base sequence, they may code for a different amino acid sequence, and therefore a different polypeptide.

▶ 🚩 The trap — the mark almost nobody scores

Wrong answers to "define a locus" typically guess "where proteins are stored" or "where tRNA is made" — both describe something else entirely. A locus is a position, full stop; it says nothing about storage or synthesis. National examiner data on this exact one-mark question puts the correct-answer rate well under a fifth of candidates, despite the definition sitting in the very first idea of this spec point.

▶ 🚩 Trap — locus, allele and exon get muddled

Keep all three separate: locus = a position (where a gene sits); allele = a version (of the gene at that position); exon = a coding region (a part of the gene itself — see Section 7). All three are one-line recall definitions, and all three are commonly answered wrong by being swapped for one another.

📎

Vocabulary preview — genome and proteome (formally defined later, at 3.4.2, but worth learning precisely now): the genome is the complete set of genes, or all the DNA, in a single cell or organism — never "within a species" or "within a population," since a genome belongs to one cell or organism, not a group of them. The proteome is the range, or number, of different proteins a cell is able to produce — a bare "number of proteins," with no reference to what the genome can produce, is not precise enough.

Section 6 · spec content point

🔤 The triplet code

Three bases, one amino acid — and three named properties that between them explain almost every "why does this mutation matter" question later in the course.

📖

What the specification says: "A sequence of three DNA bases, called a triplet, codes for a specific amino acid. The genetic code is universal, non-overlapping and degenerate."

1
2
3

DNA only has 4 different "letters" to work with, but proteins are built from 20 different amino acid "words." Reading the letters three at a time — a triplet — gives enough different three-letter combinations to cover all 20 words, with plenty to spare. That's why the same word can sometimes be spelled two different ways (degenerate), why every three-letter group is read as a separate word never sharing letters with its neighbour (non-overlapping), and why the same three-letter word means the same thing in basically every living thing on Earth (universal).

A triplet is a sequence of three DNA bases that codes for one specific amino acid. With only 4 possible bases, reading them one at a time gives just 4 combinations, and two at a time gives 16 — neither is enough to specify all 20 amino acids. Reading three at a time gives 64 combinations, comfortably enough. The genetic code built from these triplets has three properties: it is universal (the same triplet codes for the same amino acid in essentially all organisms), non-overlapping (each base belongs to only one triplet, read in one direction), and degenerate (more than one triplet can code for the same amino acid).

Why three bases, not one or two

With 4 possible bases, a 1-base code gives only 4¹ = 4 combinations, and a 2-base code only 4² = 16 combinations — both far short of the 20 amino acids that need specifying. A 3-base (triplet) code gives 4³ = 64 combinations, comfortably more than enough — and it's exactly this surplus over 20 that makes the code able to be degenerate as well.

The three properties — state it, then state the consequence

PropertyWhat it meansWhy it matters
Universal(With rare exceptions) the same triplet codes for the same amino acid in almost every living organismMakes gene technologies such as recombinant DNA possible across species
Non-overlappingEach base forms part of only one triplet, read in one direction in discrete groups of threeA base is never shared between adjacent triplets — the reading frame runs one way only
DegenerateMore than one triplet can code for the same amino acidA base substitution does not always change the amino acid sequence — a "no effect" mutation

Each property is credited as a paired statement: name it, and state its consequence — a bare "the code is degenerate" with no explanation of what that changes is a weaker answer than one that finishes the thought.

Left: a DNA triplet is transcribed into its mRNA codon (T→U), specifying one amino acid. Middle: reading is non-overlapping — fixed, non-shared groups of three. Right: the code is degenerate — GCU and GCC both code for alanine, so some base substitutions change nothing about the protein made.

Keep triplet, codon and anticodon in their own molecules

TermWhere it's found
TripletA group of 3 bases on DNA
CodonThe corresponding group of 3 bases on mRNA
AnticodonThe complementary group of 3 bases on tRNA
▶ 🚩 Trap — codon/triplet confusion is "extremely common"

Using "codon" for a question specifically about a DNA triplet — or the reverse — is one of the most frequently repeated wording errors in this whole area. Always keep your answer inside the correct molecule's vocabulary: triplet on DNA, codon on mRNA, anticodon on tRNA.

▶ Using "degenerate" to explain a "no effect" mutation

Because the genetic code is degenerate, a base substitution may produce a different triplet that still codes for the same amino acid, so the polypeptide's primary structure — and therefore its function — is unaffected. This is one of the most frequently credited "no effect" reasons in mutation questions later in the course (3.4.3, 3.8.1), and it is this spec point's own vocabulary that supplies it.

Section 7 · spec content point

✂️ Non-coding DNA, exons & introns

A eukaryotic gene isn't wall-to-wall coding sequence — and neither is the DNA around it. Two different kinds of "non-coding" show up here, and mixing them up is a common slip.

📖

What the specification says: "In eukaryotes, much of the nuclear DNA does not code for polypeptides. There are, for example, non-coding multiple repeats of base sequences between genes. Even within a gene only some sequences, called exons, code for amino acid sequences. Within the gene, these exons are separated by one or more non-coding sequences, called introns."

1
2
3

Not every part of a gene's recipe (Section 4) is actually used — some of it is more like scribbled-out lines that get skipped when the recipe is read. The bits that ARE used to build the protein are called exons; the skipped bits inside the recipe are introns. And between recipes, in the gaps of the book, there's a whole load of repeated filler text that isn't part of any recipe at all.

Within a gene, only exons code for the amino acid sequence; they're separated from each other by one or more non-coding introns. This is a completely different thing from the non-coding repeats of base sequences that sit between genes — that's repetitive DNA that isn't part of any gene at all. DNA itself also differs from mRNA in several structural ways: DNA is double-stranded, very long, contains thymine, has deoxyribose sugar, shows complementary base pairing, and contains introns as well as exons; mature mRNA is single-stranded, much shorter, contains uracil instead of thymine, has ribose sugar, doesn't base-pair with itself, and — after splicing — contains only exons.

Within a gene, only exons code for the amino acid sequence; exons are separated by one or more non-coding introns. This is distinct from the non-coding multiple repeats of base sequences between genes — repetitive DNA that is not part of any gene at all, and which is the basis of the VNTRs used later in genetic fingerprinting (3.8.4.3).

🗂️

Two different "non-coding" categories — don't conflate them: non-coding between genes = repetitive base sequences with no gene function at all. Non-coding within a gene = introns, interrupting the exons of a single gene.

Section 8 · exam technique

🎯 Common mistakes & precise wording

This spec point is short and almost entirely definitional — which is exactly why it's hard to score well on. The content isn't conceptually difficult; the exact wording attached to each definition is what's actually marked, and evidence from examiner reports shows that wording is very often missed.

▶ 🚩 The hardest transferable skill on this page — writing genuinely comparative sentences

Most students can state the facts about prokaryotic and eukaryotic DNA individually — the actual difficulty is combining them into one sentence with a comparative connective ("X does A, whereas Y does B"), because that is specifically what "contrast" questions are marked against. "Eukaryotic DNA is linear and histone-associated, whereas prokaryotic DNA is circular and naked" scores; "Eukaryotic DNA is linear. Prokaryotic DNA is circular." as two isolated sentences risks losing the comparative mark even though both facts are correct. Before writing any contrast answer, pick a connective (whereas / in contrast / but) and force every sentence through it.

▶ Where the difficulty on this spec point actually comes from

The content itself is short and largely definitional — memorisation load is low-to-medium, and there's very little application depth required. What makes it hard is a combination of high misconception frequency and high terminology/wording-precision risk: "locus" defined in the first idea of the page and still poorly scored nationally; contrast questions answered as two separate lists rather than paired statements; and several specific, recurring misconceptions about prokaryotic DNA. Genome and proteome definitions, threaded in from 3.4.2, are also commonly imprecise — "all the genetic information" with no mention of DNA, or "all the proteins in a cell" with no reference to what the genome can produce.

Section 9 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

This content supplies the opening vocabulary for essay-style questions about DNA as an information-carrying molecule — the units in which that information is organised, before later spec points cover how it's expressed, varied or exploited.

▶ Example paragraph — DNA structure as the foundation of "DNA as information"

Eukaryotic nuclear DNA is very long, linear and associated with histone proteins, forming chromosomes, while prokaryotic and organellar DNA is short, circular and not associated with protein — a structural difference reflecting the very different amount of genetic information each must package. Within a eukaryotic gene, only exons code for amino acids; introns are non-coding sequences separating them, and there is further non-coding, repetitive DNA between genes — so the structure of the DNA molecule directly determines which parts are functional in producing a protein.

Essay technique worth reusing: tie every structural fact explicitly back to information. Histones package a very long molecule so it fits in the nucleus without losing the sequence; introns and exons show that not every base in the sequence is "read"; loci give each piece of information a fixed, findable address. Treat this content as the opening paragraph of any essay theme about DNA's structure — then let it transition into how that information is actually expressed (3.4.2) and altered (3.4.3).

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · DNA in prokaryotic cells

  • Short, circular, not associated with protein ("naked") — one main loop, free in the cytoplasm.
  • May carry plasmids (small extra circular loops), a capsule/glycocalyx, a flagellum, a pilus, and smaller 70S ribosomes.
  • Never write "capsid" (that's viral) — say capsule.
  • Never say prokaryotic DNA is single-stranded, contains uracil, or isn't replicated semi-conservatively.

2 · Eukaryotic DNA & the chromosome

  • Very long, linear, associated with histones.
  • Chromosome = DNA molecule + its associated histone proteins — not just "a strand of DNA."
  • Packaging: linear DNA → nucleosomes ("beads on a string") → supercoiled chromatin → chromosome.

3 · Mitochondrial & chloroplast DNA

  • Short, circular, not associated with protein — like prokaryotic DNA, unlike nuclear DNA in the same cell.
  • Cited as evidence for the endosymbiotic theory.

4 · What a gene is

  • A base sequence of DNA coding for: the amino acid sequence of a polypeptide, or a functional RNA (rRNA, tRNA).
  • "Codes for a protein" alone is not a complete definition — the functional-RNA half is needed too.

5 · The locus

  • Locus = the fixed position of a gene on a particular DNA molecule/chromosome — not "where proteins are stored."
  • Allele = a different version of the gene at that locus.
  • Genome = all the DNA in one cell/organism (never "within a species"); proteome = the range of proteins a cell can produce.

6 · The triplet code

  • 3 bases (4³ = 64 combinations) needed because 1 or 2 bases (4 or 16) can't cover 20 amino acids.
  • Universal (same triplet, same amino acid, almost all organisms), non-overlapping (fixed reading frame, no shared bases), degenerate (more than one triplet per amino acid).
  • Triplet = DNA; codon = mRNA; anticodon = tRNA — never mix these up.

7 · Non-coding DNA, exons & introns

  • Within a gene: exons code, introns don't (and separate the exons).
  • Between genes: non-coding repeats — a different thing from introns, and the basis of VNTRs later.
  • DNA vs mRNA: double- vs single-stranded, longer vs shorter, thymine vs uracil, deoxyribose vs ribose, base-pairs vs doesn't, introns+exons vs exons only (after splicing).
  • Gene length in bases ≠ mRNA length × 3 — introns are removed first.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

DNA in prokaryotic cells

❓ State three features, other than the nature of its DNA, that a prokaryotic cell might have.
Any three of: a capsule or glycocalyx; one or more plasmids; a flagellum; a pilus; smaller 70S ribosomes. (Accept cell wall only if qualified as murein/peptidoglycan. Do not accept "capsid," which is a viral structure.)
❓ A student states that bacterial DNA is single-stranded because it is "simpler" than eukaryotic DNA. Explain why this is incorrect.
Prokaryotic DNA is double-stranded, exactly like eukaryotic DNA — the difference between the two is that prokaryotic DNA is short, circular and not associated with protein, whereas eukaryotic nuclear DNA is very long, linear and associated with histones; strandedness is not what distinguishes them.
❓ Give two structural features of prokaryotic DNA.
It is short, and it is circular; it is also not associated with protein (described as "naked").

Eukaryotic DNA & the chromosome

❓ Define a chromosome.
A DNA molecule together with its associated (histone) proteins. Not simply "a strand of DNA" — the histones are part of the definition.
❓ Describe how a linear DNA molecule becomes a condensed chromosome.
The DNA winds around histone proteins to form nucleosomes ("beads on a string"); these coil further into supercoiled chromatin; further condensation produces the fully packaged chromosome — DNA plus its associated histones.
❓ A diagram shows a molecule described as "very long, linear, and associated with histone proteins." Name the structure this molecule and its proteins together form.
A chromosome.

Mitochondrial & chloroplast DNA

❓ Contrast the DNA found in the nucleus of a eukaryotic cell with the DNA found in its mitochondria. [2 marks]
Nuclear DNA is very long and linear, and is associated with histone proteins forming a chromosome, whereas mitochondrial DNA is short and circular, and not associated with protein. (Contrast requires both parts of the statement together — one-sided facts about only one type score nothing for that point.)
❓ Explain how the structure of mitochondrial DNA provides evidence for the endosymbiotic theory.
Mitochondrial DNA is short, circular and not associated with protein — structurally identical in kind to prokaryotic DNA — supporting the idea that mitochondria originated as free-living prokaryotic cells that were engulfed by, and came to live inside, a larger host cell.

What a gene is

❓ Define a gene.
A base sequence of DNA that codes for the amino acid sequence of a polypeptide, or for a functional RNA — including ribosomal RNA and tRNA.
❓ A student defines a gene as "a sequence of DNA that codes for a protein." Explain why this answer is incomplete.
It only covers the polypeptide half of the definition; some genes code for a functional RNA instead — such as ribosomal RNA or tRNA — which is never translated into a polypeptide at all, so a complete definition must include both possibilities.

The locus

❓ Define the term locus.
The fixed position of a gene on a (particular) DNA molecule / chromosome.
❓ Distinguish between a locus and an allele.
A locus is the fixed position of a gene on a DNA molecule; an allele is a different version (base sequence) of the same gene, found at that same locus.
❓ Define the genome of an organism, and explain why "all the DNA within a species" would not be accepted as an answer.
The genome is the complete set of genes, or all the DNA, in a single cell or organism. "Within a species" is rejected because a genome belongs to one cell or organism, not to a group of them.

The triplet code

❓ Explain why a triplet code, rather than a code based on one or two bases, is needed to specify 20 different amino acids.
With 4 possible bases, a one-base code gives only 4¹ = 4 combinations and a two-base code gives only 4² = 16 combinations, both insufficient for 20 amino acids; a three-base (triplet) code gives 4³ = 64 combinations, which is more than enough.
❓ State and explain the consequence of the genetic code being degenerate.
More than one triplet can code for the same amino acid; as a result, a base substitution mutation may produce a different triplet that still codes for the same amino acid, so the amino acid sequence of the polypeptide — and therefore its function — may be unaffected.
❓ A question refers to "a sequence of three bases on a molecule of tRNA that is complementary to a codon." Name this sequence, and identify which molecule it is found on.
An anticodon, found on tRNA (as distinct from a triplet on DNA or a codon on mRNA).

Non-coding DNA, exons & introns

❓ Define exon and intron.
An exon is a base sequence within a gene that codes for amino acids. An intron is a non-coding sequence within a gene, separating the exons.
❓ Contrast DNA with mRNA. [3 marks]
Any three of: DNA is double-stranded whereas mRNA is single-stranded; DNA is (much) longer than mRNA; DNA contains thymine whereas mRNA contains uracil; DNA's sugar is deoxyribose whereas mRNA's is ribose; DNA shows complementary base pairing whereas mRNA does not; DNA contains non-coding introns as well as coding exons whereas (mature) mRNA does not.
❓ A gene contains 1200 bases and produces an mRNA transcript, after splicing, of only 600 bases. Explain this difference.
The gene contains non-coding introns as well as coding exons; during splicing the introns are removed from the pre-mRNA, so the length of the mature (spliced) mRNA is less than the length of the original gene in DNA bases.
