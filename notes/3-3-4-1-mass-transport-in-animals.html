The single rule that costs the most marks on this whole spec point: a lower affinity for oxygen (a curve shifted right) always means oxygen is unloaded / dissociated / released more readily — never "loaded" more readily. Reject that phrasing wherever the point being made is about unloading, whether it's the Bohr effect, comparing two haemoglobins on a graph, or an application question about carbon monoxide, fetal haemoglobin, myoglobin or altitude.

Section 1 · spec content point

📏 Why animals need mass transport at all

Before any of the detail on haemoglobin or the heart, it's worth being able to justify why a mass transport system exists in the first place — this is the idea every other section in this guide ultimately serves.

📖

What the specification says: "Over large distances, efficient movement of substance to and from by mass transport."

1
2
3

Imagine a tiny cottage where you can reach every room by just leaning over — nothing needs a delivery van. Now imagine a huge tower block: you can't just lean from the ground floor to the top floor, so the building needs lifts and pipes to move things around quickly. A single-celled organism is the cottage — it can just let oxygen and food drift in on their own. A big animal like a human is the tower block — it needs a pumped delivery system (blood) to move things around fast enough, because drifting alone would take far too long.

Diffusion — molecules randomly spreading from a high to a low concentration — works fine over a short distance, but it gets dramatically slower as the distance gets bigger. A large, multicellular animal has cells deep inside its body, far from the surface where oxygen and nutrients come in, so diffusion alone would be far too slow to keep those inner cells supplied. Large animals also tend to have a smaller surface area to volume ratio, which makes the diffusion problem worse still. The solution is a mass transport system: a pump (the heart), a transport medium (blood) and a network of vessels that move substances around the body in bulk, instead of relying on individual molecules wandering there by themselves.

Diffusion rate depends on concentration gradient, surface area and distance — and it falls off sharply as diffusion distance increases, because a molecule's average time to travel a given distance by random movement rises with the square of that distance. Over the few millimetres between neighbouring cells this is fast enough; over the centimetres separating a large animal's gut, lungs and deep tissues, it is not.

  • Larger, more complex animals have a relatively small surface area to volume ratio (fewer cells directly exposed to the exchange surface, relative to the total number of cells needing supply) — this link back to SA:V is exactly the same idea used throughout Topic 3.3.
  • Their cells also have a high metabolic demand, needing a continuous, fast supply of oxygen and glucose and a continuous, fast removal of carbon dioxide and other waste.
  • A mass transport system solves both problems by moving fluid in bulk — driven by a pressure difference created by a pump — rather than relying on the random movement of individual molecules over long distances.
  • In mammals this system is the closed, double circulatory system: a muscular pump (the heart), a transport medium (blood, principally via haemoglobin for oxygen), and a branching network of vessels that keeps the final diffusion distance from any capillary to any cell extremely short.
📌

The idea that ties the whole spec point together: mass transport doesn't replace diffusion — it exists to keep the final diffusion distance short. Blood is pumped in bulk over the long distance, and only the last, very short step (capillary wall to cell) is left to diffusion. Every section below, from haemoglobin's affinity to capillary bed structure, is really this same principle worked out in detail.

Section 2 · spec content point

🧬 Haemoglobin — a quaternary protein built to carry oxygen

A short section with a precise one-mark definition at its centre: know exactly what makes a structure "quaternary," and exactly why iron is the feature that makes oxygen transport possible at all.

📖

What the specification says: "The haemoglobins are a group of chemically similar molecules found in many different organisms. Haemoglobin is a protein with a quaternary structure."

1
2
3

Haemoglobin is like a taxi with four seats, and each seat has its own little magnet (iron) that grabs onto one oxygen passenger. Four seats means one haemoglobin can carry four oxygen molecules at once. The magnet has to be able to let go again too, or the oxygen would never get dropped off where it's needed.

Haemoglobin is a protein built from four separate folded chains joined together — that's what makes its structure "quaternary." Two of the chains are called alpha chains and two are called beta chains, and each one is folded around a small group containing an iron ion. Iron is the key feature: it can change its oxidation state, which lets it grab an oxygen molecule and then release it again later, rather than holding on permanently. With four chains, each carrying one iron-containing group, one haemoglobin molecule can carry four oxygen molecules in total.

  • Haemoglobin is a protein with a quaternary structure — more than one polypeptide chain is what makes a structure quaternary, and this single phrase is the one-mark version of "describe the structure of haemoglobin."
  • Specifically, it is built from four polypeptide chains — two alpha and two beta — each folded around an iron-containing haem group.
  • Each haem group binds one oxygen molecule, so one haemoglobin molecule carries four oxygen molecules — eight oxygen atoms in total.
  • Why iron matters: iron can change oxidation state and bind oxygen reversibly — this is precisely what lets oxygen be both loaded and unloaded, rather than bound to haemoglobin permanently.
📌

Precision worth banking: "haemoglobin contains iron" on its own earns little — the credited answer names the property of iron (reversible binding / changing oxidation state) that makes loading and unloading possible.

▶ 🚩 Trap — a vague "quaternary" answer

"Made of four chains" is good, but if a question specifically asks what makes a structure quaternary in general (not just for haemoglobin), the credited idea is simply more than one polypeptide chain — a protein with a single folded chain, however complex its tertiary structure, is not quaternary.

Section 3 · spec content point

📈 Loading, unloading & the oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve

The S-shape of this curve is not decoration — every part of its shape has a named mechanism behind it, and that mechanism is exactly what "cooperative binding" means.

📖

What the specification says: "The role of haemoglobin and red blood cells in the transport of oxygen. The loading, transport and unloading of oxygen in relation to the oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve. The cooperative nature of oxygen binding to show that the change in shape of haemoglobin caused by binding of the first oxygens makes the binding of further oxygens easier. The effects of carbon dioxide concentration on the dissociation of oxyhaemoglobin (the Bohr effect)." (This section covers the sigmoid curve and cooperative binding — Section 4 covers the Bohr effect half of the same content point.)

1
2
3

Picture haemoglobin's four seats being hard to fill at first, because the first seat is tucked away in the middle and awkward to reach. But once someone squeezes into that first seat, it sort of nudges the taxi's shape so the other three seats become much easier to fill — that's why the graph shoots up steeply once it gets going. Near the lungs, almost every seat is full (that's "loading"). Down in the busy tissues, seats empty out again (that's "unloading") because that's where the oxygen is needed.

The dissociation curve plots how saturated haemoglobin is with oxygen against the partial pressure of oxygen (pO₂) around it. At very low pO₂ the curve starts shallow, because the haem groups sit buried inside the molecule and the first oxygen struggles to bind. Once that first oxygen does bind, it changes haemoglobin's shape slightly, which makes it easier for the next oxygens to bind — this knock-on effect is called cooperative binding, and it's why the middle of the curve rises so steeply. At high pO₂ the curve flattens off again because haemoglobin is running out of empty spaces — it's nearly fully saturated. In the lungs, where pO₂ is high, haemoglobin sits on the flat top and loads oxygen; at respiring tissues, where pO₂ is much lower, it sits partway down the steep section and unloads oxygen instead.

Why the curve is S-shaped (sigmoid)

  • At low pO₂, the first oxygen binds with difficulty, because the haem groups are buried in the centre of the molecule — this produces the shallow start.
  • Binding of the first oxygen changes haemoglobin's shape (its tertiary/quaternary structure).
  • This shape change makes it easier for the next oxygens to bind — this is cooperative binding, and it's why the curve rises so steeply through the middle.
  • At high pO₂ the curve flattens off, because haemoglobin is (almost) fully saturated — nearly every haem group is already occupied.
📌

The word AQA wants: always name the mechanism as cooperative binding, and always state explicitly that the shape change makes subsequent binding easier — not just that the curve "goes up".

Loading in the lungs, unloading at the tissues

  • Loading (uptake / association of oxygen) happens at high pO₂: in the lungs (≈13 kPa), haemoglobin is (almost) fully saturated — it has a high affinity for oxygen in that context, and this "high affinity" point must specifically be linked to the lungs.
  • Unloading (release / dissociation of oxygen) happens at low pO₂, at the tissues (≈4 kPa) — haemoglobin has a comparatively low affinity there and releases most of its oxygen.
  • Unloading is also linked to the higher carbon dioxide concentration found at respiring tissue — see the Bohr effect, next section.

▶ How to read the curve in an unfamiliar-context question

For any question giving an unfamiliar dissociation curve — carbon monoxide poisoning, a different species, an altitude scenario — explicitly state what is different about that curve compared with the standard adult curve (shifted left, shifted right, or a lower maximum saturation) before applying the underlying loading/unloading rule. Answering with only the standard curve's shape, without engaging with what's different in the given context, is the single most common way marks are lost on this content.

Section 4 · spec content point

🫁 The Bohr effect & comparing dissociation curves

The same content point continued: what happens to the curve — and to oxygen delivery — when carbon dioxide concentration changes, and how to read a "compare two haemoglobins" graph question correctly.

📖

What the specification says: "The role of haemoglobin and red blood cells in the transport of oxygen. The loading, transport and unloading of oxygen in relation to the oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve. The cooperative nature of oxygen binding to show that the change in shape of haemoglobin caused by binding of the first oxygens makes the binding of further oxygens easier. The effects of carbon dioxide concentration on the dissociation of oxyhaemoglobin (the Bohr effect)." (Same content point as Section 3 — this half covers the Bohr effect and graph-comparison skill.)

1
2
3

When a muscle is working hard, it makes more carbon dioxide, which makes the blood around it slightly more acidic. That acidity is like a little nudge that tells haemoglobin "let go of your oxygen here, it's needed" — so haemoglobin drops off more oxygen exactly where it's being used most. It's never that haemoglobin "grabs more oxygen" in this situation — it's the opposite: it lets more go.

A higher carbon dioxide concentration makes haemoglobin release oxygen more readily — this is called the Bohr effect. The mechanism is that carbon dioxide reacts with water to form carbonic acid, which releases hydrogen ions and lowers the pH of the blood; that drop in pH lowers haemoglobin's affinity for oxygen, so more is unloaded. This is useful because respiring tissue produces more carbon dioxide exactly where more oxygen is needed, so more oxygen becomes available for aerobic respiration there, delaying the switch to anaerobic respiration. On a graph, a higher carbon dioxide concentration shows up as the whole curve shifting to the right.

Explaining the Bohr effect

  • A higher CO₂ concentration increases oxygen dissociation / unloading — equivalently, it decreases haemoglobin's affinity for oxygen.
  • Mechanism: CO₂ decreases blood pH / increases acidity — carbon dioxide forms carbonic acid, releasing H⁺ ions.
  • Consequence: more oxygen becomes available for aerobic respiration at the tissues, so anaerobic respiration is delayed and less lactate is produced.

Accepted phrasings for dissociation include "more readily," "releases more O₂," and "unloading / release / reduced affinity" — any of these can stand in for each other. On a graph: a higher CO₂ concentration shifts the curve to the right (the Bohr shift) — at any given pO₂, haemoglobin is less saturated, meaning more oxygen has been released.

▶ 🚩 Trap — never mention an active site

Haemoglobin is not an enzyme — explaining the Bohr effect in terms of an "active site" is incorrect and will not be credited. The correct mechanism is a pH-driven change to haemoglobin's affinity, via a shape change, not enzyme–substrate binding.

Comparing two haemoglobins from a graph

On the graphWhat it means
Curve shifted right, same pO₂Lower affinity, less saturated at that pO₂ — more oxygen dissociated / released / unloaded for respiration ("less saturated" can also be phrased as "less oxygen will be bound")
Curve shifted left, same pO₂Higher affinity — dissociates oxygen less readily / associates more readily; this allows respiration to continue at low pO₂, lets the haemoglobin act as an oxygen store, or delays anaerobic respiration and lactate production
🚩

The commonest error in the entire topic: never write "oxygen loaded more readily" or "more oxygen loaded" where the point being made is about unloading. A right-shifted curve always means more oxygen is released, not more oxygen taken up.

Section 5 · spec content point

🌍 Different haemoglobins as adaptations to environment

Four scenarios, one underlying logic: an animal's habitat or physiology sets what oxygen affinity its haemoglobin needs — always reason it through rather than just recalling the table.

📖

What the specification says: "Many animals are adapted to their environment by possessing different types of haemoglobin with different oxygen transport properties."

1
2
3

Different animals need their oxygen taxi to behave differently depending on where they live. An animal living somewhere with very little oxygen needs a taxi that grabs on very easily, even when oxygen is scarce. A baby growing inside its mother needs a taxi that can steal oxygen from its mother's taxi. A small, busy animal that burns energy fast needs a taxi that lets go of oxygen easily, so muscles get fed quickly. A diving animal needs a taxi that holds on tight and only lets go deep underwater, when oxygen is really needed.

An animal's haemoglobin affinity is tuned to match how much oxygen is actually available and how fast its cells need it. In low-oxygen environments (altitude, burrows, mud), a higher-affinity haemoglobin — curve shifted left — can still become saturated even though there's little oxygen around. A fetus needs a higher affinity than its mother's haemoglobin, so oxygen moves across the placenta from mother to baby. A small mammal with a high metabolic rate needs a lower-affinity haemoglobin — curve shifted right — so it unloads oxygen readily to fuel fast respiration. A diving mammal often relies on myoglobin, or a haemoglobin with an extremely high affinity, which acts as an oxygen store and only releases oxygen at very low pO₂, deep into a dive.

SituationAdaptation and reason
Low environmental oxygen (altitude, burrows, mud, polluted water)Higher affinity — curve shifted left. Loads oxygen readily even at low pO₂, so the blood can still become saturated.
FetusHigher affinity than the mother's, so oxygen moves from maternal to fetal blood across the placenta.
Small mammal / high metabolic rateLower affinity — curve shifted right. Unloads oxygen readily at the tissues to supply a high rate of respiration.
Diving mammalMyoglobin, or a haemoglobin with a very high affinity, acts as an oxygen store, releasing oxygen only at very low pO₂.

The surface-area-to-volume link, for small/high-metabolic-rate animals

  • A smaller animal has a larger surface area to volume ratio, so it loses heat more/faster per gram of body mass.
  • It therefore needs a faster rate of respiration/metabolism to replace that heat loss.
  • This is why it evolves a lower-affinity haemoglobin, which unloads oxygen more readily to support that faster respiration rate.
🚩

Trap: "produces/generates heat/energy" is not an acceptable substitute for this reasoning chain — both halves of the comparison (surface-area-to-volume ratio, and rate of heat loss) must be stated as genuinely comparative points, not just asserted.

Section 6 · spec content point

🔁 The general pattern of circulation & named vessels

Only a fixed list of vessels needs to be known by name — but they need to be known precisely, including which vessel carries oxygenated versus deoxygenated blood.

📖

What the specification says: "The general pattern of blood circulation in a mammal. Names are required only of the coronary arteries and of the blood vessels entering and leaving the heart, lungs and kidneys."

1
2
3

Blood doesn't just do one big loop of the body — it goes through the heart twice on every full trip, like a delivery van stopping back at the depot to refuel between two separate delivery routes: one short trip to the lungs to pick up oxygen, and one longer trip around the whole body to drop it off.

Mammals have a double circulatory system: blood passes through the heart twice for every full circuit of the body — once on a short trip to the lungs and back, and once on a longer trip around the rest of the body and back. This matters because it lets blood be re-pressurised by the heart after passing through the low-pressure lung capillaries, so it can still arrive at the body's tissues at high pressure and a fast flow rate. A fixed set of named vessels carries blood into and out of the heart, lungs and kidneys — each organ has one named vessel bringing blood in and one taking it away.

OrganVessel inVessel out
Heart (its own muscle)Coronary arteryCoronary vein
Heart (body circuit)Vena cava (to right atrium)Aorta (from left ventricle)
LungsPulmonary artery (deoxygenated blood)Pulmonary vein (oxygenated blood)
KidneysRenal arteryRenal vein

Double circulation: blood passes through the heart twice per full circuit — once to the lungs, once to the body — so it can be re-pressurised after the low-pressure lung capillaries, maintaining a high pressure and fast flow rate to the body.

📌

Notice the pulmonary vessels are named the opposite way round to what their blood oxygenation might suggest: the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood (away from the heart, to the lungs), and the pulmonary vein carries oxygenated blood (towards the heart, from the lungs) — "artery" and "vein" are defined by direction relative to the heart, not by oxygen content.

Section 7 · spec content point

❤️ The gross structure of the human heart

Four chambers, four valves, and one asymmetry — the left ventricle wall — that always needs a proper explanation, not just a description.

📖

What the specification says: "The gross structure of the human heart. Pressure and volume changes and associated valve movements during the cardiac cycle that maintain a unidirectional flow of blood." (This section covers the gross structure — Section 8 covers the cardiac cycle half of the same content point.)

1
2
3

The heart has four rooms — two upstairs (atria) and two downstairs (ventricles) — with one-way doors (valves) between them so blood can't flow backwards. The left downstairs room has much thicker walls than the right, because it has to squeeze blood all the way around the entire body, while the right side only has to squeeze blood a short trip to the lungs next door.

The heart has four chambers: a right and left atrium above, and a right and left ventricle below. Blood flows from atrium to ventricle through an atrioventricular valve (tricuspid on the right, bicuspid/mitral on the left), and out of each ventricle through a semilunar valve (pulmonary on the right, aortic on the left) — four valves in total, all one-way, and none of them contain muscle. The left ventricle has a visibly thicker wall than the right, because it has to pump blood around the entire body, while the right ventricle only pumps blood the short distance to the lungs; doing more work over a longer, higher-pressure circuit requires a thicker, more muscular wall.

  • Four chambers: right atrium, left atrium (upper), right ventricle, left ventricle (lower).
  • Four valves, all one-way, none containing muscle: tricuspid (right atrioventricular valve), bicuspid/mitral (left atrioventricular valve), pulmonary semilunar valve, aortic semilunar valve.
  • The right ventricle has a thinner wall and pumps blood only to the lungs — a short, lower-pressure circuit.
  • The left ventricle has a visibly thicker wall and pumps blood around the whole body.
  • The coronary artery supplies the heart muscle itself with oxygenated blood.
All four chambers, four valves and the named great vessels — the left ventricle's thicker wall is drawn to emphasise the point tested most often about this diagram.
▶ 🚩 Trap — explaining the thick left ventricle wall

"It pumps harder" or "it pushes more" on its own is not enough. The credited reasoning is two linked points: (1) the left ventricle pumps blood to the whole body — it pumps blood further — and (2) it therefore does more work, producing a greater pressure or force. Reject "push" used alone.

Section 8 · spec content point

🔄 The cardiac cycle — pressure, volume & valve movements

One continuous, comparative pressure sequence — never explain a valve by its size, always by which side of it has higher pressure at that moment.

📖

What the specification says: "The gross structure of the human heart. Pressure and volume changes and associated valve movements during the cardiac cycle that maintain a unidirectional flow of blood." (Same content point as Section 7 — this half covers the cardiac cycle.)

1
2
3

Think of each valve as a one-way door that only opens when someone pushes harder from behind than from in front. As the top rooms of the heart squeeze, blood pressure there gets higher than in the rooms below, so the door between them swings open. Once the bottom rooms squeeze back, pressure flips the other way, and the door swings shut again — over and over, keeping blood moving in one direction only.

Every valve in the heart works on the same rule: it opens when the pressure behind it is higher than the pressure in front of it, and closes when that reverses. First, the atria fill and contract, so atrial pressure rises above ventricular pressure, and the atrioventricular valves open. Then the ventricles contract, atrial pressure is overtaken, and the atrioventricular valves close. As the ventricles keep contracting, their pressure rises above the pressure in the arteries leaving them, so the semilunar valves open and blood is ejected. Finally, as the ventricles relax, artery pressure exceeds ventricle pressure again, and the semilunar valves close. It's the muscle contracting — not just "chambers filling" — that actually causes each pressure rise.

A valve is open when the pressure behind it is higher than the pressure in front, and closed when the reverse is true — never explain a valve by its size. Click a numbered dot, or use Next/Back above, to step through the full nine-point sequence.
▶ 🚩 The single most commonly forgotten point

It is muscle contraction — atrial or ventricular — that causes each pressure increase. Many answers describe only "chambers filling with blood" and never state that contraction itself is what raises the pressure; this link must be stated explicitly.

▶ Marking habits worth knowing

You can start describing the sequence at any point, but the events must stay in the correct order. "Bicuspid" or "mitral" are both accepted for the left AV valve, "aortic valve" is accepted for the aortic semilunar valve, and "blood passes through the valve" can stand in for "the valve is open." Reject "tricuspid" for the left AV valve, and don't rely on "prevents backflow" alone — it must be tied explicitly to the pressure gradient that causes it. Abbreviations like "S-L valve" or "A-V valve" are not accepted where the full valve name is expected.

Calculating heart rate and cardiac output

  • Heart rate (beats per minute) = 60 ÷ length of one cardiac cycle, in seconds — read the cycle length from one identical point on a trace to the next.
  • Cardiac output = stroke volume × heart rate.
  • Stroke volume = maximum ventricle volume − minimum ventricle volume.
🧮

Worked example: if maximum ventricle volume is 120 cm³ and minimum is 40 cm³, stroke volume = 80 cm³. If one cycle takes 0.76 s, heart rate = 60 ÷ 0.76 ≈ 79 beats/min, so cardiac output ≈ 80 × 79 ≈ 6320 cm³/min. Every intermediate step is worth marking even if the final answer is wrong — and it's worth practising rearranging the cardiac output equation in all three directions, since many students can only use it one way round.

📌

Reading valve state from a table or trace: work it out from the pressure logic rather than memorising a pattern — a valve is open whenever the pressure behind it is higher than the pressure in front, and closed whenever the reverse is true.

Section 9 · spec content point

🩸 Arteries, arterioles & veins — structure fits function

A textbook "structure fits function" comparison — every structural feature must be paired with the functional consequence it produces, in the same sentence.

📖

What the specification says: "The structure of arteries, arterioles and veins in relation to their function."

1
2
3

An artery is like a thick, springy fire-hose carrying blood away from the heart at high pressure — its stretchy, muscular wall smooths out each surge. A vein is like a floppy, wide drainpipe bringing blood back at low pressure, with little trapdoors (valves) so blood can't slosh backwards. An arteriole is a smaller, muscly pipe with its own dimmer switch — it can squeeze down to control exactly how much blood reaches a particular area.

Arteries carry blood away from the heart at high pressure, so they have thick walls with a lot of elastic tissue and smooth muscle, and a narrow lumen — the elastic tissue stretches and recoils with every heartbeat, smoothing the flow and helping maintain pressure between beats. Arterioles are smaller branches with relatively more smooth muscle and less elastic tissue than arteries; that muscle can contract to narrow the lumen, controlling exactly how much blood reaches a particular capillary bed — this is the basis of vasoconstriction and vasodilation. Veins carry blood back to the heart at low pressure, so they can afford thin walls and a wide lumen, which reduces resistance; because pressure alone isn't enough to keep blood moving, veins also contain valves to stop it flowing backwards, and rely partly on surrounding skeletal muscle squeezing them.

VesselStructureFunction it serves
ArteryThick wall; thick layer of elastic tissue; thick smooth muscle; narrow lumen; no valves; smooth endotheliumElastic tissue stretches and recoils to smooth the flow and maintain pressure between heartbeats; thick wall withstands high pressure; narrow lumen helps maintain pressure
ArterioleRelatively more smooth muscle, less elastic tissue than an artery; no valvesMuscle contracts to constrict the lumen, controlling the volume of blood allowed into each capillary bed — the basis of vasoconstriction/vasodilation
VeinThin wall; little elastic or muscle tissue; wide lumen; valves presentLow pressure so no need for thick walls; wide lumen gives little resistance; valves prevent backflow; blood is moved mainly by contraction of surrounding skeletal muscle
Wall thickness and lumen width are shown to relative scale — an artery's wall is far thicker and its lumen far narrower than a vein's of similar overall diameter; only the vein has valves.

Elastic recoil and vasodilation, explained precisely

  • Elastic recoil of the aorta wall / artery tissue smooths the blood flow, or maintains the rate of blood flow, or maintains blood pressure — reject "muscle contracting" as the explanation, and don't credit references to muscle relaxing or to preventing backflow, since that describes a valve's job, not elastic recoil.
  • Vasodilation gives a larger lumen / volume in the blood vessel, which produces less friction / resistance, so blood pressure is reduced ("more space/room" and "more blood flow" are accepted equivalents).
▶ 🚩 Two national misconceptions worth eliminating now

First: arterioles do not have valves — only veins do. Arterioles are instead defined by relatively more smooth muscle and less elastic tissue than an artery. Second: vasodilation increases blood flow to a tissue (larger lumen, less resistance) — never describe it as reducing flow.

Section 10 · spec content point

🔬 Capillaries & capillary beds as exchange surfaces

The capillary is the vessel where mass transport hands back over to diffusion — its structure is built entirely around minimising diffusion distance.

📖

What the specification says: "The structure of capillaries and the importance of capillary beds as exchange surfaces. The formation of tissue fluid and its return to the circulatory system." (This section covers capillary structure and exchange — Section 11 covers tissue fluid, from the same content point.)

1
2
3

A capillary's wall is so thin — just one cell thick — that oxygen barely has any distance left to cross to reach a cell. And there are so many capillaries branching through every tissue, like an enormous, incredibly fine network of tiny streets, that between them they still deliver a huge amount of blood to the tissue overall, even though each individual "street" is narrow and slow.

A capillary bed is a branching network of thousands of capillaries running through a tissue, fed by an arteriole and drained by a venule. Each capillary wall is only one cell thick, which keeps the diffusion distance between blood and tissue as short as possible, and the lumen is so narrow that red blood cells are squeezed right up against the endothelium, shortening the diffusion path for oxygen even further. Even though flow through any single capillary is slow, the sheer number of capillaries gives an enormous total surface area, so a large total volume of blood can exchange substances with the tissue every second. Small gaps between neighbouring endothelial cells also allow tissue fluid to form.

  • Wall one cell thick (endothelium only) — minimises the diffusion distance for exchange.
  • Very narrow lumen — red blood cells squeeze against the wall, which slows flow and further shortens the diffusion path for oxygen.
  • Gaps/pores between endothelial cells — allow tissue fluid to form (covered in full in Section 11).
  • Enormous total surface area — a capillary bed is a branching network of thousands of capillaries fed by an arteriole and drained by a venule; even though flow through any one capillary is slow, the total volume of blood exchanging substances with the tissue per second is large.
📌

Wall thickness and lumen diameter are worth being able to compare across all three vessel types on sight: a capillary's diameter is roughly 1/1000 of an artery's — this scale difference is exactly what makes capillaries efficient exchange surfaces and arteries/veins unsuitable for the same job.

Section 11 · spec content point

💧 The formation & return of tissue fluid

A pressure-balance story with two ends: hydrostatic pressure pushes fluid out at the arteriole end, and osmosis pulls most of it back in at the venule end.

📖

What the specification says: "The structure of capillaries and the importance of capillary beds as exchange surfaces. The formation of tissue fluid and its return to the circulatory system." (Same content point as Section 10 — this half covers tissue fluid formation and return.)

1
2
3

Imagine squeezing a wet sponge — water gets pushed out through tiny gaps. That's what the heart does to blood in the capillaries: it squeezes water and small dissolved things out through the gaps in the capillary wall to bathe the surrounding cells. Big things like proteins are too large to leak out, so they stay behind in the blood. Because of that, by the far end of the capillary the blood is a bit "saltier" with protein than the fluid outside, so water gets soaked back in like a sponge re-absorbing liquid. Whatever doesn't get soaked back in drains away through a separate set of pipes (the lymphatic system) and rejoins the blood later.

Tissue fluid forms because contraction of the ventricles creates high hydrostatic (blood) pressure, which forces water and small dissolved substances out through gaps in the capillary wall — plasma proteins are too large to leave, so they stay in the blood. By the time blood reaches the venule end of the capillary, hydrostatic pressure has fallen (from loss of fluid and friction against the capillary lining), while the blood remaining is now more concentrated in protein than the tissue fluid outside — this gives the blood a lower (more negative) water potential, so water moves back in by osmosis. Not all of it returns this way: the remainder drains into the lymphatic system and rejoins the blood near the heart. If either the balance of pressures or the protein concentration is disturbed, excess tissue fluid builds up — this is oedema.

Formation of tissue fluid

  • Contraction of the ventricle(s) produces high blood / hydrostatic pressure.
  • This forces water and some dissolved substances out of the blood capillaries.
  • The net outward pressure at the arteriole end forces small molecules out of the capillary.
🚩

Do not credit "contraction/pumping of the heart" alone — it must specifically be the ventricle. Also reject "blood/plasma/tissue fluid forced out": it is water and dissolved substances that are forced out, and this then becomes tissue fluid.

Return of tissue fluid

  • Water has left the capillary, but plasma proteins are too large to leave it.
  • This gives a higher concentration of blood proteins, so the plasma has a lower (more negative) water potential.
  • At the venule end, hydrostatic pressure has fallen, so water returns to the capillary by osmosis.
  • The remainder drains into the lymphatic system and returns to the blood near the heart.

Why does hydrostatic pressure fall along the capillary? Loss of water/fluid, and friction against the capillary lining.

Explaining oedema (fluid accumulation)

CauseReasoning
High blood pressureHigh blood pressure means high hydrostatic pressure, which increases outward pressure at the arterial end and/or reduces inward pressure at the venule end, so more tissue fluid is formed / less is reabsorbed. ("The lymph system cannot drain the tissues fast enough" is also an accepted explanation.)
Low blood proteinThe plasma has a higher (less negative) water potential, so less water returns by osmosis at the venule end.
🚩

Reject "more tissue fluid is forced out" and "more plasma forced out" — it is fluid/water that is forced out, which then becomes tissue fluid. The essential idea for a summary mark is that excess tissue fluid cannot be reabsorbed / builds up.

📖

1
2
3

For this practical you actually cut open a real heart (or a similar organ) to see the things you've been learning about — thick and thin walled chambers, valves, and the big blood vessels — for real. The exam doesn't usually ask you to write up the dissection like a report; it asks safety questions (how to handle a scalpel safely) and "what is this, and why" questions that test the same theory as the rest of this page.

Required Practical 5, for this spec point, is most often carried out as a dissection of a mammalian heart, or a section through one — sometimes a different mass-transport organ or vessel instead. Exam questions on it focus on safety precautions, technique, and identifying structures, rather than asking you to write up a full method. You're expected to know the standard safety rules for any dissection (sharp tools, cutting away from yourself, a proper board, hygiene, safe disposal), and to be able to link what you observe in the dissection — chamber wall thickness, valve position, which vessel is which — straight back to the structure-function theory covered earlier in this guide.

Safety precautions credited by the mark scheme

  • Use a sharp scalpel or scissors (a blunt blade needs more force, and slips more easily).
  • Cut away from yourself and others.
  • Cut against a hard, non-slip, flat surface (a tile or dissecting board), not held in the hand.
  • Wash hands / wear gloves before and after; disinfect the bench and equipment before and after.
  • Cover any cuts on your own skin before starting.
  • Dispose of the specimen and any sharps safely, following your centre's procedure.
🚩

Vague answers such as "take care with the scalpel" or "keep away from fingers" are too imprecise to be credited — always state the specific precaution. "Wear goggles" is also not a credited point for this particular practical.

Technique & structure identification

  • Identify the chambers by wall thickness — the left ventricle wall is visibly, palpably thicker than the right.
  • Trace each of the four named vessels back to the chamber it enters or leaves.
  • Locate the valves by inserting a blunt probe through each vessel and observing which way it can pass.
  • Relate every structure you cut open back to the structure-function table from Sections 7–9 — a dissection question is very often followed by an "explain why…" question testing the underlying theory, not just naming what you see.
📌

Section 13 · applied exam skill

🫀 Cardiovascular disease, risk factors & correlation vs causation

This content is assessed alongside RP5 as a data-handling and application skill — worth knowing well even though it isn't a standalone content bullet on its own.

The development of atheroma, step by step

  1. Too much saturated fat / cholesterol in the diet leads to an increase in LDL / cholesterol in the blood.
  2. Cholesterol / plaque / lipoprotein / fatty material is deposited in the artery wall, under the lining (endothelium) of the blood vessel — this deposit is called an atheroma.
  3. This reduces the diameter of, or blocks, the affected artery (commonly the coronary artery), and is linked to the risk of a blood clot / thrombosis forming.
  4. Less oxygen and glucose then reach the heart muscle, so that muscle is unable to respire properly, and heart tissue can die.
📌

LDL, triglyceride and cell debris are all accepted as the deposited material, and too much salt or alcohol can be credited as an additional cause. Reject fatty acids and HDL as causes, and don't treat veins or capillaries as equivalent to arteries — if the coronary artery isn't specifically named or described, referring to heart muscle/tissue/cells instead is required.

Distinguishing correlation from causation

  • First describe the correlation shown by the data itself — is it positive or negative, and by how much, quoting the actual figures given.
  • State explicitly that correlation does not prove causation — a correlation shows an association, or possibly a difference in risk, not that one factor directly causes the other, and it is never evidence of "prevention."
  • Suggest that the pattern could instead be explained by another factor — give a plausible, named example rather than leaving it vague.
📌

References to methodology, such as sample size or the line of best fit, are treated as neutral — neither rewarded nor penalised on their own.

🚩

A frequently flagged spelling error: it's "causal," not "casual" — writing "casual relationship" instead of "causal relationship" is marked as an error, not a style choice.

✍️ Using this content in longer written answers

This spec point works well as supporting material inside a longer essay-style answer on more than one theme — the trick is closing each paragraph with a sentence that ties the mechanism explicitly back to whatever the title is actually about.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around diffusion

Loading and unloading of oxygen on haemoglobin depends on the partial pressure gradient of oxygen between air/water and blood, and between blood and respiring tissue — a diffusion-based process, with haemoglobin's affinity tuned by the Bohr effect. 5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Why mass transport at all

  • Diffusion alone is too slow over the large distances inside a big, complex animal.
  • Large animals have a smaller SA:V ratio and high metabolic demand.
  • Mass transport moves fluid in bulk so only the final capillary-to-cell step is left to diffusion.

2 · Haemoglobin structure

  • Quaternary protein: 4 polypeptide chains (2 alpha, 2 beta), each with an iron-containing haem group.
  • 4 haem groups → carries 4 O₂ molecules (8 O atoms) per haemoglobin.
  • Iron can change oxidation state / bind O₂ reversibly — this is why iron matters.
  • "More than one polypeptide chain" = the 1-mark definition of quaternary.

3 · The dissociation curve

  • Shallow start: haem groups buried, 1st O₂ binds poorly.
  • Steep middle: cooperative binding — shape change makes further binding easier.
  • Flat top: (almost) fully saturated.
  • Lungs (high pO₂) = loading, high affinity; tissues (low pO₂) = unloading, low affinity.

4 · The Bohr effect

  • ↑CO₂ → ↓pH (carbonic acid → H⁺) → ↓affinity → more O₂ unloaded.
  • Graph: higher CO₂ shifts curve right.
  • Never mention an "active site" — haemoglobin isn't an enzyme.
  • Never say "loaded more readily" for a right-shift — that's the commonest error in the topic.

5 · Haemoglobins as adaptations

  • Low O₂ environment / fetus → higher affinity, curve left.
  • Small mammal, high metabolic rate → lower affinity, curve right.
  • Diving mammal → myoglobin/very high affinity, releases O₂ only at very low pO₂.
  • Small animal reasoning: ↑SA:V → faster heat loss → faster metabolism → lower-affinity Hb.

6 · Circulation & named vessels

  • Double circulation: through the heart twice per circuit, re-pressurises blood after the lungs.
  • Heart: coronary artery in / coronary vein out. Body: vena cava in / aorta out.
  • Lungs: pulmonary artery (deoxygenated) in / pulmonary vein (oxygenated) out. Kidneys: renal artery in / renal vein out.

7 · Gross structure of the heart

  • 4 chambers, 4 valves (tricuspid, bicuspid/mitral, pulmonary semilunar, aortic semilunar) — all one-way, no muscle.
  • Left ventricle thicker: pumps further (whole body) → more work → greater pressure/force. Never just "pushes harder."
  • Coronary artery supplies the heart muscle itself.

8 · The cardiac cycle

  • A valve is open when pressure behind > pressure in front; closed when the reverse.
  • Atrial > ventricular → AV opens; ventricular > atrial → AV closes; ventricular > aortic → SL opens; aortic > ventricular → SL closes.
  • Muscle contraction causes each pressure rise — don't just say "chambers fill."
  • Heart rate = 60 ÷ cycle length (s); cardiac output = stroke volume × heart rate.

9 · Arteries, arterioles & veins

  • Artery: thick elastic/muscular wall, narrow lumen, no valves — elastic recoil smooths flow.
  • Arteriole: more smooth muscle than artery, no valves — controls flow into capillary beds.
  • Vein: thin wall, wide lumen, valves present — low pressure, aided by skeletal muscle.
  • Arterioles have no valves; vasodilation increases flow (less resistance).

10 · Capillaries & exchange

  • Wall one cell thick, very narrow lumen, gaps between cells, enormous total surface area.
  • Red cells squeeze against wall — shortens diffusion path further.
  • Thousands of capillaries per bed → huge total exchange even though each one flows slowly.

11 · Tissue fluid formation & return

  • Formation: ventricle contraction → high hydrostatic pressure → forces water + small solutes out (never "blood/plasma" out).
  • Return: proteins stay in blood → lower water potential → water re-enters by osmosis at venule end (pressure has fallen).
  • Remainder drains via lymphatic system, back to blood near heart.
  • Oedema: high BP → more forced out/less reabsorbed; low blood protein → less osmotic return.

12 · RP5: dissection

  • Safety: sharp tool, cut away from self, hard non-slip board, hygiene, safe disposal — vague answers not credited.
  • Identify chambers by wall thickness; trace named vessels; probe valves for direction.
  • Almost always paired with a "why" theory question from Sections 7–9.
🧠

Shift right, unload more; shift left, load and hold. A valve is open when pressure behind it is higher. Tissue fluid: water out at the arteriole end, water back in by osmosis at the venule end.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

1 · Why mass transport at all

❓ Explain why a large, complex animal cannot rely on diffusion alone to supply its cells with oxygen.
Diffusion is only efficient over short distances; a large animal has cells far from any exchange surface, and a relatively small surface area to volume ratio, so diffusion alone would be too slow to meet the high metabolic demand of its tissues. A mass transport (bulk flow) system, driven by a pump, is needed to move substances over these longer distances instead.
❓ "Mass transport replaces diffusion in large animals." Explain why this statement is not quite accurate.
Mass transport does not replace diffusion — it exists to keep the final diffusion distance short. Blood is moved in bulk over the long distance by the circulatory system, and only the final step, from capillary to cell, is left to diffusion.

2 · Haemoglobin structure

❓ Describe the structure of a haemoglobin molecule.
Haemoglobin is a protein with a quaternary structure, made of four polypeptide chains — two alpha and two beta — each folded around an iron-containing haem group. Each haem group binds one oxygen molecule, so one haemoglobin molecule carries four oxygen molecules (eight oxygen atoms) in total.
❓ Explain why iron is essential to haemoglobin's function.
Iron can change oxidation state and bind oxygen reversibly, allowing oxygen to be both loaded and unloaded rather than bound permanently.

3 · The dissociation curve

❓ Explain why the oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve has a sigmoid (S) shape.
At low partial pressures of oxygen the haem groups are buried within the molecule, so the first oxygen binds with difficulty. Binding of the first oxygen changes haemoglobin's shape, making it easier for further oxygens to bind — cooperative binding — which produces the steep middle section. At high partial pressures the curve flattens because haemoglobin is almost fully saturated.
❓ Describe how loading and unloading of oxygen differ between the lungs and respiring tissue.
In the lungs, partial pressure of oxygen is high, so haemoglobin has a high affinity for oxygen and becomes almost fully saturated — loading. At respiring tissue, partial pressure of oxygen is low, so haemoglobin has a lower affinity and releases most of its oxygen — unloading; unloading is also promoted by the higher carbon dioxide concentration found at respiring tissue.

4 · The Bohr effect

❓ Explain the Bohr effect.
A higher carbon dioxide concentration decreases haemoglobin's affinity for oxygen, increasing oxygen dissociation/unloading. This happens because carbon dioxide reacts with water to form carbonic acid, releasing hydrogen ions and lowering blood pH; on a graph, this shifts the dissociation curve to the right. The consequence is that more oxygen becomes available for aerobic respiration at respiring tissue, delaying anaerobic respiration and lactate production.
❓ Two dissociation curves are plotted on the same axes; curve B is shifted to the right of curve A. Compare what this tells you about the two haemoglobins at the same partial pressure of oxygen.
At the same partial pressure of oxygen, the haemoglobin represented by curve B has a lower affinity for oxygen and is less saturated than the haemoglobin in curve A, so more oxygen is dissociated/released/unloaded from it for respiration.
❓ A student writes: "at higher carbon dioxide concentrations, haemoglobin's active site changes shape, so oxygen loads more readily." Identify what is wrong with this answer.
Haemoglobin is not an enzyme and does not have an active site — this term should never be used. The statement is also the wrong way round: a higher carbon dioxide concentration lowers haemoglobin's affinity for oxygen, so oxygen is unloaded/released more readily, not loaded.

5 · Haemoglobins as adaptations

❓ Explain why a fetus's haemoglobin has a higher oxygen affinity than its mother's.
Fetal haemoglobin has a higher affinity for oxygen — its dissociation curve is shifted to the left of the mother's. This means that at the partial pressure of oxygen found in the placenta, fetal haemoglobin becomes more saturated than maternal haemoglobin, so oxygen moves from the mother's blood into the fetus's blood.
❓ A small mammal has a much higher metabolic rate than a large mammal. Explain how this is reflected in its haemoglobin, and why.
The small mammal has a lower-affinity haemoglobin, with a dissociation curve shifted to the right, so it unloads oxygen more readily at the tissues. This is because a smaller animal has a larger surface area to volume ratio, so it loses heat faster per gram of body mass, and therefore needs a faster rate of respiration to replace that heat, requiring oxygen to be released more readily.

6 · Circulation & named vessels

❓ Explain the advantage of a double circulatory system over a single circulatory system.
In a double circulatory system, blood passes through the heart twice for each full circuit of the body — once to the lungs and once to the rest of the body. This allows blood to be re-pressurised by the heart after passing through the low-pressure lung capillaries, so it can still reach the body's tissues at a high pressure and fast flow rate.
❓ Name the vessel carrying blood into the right atrium, and the vessel carrying blood away from the left ventricle.
The vena cava carries deoxygenated blood into the right atrium; the aorta carries oxygenated blood away from the left ventricle.

7 · Gross structure of the heart

❓ Explain why the wall of the left ventricle is thicker than the wall of the right ventricle.
The left ventricle pumps blood to the whole body, except the lungs — it pumps blood further than the right ventricle. It therefore does more work and must produce a greater pressure/force, requiring a thicker, more muscular wall.
❓ Name all four valves of the heart and state which chambers or vessels each separates.
Tricuspid valve — between the right atrium and right ventricle. Bicuspid/mitral valve — between the left atrium and left ventricle. Pulmonary (semilunar) valve — between the right ventricle and the pulmonary artery. Aortic (semilunar) valve — between the left ventricle and the aorta.

8 · The cardiac cycle

❓ Explain the pressure changes and valve movements that occur as the atria contract, using comparative pressure language.
As the atria fill and contract, atrial pressure rises above ventricular pressure; because pressure is higher behind the atrioventricular valves than in front of them, the atrioventricular valves open, allowing blood to flow into the ventricles. Muscle contraction of the atria is what causes this rise in pressure.
❓ Explain why the semilunar valves open during ventricular systole.
As the ventricles contract, ventricular pressure rises above the pressure in the arteries leaving them (the pulmonary artery or aorta); since pressure behind the semilunar valves is now higher than pressure in front of them, the semilunar valves open and blood is ejected into the arteries.
❓ A cardiac cycle trace shows the ventricle's volume falling from 130 cm³ to 55 cm³, over a cycle lasting 0.8 s. Calculate the stroke volume, heart rate and cardiac output.
Stroke volume = 130 − 55 = 75 cm³. Heart rate = 60 ÷ 0.8 = 75 beats per minute. Cardiac output = stroke volume × heart rate = 75 × 75 = 5625 cm³ per minute.

9 · Arteries, arterioles & veins

❓ Explain how the structure of an artery wall relates to its function.
An artery has a thick wall containing a thick layer of elastic tissue and smooth muscle, and a narrow lumen. The elastic tissue stretches and recoils with each heartbeat, smoothing the flow of blood and helping to maintain pressure between heartbeats; the thick wall allows the vessel to withstand the high pressure of blood leaving the heart.
❓ A student states that arterioles control blood flow into a capillary bed using valves. Correct this statement.
Arterioles do not have valves — only veins do. Arterioles control blood flow into a capillary bed because they have relatively more smooth muscle than an artery; contraction of this muscle constricts the lumen, restricting flow (vasoconstriction), while relaxation widens it, increasing flow (vasodilation).

10 · Capillaries & exchange

❓ Explain why capillary beds are effective exchange surfaces.
A capillary wall is only one cell thick, minimising the diffusion distance between blood and tissue; the lumen is so narrow that red blood cells are squeezed against the endothelium, shortening the diffusion path for oxygen further. Although flow through any single capillary is slow, the huge number of capillaries in a bed gives an enormous total surface area, so a large total volume of blood exchanges substances with the tissue every second.
❓ State one structural feature of a capillary wall that allows tissue fluid to form.
Gaps/pores exist between adjacent endothelial cells, allowing water and small dissolved substances to pass out of the capillary.

11 · Tissue fluid formation & return

❓ Explain how tissue fluid is formed.
Contraction of the ventricles produces high hydrostatic pressure in the blood. At the arteriole end of the capillary, this creates a net outward pressure that forces water and small dissolved substances out through gaps in the capillary wall; plasma proteins are too large to leave and remain in the blood.
❓ Explain how most tissue fluid is returned to the capillaries.
Because plasma proteins remain in the capillary while water has left, the blood has a higher protein concentration and therefore a lower (more negative) water potential than the surrounding tissue fluid. At the venule end of the capillary, hydrostatic pressure has fallen, so water moves back into the capillary by osmosis, down the water potential gradient. Any fluid not reabsorbed this way drains into the lymphatic system and is returned to the blood near the heart.
❓ Explain why a low concentration of plasma protein can lead to oedema.
Low plasma protein concentration gives the blood a higher (less negative) water potential, reducing the water potential gradient between blood and tissue fluid at the venule end. Less water therefore returns to the capillary by osmosis, so excess tissue fluid accumulates in the tissues.

12 · RP5: dissection

❓ State two safety precautions that should be followed when dissecting a mammalian heart.
Use a sharp scalpel or scissors, since a blunt blade requires more force and is more likely to slip; and cut against a hard, non-slip, flat surface such as a dissecting board, rather than holding the specimen in the hand while cutting.
❓ Describe how you would identify the left ventricle during a heart dissection, and explain the structural reason for the feature you use.
The left ventricle can be identified because its wall is visibly and palpably thicker than the right ventricle's; this is because the left ventricle pumps blood around the whole body, doing more work and producing a greater pressure than the right ventricle, which pumps blood only to the lungs.
