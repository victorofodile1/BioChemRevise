Investigating Diversity

Two toolkits examined side by side: comparing how genetically diverse organisms are — from observable characteristics through to DNA, mRNA and amino acid sequence — and designing, then statistically testing, a quantitative investigation of variation. Short in content, but marked on exact wording throughout.

⚠️

The single habit that protects the most marks on this spec point: when a question restricts you to comparing genetic diversity using something specific — "other than DNA/RNA base sequence," "using amino acid sequence," "using immunological data" — answer using exactly that named method. Defaulting to DNA/RNA sequencing anyway, however good the biology, is not credited. The same "answer exactly what's named" discipline applies to statistical tests: the test name alone is never enough, it always needs its justification stated alongside it.

Section 1 · spec content point

🧬 Comparing genetic diversity — four methods, ranked by directness

AQA gives you exactly four tools for comparing how genetically diverse organisms are, within or between species. The biggest source of lost marks here isn't getting the biology wrong — it's answering with the wrong tool when a question names which one it wants.

📖

What the specification says: "Genetic diversity within, or between species, can be made by comparing: the frequency of measurable or observable characteristics; the base sequence of DNA; the base sequence of mRNA; the amino acid sequence of the proteins encoded by DNA and mRNA."

1
2
3

Imagine trying to work out how different two people are. You could just look at them — hair colour, height, that sort of thing — but people who look alike aren't always closely related, and people who look different sometimes are. A much more trustworthy way is to compare their actual "instructions" — like comparing two recipes word for word, ingredient by ingredient. The fewer differences in the recipe, the more closely related the two things are.

  • The recipe itself = the DNA base sequence
  • A copy of one part of the recipe = the mRNA base sequence
  • What the recipe actually builds = the amino acid sequence of the protein
  • Just looking at the finished dish = comparing observable characteristics — the least reliable option

There are four ways examiners will accept for comparing genetic diversity: comparing the frequency of observable/measurable characteristics, comparing DNA base sequences, comparing mRNA base sequences, and comparing amino acid sequences of the proteins those genes code for. The more differences two organisms have in any of these, the more distantly related they are, and the longer ago they shared a common ancestor. DNA comparison is the most sensitive of the four, because the genetic code is degenerate — some DNA base changes don't actually change the amino acid produced, so a protein-level comparison can miss differences that a DNA-level comparison picks up. That's also part of why gene sequencing has largely replaced just comparing what organisms look like: appearance is affected by environment and can mislead through convergent evolution, whereas DNA is a direct read of the genotype itself.

MethodWhat it actually comparesDirectness / reliability
Frequency of measurable or observable characteristicsThe phenotype — physical or measurable features across a populationLeast direct: phenotype is shaped by the environment as well as the genotype, so it doesn't reliably reflect genetic diversity on its own
Base sequence of DNAThe genotype itself, base by baseMost direct and most sensitive — detects every base difference, including ones that never show up in the protein
Base sequence of mRNAThe transcribed copy of the coding DNAReflects which genes are being expressed as well as their sequence
Amino acid sequence of the encoded proteinThe translated product of DNA/mRNALess sensitive than DNA comparison — some base changes are "invisible" at this level

Interpreting sequence data to rank relatedness

The underlying logic examiners are testing: fewer sequence differences from a reference organism means the two organisms are more closely related, and share a more recent common ancestor. To rank a set of organisms, count how many differences each one has from the reference organism and order them — fewest differences first. A high-scoring answer states this explicitly in terms of the timing of divergence (e.g. an exact percentage similarity, or "shared a common ancestor more recently"); a weak answer just says the organisms "are related" without saying what the data shows about degree or timing of divergence.

Why DNA comparison is more sensitive than protein comparison

Because the genetic code is degenerate (more than one codon can code for the same amino acid), some base changes are synonymous mutations — they leave the amino acid sequence completely unchanged. DNA-level comparison can therefore reveal genetic differences that a protein/amino-acid-level comparison hides entirely, which is exactly why DNA comparison is described as the more sensitive of the two techniques.

Why gene-sequence comparison has replaced relying on observable characteristics

  • Observable/measurable characteristics are influenced by the environment, so they don't reliably reflect the underlying genotype.
  • Convergent evolution can make unrelated species look similar, while environmental variation can make the same species look different — so appearance can actively mislead.
  • Many characteristics are polygenic and hard to classify or measure objectively.
  • Direct sequence comparison examines the genotype itself, is quantitative, and can detect differences that produce no visible effect at all.
The same four spec-named methods, arranged by how directly each one reads the genotype rather than an indirect consequence of it.
📌

Knowledge of how the underlying gene technologies themselves work is not required within this spec point — see Section 2. Here you only need to interpret and compare the sequence or characteristic data those technologies produce.

▶ 🚩 Trap — writing about DNA/RNA sequencing regardless of what the question restricts you to

If a question limits you to a specific named method — amino acid sequence comparison, or immunological comparison (e.g. comparing the amount of antibody bound to an antigen/protein in different species) — writing about DNA/RNA sequencing instead scores nothing, no matter how accurate the biology is. Always check exactly which technique the question has restricted you to before you start answering. Also watch for plausible-sounding distractors that are never credited here: courtship behaviour, mutation count, chromosome number, allele frequency, species richness and the index of diversity all appear elsewhere in the specification, but not as valid answers to "name a method of comparing genetic diversity."

Section 2 · spec content point

🚫 Why gene technologies themselves aren't tested here

This is a short but important boundary line: the specification tells you explicitly what this spec point does not ask of you, and knowing that boundary saves you from over-preparing the wrong material.

📖

What the specification says: "Knowledge of gene technologies will not be tested."

1
2
3

Imagine you're handed a printed DNA test result and asked what it tells you about two animals — you don't also need to know how the machine that produced it actually works. That's exactly this rule: you'll be given sequence data (or told how similar two sequences are) and asked to read and compare it, but you're never asked to explain how scientists actually worked the sequence out in the first place.

This spec point is entirely about interpreting the output of gene technology — sequence data, or characteristic frequencies — not about describing how that data was generated. You don't need to know how DNA sequencing machines work, how PCR amplifies DNA, or any other laboratory technique here; that content sits elsewhere in the course (gene technologies proper are covered in topic 3.8). Practically, this means exam questions on 3.4.7 will hand you sequence or characteristic data already collected, and the marks are entirely for what you do with it — comparing, ranking by relatedness, or justifying a conclusion — never for describing the technique that produced it.

This exclusion sits directly underneath the four comparison methods in Section 1, and it draws a deliberate line: the specification wants you to be able to interpret and compare sequence or characteristic data, not to describe or explain the gene technologies (such as DNA sequencing techniques) that were used to generate it in the first place. Those techniques — and the reasoning behind them — belong to a different part of the specification (gene technologies, topic 3.8), where they're examined directly.

The practical consequence for how a question is set: you'll always be given the sequence, the percentage similarity, or the characteristic frequency data ready-made, and asked to interpret it — rank organisms by relatedness, comment on sensitivity, or draw a conclusion about diversity. You are never asked, within this spec point, to explain how a DNA or amino acid sequence was actually obtained.

🧭

Revision implication: don't spend time here learning the mechanics of sequencing techniques for this spec point specifically — that revision time is better spent making sure you can fluently interpret sequence-difference data (Section 1) and quantitative variation data (Sections 3–5), since that's where every mark on 3.4.7 actually sits.

Section 3 · spec content point

🎲 Random sampling, then calculating a mean and standard deviation

A quantitative investigation of variation starts with getting the sample itself right — every mark for "describe how to sample" needs both a valid random method and the reason it matters, not one without the other.

📖

What the specification says: "Quantitative investigations of variation within a species involve: collecting data from random samples; calculating a mean value of the collected data and the standard deviation of that mean; interpreting mean values and their standard deviations." (This is the same specification content point covered in Section 4 — this half covers collecting the sample and calculating the statistics; Section 4 covers interpreting them.)

1
2
3

If you want to know something about a whole group by only checking a few members of it, you have to pick those few fairly — like pulling names out of a hat instead of just choosing your friends. That's random sampling: use a way of picking that gives every single item an equal chance of being chosen, and check enough of them that one weird result doesn't throw everything off. Once you've measured your sample, you find the average (the mean) and how spread out the results are (the standard deviation) — though you're never actually made to do that maths by hand in the exam.

Valid sampling needs two things together, each with its reason: results must be selected at random — for example by generating coordinates from a random number table or calculator and using them as a grid reference — so that the investigator's own choices can't bias which items get measured; and the sample must be large (the mark scheme accepts more than 10) so that anomalous results have less effect and a statistical test is actually valid to use afterwards. Once the data is collected, you calculate the mean (the average) and the standard deviation (a measure of how spread out the data is around that mean) — but you're never asked to actually work out a standard deviation by hand in a written exam, only to interpret one you're given.

Describing a valid sampling method

RequirementThe method & its reason
Random selectionDivide the area (or population) into a numbered grid, then generate coordinates using a random number table or calculator — never by choosing "by eye" or spreading points evenly. This gives every point an equal chance of being sampled, avoiding investigator bias.
A large sample sizeThe mark scheme accepts more than 10. A larger sample reduces the effect of chance or anomalous results on the mean, and allows a valid statistical test to be carried out afterwards.

A method described without its reason is an incomplete answer — "generate random coordinates" alone is worth less than "…so that every point has an equal chance of being sampled, avoiding bias."

🌿

Worked context worth knowing: investigating the effect of position on the growth of leaves. A plant is divided into zones — say, top, middle and bottom of the canopy — and leaves are picked at random from each zone using the same random-coordinate method, so that any measured difference between zones reflects a genuine effect of position, rather than the investigator unconsciously picking the most convenient or most striking leaves.

What to calculate once the data is in

State the mean and the standard deviation — accepted equivalents include standard error or interquartile range. Where a data set contains outliers, or the sample size is small, the median can be a more representative measure than the mean, since extreme values distort a mean much more than they distort a median.

▶ 🚩 Trap — sampling "by eye" or "spread evenly"

Choosing sample points "by eye," or deliberately spacing them evenly across an area, is not random sampling and does not avoid bias — only a genuine random number generator or table gives every point an equal chance of selection. This distinction also separates random quadrat placement (used to avoid bias, and the skill this spec point itself tests) from systematic transect sampling, where quadrats are placed at fixed, regular intervals specifically to reveal a trend across an environmental gradient rather than to avoid bias — a deliberately different, non-random technique used for a different purpose. Systematic transects and the mark–release–recapture technique share the same fieldwork sampling skill as this page, but their full methods and calculations belong to a different part of the specification (Populations in ecosystems).

Section 4 · spec content point

📊 Interpreting spread, and choosing the right statistical test

This is where "interpreting mean values and their standard deviations" is actually examined: reading what a standard deviation tells you about spread and significance, then picking — and justifying — the one statistical test that fits the data in front of you.

📖

What the specification says: "Quantitative investigations of variation within a species involve: collecting data from random samples; calculating a mean value of the collected data and the standard deviation of that mean; interpreting mean values and their standard deviations." (Same specification content point as Section 3 — this half covers interpreting the mean and SD, including choosing and justifying the statistical test that supports that interpretation.)

1
2
3

A big spread of results (a big standard deviation) means the data is scattered a long way from the average; a small spread means it's all clustered close together. If you draw the spread for two different groups as little "whiskers" around each average and they don't overlap at all, the two groups are probably genuinely different. If the whiskers do overlap, the difference might just be luck. To actually check whether a difference is real, scientists pick one of three tests depending on what kind of data they have — and they always have to say a "guess" first (the null hypothesis) about what they expect to find if there's really no effect.

A large standard deviation means the data is widely spread about the mean; a small one means the data is closely clustered. If the spread (the SD, or twice the SD) of two means doesn't overlap, the difference between them is unlikely to be down to chance; if it does overlap, chance could explain it — but a bare "it's significant" is never enough, you must name the specific variable and the direction of the difference. To formally test this, you choose one of three statistical tests depending on the type of data: a t-test for comparing two means of continuous, normally distributed data; a correlation coefficient for testing whether two variables are associated; and chi-squared for comparing observed against expected frequencies of categoric data. Every test needs a correctly worded null hypothesis — a statement that there's no significant difference (or no correlation), written using the actual variables in that specific question, not a generic memorised sentence.

Reading a standard deviation

What you seeWhat it means
Large standard deviationData widely spread about the mean — more variation within the sample
Small standard deviationData closely clustered about the mean — less variation, the mean is more representative
SDs (or 2×SD) do not overlapThe difference between the means is unlikely to be due to chance — likely significant
SDs (or 2×SD) do overlapThe difference could be due to chance — not (necessarily) significant
Left: the whiskers (mean ± SD, or ±2×SD) of A and B don't overlap — a real difference is likely. Right: C and D's whiskers overlap — the difference between them could just be due to chance. Always name the variable and the direction when writing this up.
▶ 🚩 Trap — a bare "the results are significant"

Stating only "the results are significant" (or "not significant") is rejected outright. Name the specific variable whose difference is significant, and state the direction — e.g. "the mean leaf length in the shaded zone is significantly lower than in the open zone."

Choosing and justifying the right statistical test

TestUsed for
t-testComparing two means of normally-distributed, continuous data.
Correlation coefficient (e.g. Spearman's rank)Testing for an association between two variables.
Chi-squaredComparing observed with expected frequencies of categoric data.

The mark is never for the test name alone — it needs the named test and its justification, linked in one statement: e.g. "a t-test, because I am comparing the means of two sets of normally distributed, continuous data." Naming a test without saying precisely what it compares loses the justification mark even if the test itself is correct.

Stepping through a full investigation

Stating a correctly-worded null hypothesis

A null hypothesis states there is no significant difference (for a t-test or chi-squared comparison) or no significant correlation (for a correlation test) between the actual named variables in that question — never a generic memorised sentence.

▶ 🚩 Trap — writing an alternative hypothesis by mistake

Null hypothesis answers are very often actually alternative hypotheses — stating there is a difference or correlation, the opposite of what's required. Take particular care not to use "difference" wording for what is actually a correlation/association test, or vice versa, and always name the exact variables from the question rather than writing a generic template sentence.

Reliability, precision and accuracy — not interchangeable

TermComes from
ReliabilityRepeating the measurement or investigation
Precision / resolutionUsing a finer-scale measuring instrument
AccuracyCorrect calibration against a known standard

These three are not interchangeable — do not substitute one for another; each needs its own distinct justification, and mark schemes reject "more accurate" where repeats are actually the reason given.

Evaluating a full diversity investigation

📋

Standard evaluation checklist: randomness of sampling · sample size · repeats and a calculated mean · controlled variables · whether a statistical test was used and correctly justified · the limitation of drawing conclusions from a single species, site or year. Across this whole section, "no statistical test was used" and "only one species/site/study" are the two most frequently credited criticisms.

Section 5 · spec content point

🧮 Standard deviations: interpret them, never calculate them

A short but exam-shaping rule: however important calculating a standard deviation is as a genuine investigative skill, it is explicitly ruled out of written papers.

📖

What the specification says: "Students will not be required to calculate standard deviations in written papers."

1
2
3

Working out a standard deviation by hand takes a calculator and quite a few steps — the exam will never make you do that maths. Instead, it always gives you the answer already worked out (or a picture showing the spread) and just asks you to say what it means. So the exam skill isn't "can you do the sum," it's "can you read the answer."

Section 3 lists "calculating…the standard deviation" as part of a quantitative investigation — that calculation is a genuine skill, done in practical work using a calculator or software, not something ruled out of biology altogether. What this specific rule rules out is being asked to perform that calculation by hand under exam conditions. In a written paper, standard deviation values (or error bars on a graph) will always be given to you already calculated, and every mark is for interpreting them — reading spread, comparing overlap, and drawing a conclusion — exactly as covered in Section 4.

It's worth being precise about what this rule does and doesn't exclude. Section 3's spec wording lists "calculating a mean value of the collected data and the standard deviation of that mean" as part of carrying out a quantitative investigation — that calculation is a real, examinable investigative skill, ordinarily carried out with a calculator or spreadsheet during practical work. This rule specifically exempts written exam papers: you will never be asked to work through the standard deviation formula by hand under exam conditions.

In practice, this means every SD-related question on a written paper hands you the value (or a graph with error bars already drawn) and asks you to interpret it — using exactly the spread-and-overlap logic from Section 4. No marks on a written paper are ever awarded for correctly computing an SD from raw data; they're awarded for correctly reading what a given SD says about spread, and about whether an apparent difference between two means is likely to be genuine.

🔀

Related idea worth knowing: a mean can be misleading where a data set contains outliers/extreme values, or where the sample size is small — in those cases, the median is a more representative measure of central tendency, since it isn't dragged around by a small number of extreme values the way a mean is.

🧭

Revision implication: there is no value in memorising the standard deviation formula for this spec point. Spend that time instead on fluently producing the interpretation language from Section 4 — "widely/closely clustered," "the SDs do/don't overlap, so the difference is/isn't likely to be due to chance" — since that is where every mark connected to standard deviation on a written paper actually sits.

Section 6 · exam technique

▶ Where the real difficulty sits

3.4.7 doesn't appear on most "high overall difficulty" topic lists, and its content is genuinely short. What makes it tricky is that almost every demand is marked on exact wording — which named method, which named test, "difference" versus "correlation" in a null hypothesis — so it rewards precise, deliberate phrasing far more than it rewards raw content knowledge. The statistical-methods toolkit (choosing and justifying a test, stating a null hypothesis) is examined with essentially identical demands whether the question sits at AS or at full A-level — it isn't deepened further at A2, so treat the wording precision here as non-negotiable from the start, not something to tidy up later.

Section 7 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

This spec point makes an excellent "how do we actually know?" paragraph inside a longer essay about variation or diversity — every earlier claim that a population or species is "more variable" or "more diverse" is only defensible because of the sampling and statistical-testing methodology named here.

▶ Example paragraph — the methodology turn in an essay on variation and diversity

Genetic diversity can be compared using the frequency of observable characteristics or, more reliably, by directly comparing DNA base sequences, mRNA base sequences or the amino acid sequences of the proteins they encode — with DNA comparison the most sensitive of these, since a degenerate genetic code can hide differences at the protein level. Quantifying variation within a species requires data collected from random, sufficiently large samples, from which a mean and standard deviation are calculated; comparing the overlap of those standard deviations, alongside a correctly chosen and justified statistical test — a t-test for comparing means, a correlation coefficient for testing association, or chi-squared for comparing observed and expected frequencies — is what allows a claimed difference to be judged as genuinely significant rather than a product of chance. Without this methodology, any claim elsewhere in an essay that one population or species is "more diverse" than another could not actually be substantiated.

Essay technique: place a paragraph like this as the "how do we know" turn in an essay about variation, diversity, evolution or classification — every earlier paragraph asserting variation or difference becomes far stronger once it's backed by exactly this sampling-and-testing logic. Close by linking forward to the population-level equivalents of this same idea — fieldwork sampling techniques (quadrats, transects, mark–release–recapture) and Hardy–Weinberg allele-frequency testing — as the same underlying logic applied at a different scale.

Section 8

🧷 Where this fits in the course, memory hook & checklist

Where 3.4.7 reappears across the rest of the specification

  • 3.4.5 Species and taxonomy — DNA/amino-acid sequence comparison as the primary modern evidence for phylogenetic classification and evolutionary relatedness.
  • 3.4.6 Biodiversity within a community — the index of diversity, d = N(N−1) ÷ Σn(n−1), is a companion quantitative diversity statistic examined separately: it measures species diversity in a community (richness and abundance), whereas this page's mean/SD toolkit measures variation of a single characteristic within or between species. Examiners frequently pair the two in the same question sequence.
  • 3.7.2 Populations — Hardy–Weinberg as a parallel statistical technique, testing observed against predicted allele/genotype frequencies.
  • 3.7.4 Populations in ecosystems — the fieldwork half of the sampling toolkit: quadrats, transects and mark–release–recapture (including the Lincoln index, N = (n₁ × n₂) ÷ n₃), sharing this spec point's random-sampling skill and statistical-test-choice logic, but examined there in full.
  • 3.8.3 Using genome projects and 3.8.4.3 Genetic fingerprinting — direct DNA-sequence/VNTR comparison as the applied, technological extension of the comparison methods named in Section 1.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Comparing genetic diversity

  • Four credited methods only: observable characteristics, DNA base sequence, mRNA base sequence, amino acid sequence. Answer with exactly the one a question restricts you to.
  • Fewer sequence differences from a reference organism = more closely related = more recent common ancestor. State the degree/timing, not just "related."
  • DNA comparison is more sensitive than protein comparison — the degenerate code means some base changes don't change the amino acid.
  • Sequencing has replaced observable characteristics because phenotype is affected by environment, convergent evolution, and polygenic traits.

2 · Gene technologies aren't tested here

  • You interpret sequence/characteristic data — you never need to describe how it was produced.
  • Gene technology mechanics (sequencing, PCR) belong to topic 3.8, not 3.4.7.

3 · Random sampling, mean & SD

  • Random (numbered grid + random number table/calculator) → avoids bias. Large (accepted as >10) → allows a valid statistical test.
  • Method without its reason = incomplete answer.
  • Calculate mean and standard deviation (accept standard error / IQR).

4 · Interpreting spread & choosing a test

  • Large SD = widely spread; small SD = closely clustered. SDs don't overlap → unlikely due to chance; SDs overlap → could be chance.
  • Never a bare "significant" — name the variable and direction.
  • t-test = two means, normal continuous data. Correlation coefficient = association between two variables. Chi-squared = observed vs expected frequencies.
  • Test name + justification together, always. Null hypothesis: no difference/no correlation, worded for the actual variables — watch for accidentally writing the alternative hypothesis.
  • Reliability = repeats. Precision = finer instrument. Accuracy = calibration. Not interchangeable.

5 · Interpret SD, never calculate it

  • Written papers never require calculating an SD by hand — only interpreting one given to you.
  • Use the median instead of the mean where there are outliers or a small sample size.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Comparing genetic diversity

❓ State two ways, other than comparing observable characteristics, that genetic diversity between two species could be compared.
Comparing the base sequence of DNA; comparing the base sequence of mRNA; or comparing the amino acid sequence of the proteins encoded by DNA and mRNA (any two). Credit requires the idea of comparison, not just naming a molecule.
❓ Percentage sequence similarity data show that species P shares 98% DNA sequence identity with a reference species, and species Q shares 82%. Interpret this data in terms of relatedness.
Species P has fewer base sequence differences from the reference species than species Q, so P is more closely related to the reference species than Q is, and shares a more recent common ancestor with it; Q, having more differences, diverged from the reference species longer ago.
❓ Explain why comparing DNA base sequences can reveal genetic differences that comparing amino acid sequences does not.
The genetic code is degenerate, so more than one codon can code for the same amino acid; some base sequence changes are therefore synonymous mutations that do not change the amino acid sequence produced. DNA comparison can detect these differences directly, whereas amino acid sequence comparison cannot, making DNA comparison the more sensitive technique.

Gene technologies aren't tested here

❓ A student writes a full paragraph describing how DNA sequencing machines work, in answer to a question asking them to compare the genetic diversity of two species using given sequence data. Explain why this would not be credited.
Knowledge of gene technologies is not tested within this spec point; marks are awarded only for interpreting and comparing the sequence data provided (e.g. counting differences, stating relatedness), not for describing the technique used to generate that data.
❓ Where in the specification would knowledge of the actual sequencing technique used to obtain DNA data be examined?
Under the gene technologies content of the specification (e.g. genome projects, genetic fingerprinting) — not under investigating diversity, which only requires interpretation of the resulting data.

Random sampling, mean & SD

❓ Describe how a student could select a valid random sample of leaves from a tree canopy to investigate the effect of position on leaf length.
Divide the canopy into a numbered grid (or into zones, each numbered); generate coordinates/numbers using a random number table or calculator; select the leaf at each generated coordinate. This avoids bias, since every leaf has an equal chance of being selected, rather than the investigator unconsciously choosing more convenient or more striking leaves.
❓ Explain why a sample size of more than 10 is recommended for this type of investigation.
A larger sample size reduces the effect of chance or anomalous results on the calculated mean, making the mean more representative of the population, and allows a valid statistical test to be carried out on the data.
❓ State the two statistical values that should be calculated once random sample data has been collected.
The mean and the standard deviation (accept standard error or interquartile range as equivalents).

Interpreting spread & choosing a test

❓ Two plant populations have means and standard deviations that, when plotted as error bars of mean ± 2×SD, do not overlap. Interpret this finding.
Because the standard deviations do not overlap, the difference between the two means is unlikely to be due to chance — the difference in [the named variable] between the two populations is likely to be significant.
❓ A student wants to compare the mean stem height of two normally distributed, continuous data sets from two plant populations. Name and justify an appropriate statistical test.
A t-test, because it compares the means of two sets of normally distributed, continuous data.
❓ Write a null hypothesis for an investigation testing whether there is a correlation between soil pH and plant species richness.
There is no significant correlation (association) between soil pH and plant species richness.

Interpret SD, never calculate it

❓ A written exam question gives a table of raw, unprocessed measurements and asks a student to calculate the standard deviation by hand before interpreting it. Explain whether this is a valid exam demand.
No — students are not required to calculate standard deviations in written papers; a written exam will always provide the standard deviation (or an equivalent measure of spread) ready-calculated, and the marks are for interpreting it, not computing it.
❓ A data set of leaf lengths contains one unusually large outlier and has a small sample size. Suggest a more appropriate measure of central tendency than the mean, and explain why.
The median, because it is not distorted by extreme values or outliers in the way that a mean is, making it more representative of a small or outlier-affected data set.
