Methods of Studying Cells

⚠️

The single habit that protects the most marks on this spec point: whenever a structure can't be seen with a light microscope, the reason is always that resolution is too low, because the wavelength of light is too long — never "magnification." Examiners are told to ignore any mention of magnification here, and to reject "a clearer image" as a substitute for "more detail." Keep that one distinction word-perfect and a large share of this spec point's marks look after themselves.

Section 1 · spec content point

🔬 Microscopes — principles and limitations

Optical, TEM and SEM each show you something different, for a very specific physical reason. Learn what each one can and can't reveal, and — more importantly — why.

📖

What the specification says: "The principles and limitations of optical microscopes, transmission electron microscopes and scanning electron microscopes."

1
2
3

Imagine trying to see two grains of sugar sitting right next to each other using only a torch. However hard you squint, or however much you blow the photo up on a screen afterwards, they still look like one blurry blob — light itself just isn't "fine" enough to tell them apart at that distance. That's what resolution really means: how much detail you can actually pick out, not how big the picture looks. An optical microscope uses light, so there's a hard limit on the smallest detail it can ever show, no matter how much you zoom in. Two special microscopes get around this by using something other than light: a transmission electron microscope (TEM) fires a beam of electrons through a wafer-thin slice of a dead sample to reveal fine detail hidden inside it, and a scanning electron microscope (SEM) bounces electrons off the surface of a dead sample to build up a 3-D picture of what it looks like from the outside.

  • Resolution — how much fine detail you can actually see
  • Magnification — how much bigger the picture looks
  • Wavelength — roughly, the "size" of the light or electron wave being used to form the image
  • Artefact — something that shows up in the picture but was actually created while preparing the sample, and isn't really there in the living cell

Optical microscopes use visible light, and light has a fairly long wavelength, so it simply cannot separate out two points that sit closer together than roughly 200 nm — they blur into a single spot, no matter how much the image is enlarged afterwards. Electron microscopes get around this by using electrons instead of light, which behave like a much shorter wavelength, so they can resolve far finer detail. A transmission electron microscope (TEM) fires electrons through an extremely thin, stained slice of dead tissue mounted in a vacuum, producing a highly detailed 2-D image of internal structure — but the elaborate preparation can create artefacts, structures that look real but aren't actually present in the living cell. A scanning electron microscope (SEM) instead bounces electrons off the surface of a dead, vacuum-mounted specimen, producing a 3-D image of its surface — lower resolution than a TEM, and no internal detail at all, but no thin sectioning is needed.

Whenever a question asks why a structure can't be seen with an optical microscope, the answer is always about resolution, never magnification: resolution is too low, because the wavelength of light is too long to resolve two points that close together as separate. Magnifying past the resolution limit just makes the same blur bigger — it doesn't add any detail that wasn't there.

MicroscopeResolution / magnificationSpecimen requirementsKey limitation
Optical (light)≈200 nm resolution; up to about ×1500 magnificationCan be living or dead, thin, and can be stainedLong wavelength of light limits resolution — can't resolve organelles closer than ≈200 nm apart
Transmission EM (TEM)≈0.1–1 nm resolution; very high magnificationMust be dead, cut into extremely thin sections, stained, mounted in a vacuumComplex preparation risks artefacts; not living material; only a single 2-D plane is seen, so 3-D shape is hard to interpret
Scanning EM (SEM)Lower resolution than TEM, but still far above opticalMust be dead, surface-coated, mounted in a vacuumShows 3-D surface detail without thin-sectioning, but not living material and no internal detail at all
Same real distance, three microscopes. Optical light cannot resolve the two points (its wavelength is too long) so they blur into one spot; TEM's much shorter wavelength resolves them as two separate points; SEM never shows this kind of internal detail — it only images surface shape.

Comparing TEM and SEM

TEM: very high resolution, reveals fine internal (ultrastructural) detail — but the specimen must be dead, cut into extremely thin sections, stained with heavy metals, and mounted in a vacuum. This elaborate preparation can introduce artefacts, and because only a single thin plane is seen, 3-D structure is hard to interpret.

SEM: shows 3-D surface detail without needing thin sectioning — but resolution is lower than TEM, the specimen must still be dead and vacuum-mounted, and no internal detail is shown at all.

📌

Command-word habit: "compare" wants matched pairs of points — an advantage and a limitation of each microscope, side by side — not two separate, unlinked lists.

What is an artefact, and why does this matter historically?

An artefact is a structure produced by the preparation process (fixing, dehydrating, staining, sectioning) that is not actually present in the living cell. There was a considerable period of time during which the scientific community had to work out, often slowly and by repeated cross-checking across different techniques and different scientists, which structures seen under the electron microscope were genuine organelles and which were simply artefacts of preparation — as much a "how science works" point as a content point.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "black and white, so it must be an electron micrograph"

"The photograph is black and white, so it must be from an electron microscope" is not accepted reasoning. Refer instead to the 3-D nature of the image (a strong clue for SEM) or the level of internal detail visible (a strong clue for TEM) — a coloured, lower-detail image, possibly of living material, points to an optical microscope instead.

▶ 🚩 Trap — naming magnification instead of resolution

Mark schemes explicitly instruct examiners to ignore any reference to magnification when a question asks why a structure isn't visible with a light microscope, and to reject "the image would be clearer" or "you could zoom in more" as substitutes for stating that resolution — not magnification — is the limiting factor.

Section 2 · spec content point

📏 Measuring size, and the difference between magnification and resolution

Before any calculation, you need two ideas kept firmly apart, and a reliable way to actually measure something down a microscope in the first place.

📖

What the specification says: "Measuring the size of an object viewed with an optical microscope. The difference between magnification and resolution."

1
2
3

Picture looking through the microscope eyepiece and seeing a tiny ruler printed onto the lens — except its marks don't mean anything on their own. How much each mark is really worth changes every time you switch to a different zoom lens, a bit like a stretchy ruler that gets squashed or stretched whenever you touch the zoom. So before you can trust it, you have to check it against a proper, fixed-length ruler (called a stage micrometer) at that exact zoom — and check it again every single time you change zoom.

  • Eyepiece graticule — the built-in "ruler" you look through, with marks of unknown real size
  • Stage micrometer — a tiny slide with a known, fixed real length, used to check the graticule
  • Calibrate — work out what each graticule mark is really worth, at one particular zoom
  • Uncertainty — how far off your measurement could realistically be

Magnification tells you how many times bigger the image is than the real object; resolution tells you the smallest gap you can still tell apart as two separate things, rather than one blur. They are not the same idea — enlarging an image past the resolution limit just makes the same blur bigger, not more detailed. To actually measure something down a microscope, you look through an eyepiece fitted with a graticule — a scale with arbitrary divisions — but because those divisions are only meaningful at one particular objective lens, you first have to calibrate the graticule against a stage micrometer (a slide with a scale of known, real length) every single time you change magnification. Once calibrated, you take several readings and use the mean, and you should quote your final answer as a range together with an uncertainty — the uncertainty being half of the smallest division on the scale you used.

TermDefinition — learn this exactly
MagnificationHow many times larger an image is than the real object.
ResolutionThe minimum distance apart two objects can be and still be distinguished as separate.
🚩

Never treat magnification and resolution as the same thing, and never define resolution as "how clear the image is" — it is specifically the minimum separable distance. Increasing magnification past the resolution limit reveals no further detail; it simply makes the same blur bigger.

Measuring with an eyepiece graticule and a stage micrometer

  1. Measure the object using the eyepiece graticule.
  2. Calibrate the graticule against a stage micrometer (an accept­ed alternative name is "micrometer slide") at that exact magnification — a calibration done once is not valid if the magnification is changed, because the graticule's divisions are arbitrary and their real value depends on the objective lens in use.
  3. Take a number of measurements and calculate a mean — enough readings for a reliable mean (roughly five or more).

Quoting a measurement with its uncertainty

State a range plus an uncertainty value equal to half the smallest scale division, for example: "8–10 µm; ±2 µm".

▶ 🚩 Trap — a bare measurement with no uncertainty

A bare range with no stated uncertainty, or a range with no plus/minus value attached, does not score full marks. The uncertainty always has to be given explicitly, and it should equal half of the smallest division on whichever scale was actually used.

Section 3 · spec content point

🧮 The magnification formula — the calculation set piece

Simple arithmetic once it's set up correctly — the marks are actually won or lost in the unit conversion, not the division.

📖

What the specification says: "Use of the formula: magnification = size of image ÷ size of real object."

1
2
3

Imagine a photo of an ant that measures 8 cm long on your screen, while the real ant is only 8 mm long — the photo is 10 times bigger than real life, and that "10 times" is the magnification. If you already know how much bigger a picture is, and you can measure the picture itself, you can work backwards to find out how big the real thing actually is — just by dividing the measured picture by that magnification number.

  • Magnification — how many times bigger the picture is than the real thing
  • Actual (real) size — the true size of the object, before it was magnified
  • Unit conversion — changing mm into µm, or µm into nm, so everything matches up
  • Scale bar — a short printed line on an image that tells you what real length it represents

Rearranged, the formula becomes actual size = size of image ÷ magnification. In practice: measure the structure in the image with a ruler in millimetres, convert that measurement into whatever unit the answer needs (×1000 to go from mm to µm, and ×1000 again from µm to nm), then divide by the magnification stated on the image. Marks are given step by step, so writing the conversion out as its own line — even if you then make an error dividing — usually scores more than skipping straight to a final answer. A quicker alternative, when a scale bar is printed on the image, is to measure the bar itself, divide its printed length by the real length it's stated to represent, to get the magnification — then apply that same magnification to any other structure on the same image.

  1. Measure the image in mm using a ruler.
  2. Convert units carefully — mm → µm is ×1000; µm → nm is ×1000.
  3. Apply the formula: actual size = size of image ÷ magnification.
  4. Give the answer to an appropriate number of significant figures, with the correct units.

Partial credit is staged — a mark is given for each correct step even if the final answer is wrong, so always show the working, especially the conversion step, not just a final number.

Writing the mm→µm conversion as its own explicit line is the single most commonly mis-stepped part of this calculation nationally — never skip straight to the final division.

Using a scale bar to derive magnification

Measure the scale bar's printed length, convert it to the same units as its stated real-world length, then divide: magnification = length of scale bar (image) ÷ real length the bar represents. That magnification can then be applied to measure any other structure on the same image.

📌

Underused shortcut: the scale-bar method is often quicker and more reliable than the formula method, using a value already printed on the image — but it's rarely used by students even when it would save time.

▶ 🚩 Trap — skipping the unit-conversion line

Failing to convert cleanly between mm, µm and nm (×1000 at each step) before dividing is the single most common cause of a wrong final answer, even on an otherwise well-understood calculation. Because marks are staged, a converted-but-then-mis-divided answer often still scores more than a correctly divided but unconverted one — so always write the conversion as its own explicit line, not folded silently into a mental calculation.

Section 4 · spec content point

🧫 Cell fractionation and ultracentrifugation

A practical method for pulling a cell apart into its separate organelles — worth learning as a procedure you could actually carry out, not just a list to recall.

📖

What the specification says: "Principles of cell fractionation and ultracentrifugation as used to separate cell components."

1
2
3

Imagine wanting to look at just one ingredient from a smoothie, separately from all the rest. First you'd blend everything up (that's homogenising) and pour it through a sieve to get rid of any lumps (that's filtering). Then you'd spin it in something like a salad spinner — the heaviest bits fly outward and settle first, and if you keep spinning faster and faster, lighter and lighter bits settle out in turn. Cell fractionation works the same way: cells are blended and filtered in a special cold liquid, then spun in a centrifuge at increasing speed, so each type of organelle settles out on its own, heaviest first.

  • Homogenise — blend the tissue open to break the cells apart
  • Filter — pour through a sieve to remove debris and unbroken cells
  • Pellet — the solid layer that settles at the bottom of the tube
  • Supernatant — the liquid left sitting above the pellet

Before spinning, the solution the tissue is broken up in has to be cold (to slow down enzymes that would otherwise damage the organelles), isotonic — the same water potential as the cells — (so organelles don't gain or lose water by osmosis and swell or shrink), and buffered (to keep pH steady so enzymes and proteins don't denature). The tissue is homogenised (blended open) and then filtered to remove debris and whole, unbroken cells. The resulting mixture is spun in a centrifuge, starting at a lower speed: the densest, heaviest organelles form a solid pellet at the bottom first, while everything else remains as a liquid supernatant above it. That supernatant is poured off and re-spun at a higher speed, pelleting the next-heaviest organelles, and so on — nuclei first, then mitochondria (or chloroplasts in plant tissue), then lysosomes, then ribosomes and endoplasmic reticulum last.

Preparing the tissue, and why each condition matters

  • Cold → reduces enzyme activity that would otherwise damage or digest the organelles.
  • Isotonic (same water potential as the cells) → prevents organelles taking in or losing water by osmosis and bursting or shrivelling.
  • Buffered → keeps pH constant, preventing enzymes and proteins from denaturing.
🚩

Never write "the cell bursts/shrivels" when the item under discussion is an organelle in suspension, not a whole cell — mark schemes require organelle-specific language: "the organelle bursts/shrivels", or "damages the organelle".

Ultracentrifugation and the pellet order

The homogenate is spun at increasing speed; at each speed, the densest remaining component pellets out, and the supernatant (the liquid above the pellet) is drawn off and re-spun faster. In order of decreasing mass/density:

OrderFraction pelleted
1st pelletNuclei (heaviest — pellets first)
2nd pelletMitochondria / chloroplasts
3rd pelletLysosomes
4th pelletRibosomes / endoplasmic reticulum (lightest — pellets last)
Tissue is homogenised then filtered in a cold, buffered, isotonic solution. Each spin pellets the next-densest component and the supernatant is poured off and re-spun faster. Pellet order (heaviest first): nucleus → mitochondria/chloroplasts → lysosomes → ribosomes/ER.
▶ 🚩 Trap — generic vocabulary instead of "pellet" / "supernatant"

Vocabulary that is specifically credited is "pellet" (the solid material that settles at the bottom of the tube) and "supernatant" (the liquid remaining above it) — generic terms like "the solid bit" or "the liquid bit" are not accepted in place of these.

⚠️

Why full marks on this method are rare: a full-mark answer has several separately credited parts, not just the three conditions — (1) the solution is cold, buffered, and isotonic; (2) the tissue is homogenised then filtered; (3) the filtrate is centrifuged at increasing speed, and at each stage the supernatant is poured off and re-spun faster, with the pellet resuspended between spins in fresh cold buffer to wash out contaminating material before it's kept. That resuspension/wash step is the part most often left out even by otherwise strong answers — don't stop at "centrifuge at increasing speed", say what happens to the pellet in between.

Section 5 · exam technique

The biology on this spec point is simple — the reason it's still ranked medium difficulty is that nearly every mark hinges on one precise word.

▶ Where this point sits in difficulty terms

This spec point is medium difficulty overall, at both AS and A2 — the underlying biology is straightforward, but terminology-precision risk is high. At AS, the resolution-versus-magnification distinction is genuinely counter-intuitive on first meeting: it's natural to assume a bigger image always means more detail, and unpicking that assumption is a recurring stumbling block. At A2, no new microscopy facts are introduced, but the same resolution reasoning is reused in unfamiliar contexts (such as an emulsion droplet that can't be resolved), and two genuinely new skills are added — calibrating a graticule against a stage micrometer, and quoting a measurement's uncertainty — that AS-level calculation questions never require. The fix is the same for both: drill the two definitions to word-perfect recall, then specifically rehearse the resolution-not-magnification trap and the pellet-order sequence until you can explain why, not just recite them.

Section 6 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

This spec point rarely anchors a whole essay title on its own, but it supplies the opening methodology paragraph for essays touching cell structure, ultrastructure, or "how we know what we know" about organelles — before naming the organelles themselves.

▶ Example paragraph — opening a "structure and function of organelles" essay

Our understanding of cell ultrastructure depends entirely on the microscope used to study it. Optical microscopes cannot resolve structures below about 200 nm apart, because the wavelength of light is too long — not because of insufficient magnification. Electron microscopes, using a much shorter wavelength, achieve far higher resolution: transmission electron microscopes reveal fine internal detail from thin, vacuum-mounted sections, while scanning electron microscopes reveal three-dimensional surface detail without sectioning. Because specimen preparation for electron microscopy is complex, some structures first observed were later shown to be artefacts of preparation rather than genuine organelles, and it took a considerable period of time for the scientific community to distinguish reliably between the two.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around isolating an organelle to study it

Before an organelle's function can be investigated directly, it first has to be isolated intact from the rest of the cell. Cell fractionation achieves this: tissue is homogenised and filtered in a solution kept cold, buffered and isotonic, to protect the organelles from enzyme damage, pH change and osmotic damage respectively. Ultracentrifugation then separates the organelles by density, spinning the homogenate at successively higher speeds so the heaviest components — nuclei, then mitochondria or chloroplasts, then lysosomes, then ribosomes and endoplasmic reticulum — pellet out in turn. This is what allows a specific organelle's structure or biochemistry to be studied in isolation, rather than inferred indirectly from a whole cell.

Whichever essay title this content supports, treat it as the "methodology" paragraph: name the microscope or technique, state its key limitation precisely, then add one sentence linking it back to how the rest of the essay's evidence about cell structure was actually obtained.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Microscopes — principles and limitations

  • Can't see it with a light microscope? Always: resolution too low, wavelength of light too long — never "magnification."
  • TEM: highest resolution, internal detail, but dead/thin-sectioned/stained/vacuum — risk of artefacts.
  • SEM: 3-D surface detail, no sectioning needed, but lower resolution than TEM and no internal detail.
  • "Black and white" is not evidence of an EM image — use 3-D nature (SEM) or internal detail (TEM) instead.

2 · Measuring size & resolution

  • Magnification = how many times bigger; resolution = minimum distance apart still seen as separate. Not the same thing.
  • Eyepiece graticule divisions are arbitrary — must recalibrate against a stage micrometer every time magnification changes.
  • Quote a measurement as a range + uncertainty = half the smallest scale division, e.g. "8–10 µm; ±2 µm."

3 · The magnification formula

  • Actual size = size of image ÷ magnification. Convert units (×1000 per step) before dividing, as its own explicit line.
  • Staged marks — show every step, even if the final answer is wrong.
  • Scale bar shortcut: magnification = printed bar length ÷ real length it represents.

4 · Cell fractionation & ultracentrifugation

  • Solution must be cold, isotonic, buffered — reduces enzyme activity, prevents osmotic damage, prevents denaturing.
  • Homogenise → filter → centrifuge at increasing speed. Say "pellet" and "supernatant", not generic terms.
  • Order: nucleus → mitochondria/chloroplasts → lysosomes → ribosomes/ER (heaviest first).
  • Full marks need the resuspension/wash step between spins — don't stop at "increasing speed."
  • Say "the organelle bursts/shrivels", never "the cell bursts/shrivels."
🧠

Wavelength, not width. Can't see it → resolution/wavelength, never magnification. Separating components → cold, buffered, isotonic; heaviest pellets first.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Microscopes — principles and limitations

❓ A student observes a mitochondrion using a light microscope but cannot make out its internal cristae. Explain why.
The resolution of a light microscope is too low, because the wavelength of light is too long to resolve structures that close together as separate; the cristae are closer together than the light microscope's resolution limit. (Ignore any reference to magnification — increasing it would not reveal more detail.)
❓ Give one advantage and one limitation of using a TEM rather than a SEM to study an organelle's internal membranes.
Advantage: a TEM has much higher resolution, revealing fine internal (ultrastructural) detail that a SEM cannot show. Limitation: only a single thin 2-D plane is seen with a TEM, so the organelle's overall 3-D shape is hard to interpret, and the elaborate preparation risks introducing artefacts.
❓ A micrograph shows a black-and-white, three-dimensional image of a cell surface with no internal detail visible. Identify the type of microscope used and justify your answer.
Scanning electron microscope (SEM). Justification: the image shows 3-D surface detail with no internal structure visible — the fact that it is black and white is not, on its own, valid justification, since both electron microscope types typically produce black-and-white images.

Measuring size & resolution

❓ Define magnification and resolution, and explain what happens if magnification is increased beyond a microscope's limit of resolution.
Magnification is how many times larger an image is than the real object; resolution is the minimum distance apart two objects can be and still be distinguished as separate. Increasing magnification beyond the limit of resolution produces a larger image but reveals no further detail — it simply enlarges the same blur.
❓ A student measures a cell using an eyepiece graticule at ×400, then increases the objective lens to ×1000 without recalibrating. Explain why this second measurement would be unreliable.
The divisions on an eyepiece graticule are arbitrary, and the real value of each division depends on the objective lens in use; because the graticule was not recalibrated against a stage micrometer at the new magnification, the value used for each division at ×1000 is incorrect, so the resulting measurement is unreliable.
❓ A student records a structure's length simply as "9 µm." Explain why this would not be awarded full marks, and give an improved version.
A bare measurement with no stated uncertainty does not score full marks; a full-credit answer states a range together with an explicit uncertainty equal to half the smallest scale division used, for example "8–10 µm; ±2 µm."

The magnification formula

❓ An image of an organelle measures 25 mm across. It was taken at a magnification of ×5000. Calculate the actual size of the organelle in µm, showing your working.
Convert: 25 mm × 1000 = 25,000 µm. Apply the formula: actual size = size of image ÷ magnification = 25,000 ÷ 5000 = 5 µm.
❓ A micrograph includes a printed scale bar 20 mm long, stated to represent 2 µm. Describe how to use this to find the magnification, then use it to measure a second structure on the same image.
Convert the scale bar to matching units (20 mm = 20,000 µm), then magnification = length of scale bar (image) ÷ real length it represents = 20,000 ÷ 2 = ×10,000. Measure the second structure on the image in the same units, then divide by this magnification (×10,000) to find its actual size.
❓ State two things a full-mark answer to a magnification calculation should always include, beyond stating the final numerical answer.
The unit conversion (e.g. mm to µm) shown as an explicit separate step, and the final answer given to an appropriate number of significant figures with the correct unit stated.

Cell fractionation & ultracentrifugation

❓ Describe how a tissue sample should be prepared before it is centrifuged, and explain why each condition matters.
The tissue is homogenised then filtered in a solution that is cold — to reduce enzyme activity so organelles are not digested or damaged; isotonic — the same water potential as the cells, so organelles do not gain or lose water by osmosis and burst or shrivel; and buffered — to keep pH constant, preventing enzymes and proteins from denaturing.
❓ During ultracentrifugation, in what order do nuclei, mitochondria, lysosomes and ribosomes form a pellet, and why?
Nuclei pellet first (heaviest/most dense), followed by mitochondria (or chloroplasts in plant tissue), then lysosomes, then ribosomes and endoplasmic reticulum last (lightest). This is because centrifuging at successively increasing speeds pellets out the next-most-dense component remaining in the supernatant at each stage.
❓ A student's answer states only "the mixture is centrifuged at increasing speed." Explain what else a full-mark answer needs to include.
The answer should also state that after each spin the supernatant is poured off and re-spun at a higher speed, and that the pellet is resuspended in fresh cold buffer solution between spins, to wash out contaminating material before it is kept as the isolated fraction.
