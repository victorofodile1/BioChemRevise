DNA and Protein Synthesis

How the base sequence in DNA becomes a working protein: transcription in the nucleus builds mRNA from a DNA template, then translation at the ribosome reads that mRNA to build a polypeptide. AQA treats this as one of the hardest AS-level spec points — not because the ideas are conceptually difficult, but because it demands strict, molecule-specific terminology every single time.

⚠️

The single rule that costs the most marks on this spec point: two enzymes, two bonds — never mix them. RNA polymerase joins RNA nucleotides by forming phosphodiester bonds; it never forms hydrogen bonds — that's helicase's job, breaking the DNA double helix open earlier in the process. Just as costly: keep triplet (DNA), codon (mRNA) and anticodon (tRNA) strictly attached to the correct molecule, every time — examiners repeatedly flag swapping these as a national issue.

Section 1 · spec content point

🧬 Genome and proteome

Two short definitions, but both are marked strictly on the level at which they're pitched — always a single cell or organism, never a species or a population.

📖

What the specification says: "The concept of the genome as the complete set of genes in a cell and of the proteome as the full range of proteins that a cell is able to produce."

1
2
3

Imagine your body is a giant restaurant with a huge cookbook sitting in every kitchen. That cookbook, containing every single recipe your body could ever make, is the genome. But no kitchen cooks every recipe in the book at once — the dishes actually being cooked right now, in one particular kitchen, are the proteome.

  • Gene = one recipe (the instructions for making one protein)
  • Cell = one kitchen
  • Organism = the whole restaurant, every kitchen together
  • Protein = a dish that's actually been cooked

A genome is essentially the complete genetic instruction set belonging to one cell or one organism — every gene it carries, full stop. A proteome is a different, usually much smaller, thing: it's the actual collection of proteins that one particular cell is producing, which can vary depending on what that cell's job is and even what time it is, because not every gene gets "switched on" and used in every cell. Both terms only ever apply to a single cell or a single organism — never to a whole species or population, because different individuals and different species don't share one genome or one proteome.

TermDefinition to give, word for word
GenomeThe complete set of genes in a cell or organism (equally credited: all the DNA in a cell/organism; all the genes/alleles/genetic material/genetic code in a cell/organism/person; the total number of DNA bases in a cell/organism).
ProteomeThe full range of proteins that a cell is able to produce, at a given time — or, equivalently, the range of proteins the genome/DNA is able to code for.
📌

Worked answer, for practice: "A genome is the complete set of genes in a cell or organism. A proteome is the range of proteins a cell is able to produce at a given time, or the range of proteins the genome is able to code for." Notice that "proteome" is never left as a bare "number of proteins" — it always names what range or type of protein is meant.

▶ 🚩 Trap — the wrong level of organisation

Never define genome as "all the DNA/genes within a species or population" — it must sit at the level of a single cell or organism. Likewise, don't leave proteome as an unqualified "number of proteins"; it needs the qualifying detail of what range or type of protein is meant. A statement like "the range of proteins that a species/population can produce" isn't marked wrong exactly — it's simply outside what the question is asking for, so it earns nothing either way.

Section 2 · spec content point

🧵 mRNA and tRNA structure

Both molecules are single-stranded polynucleotides built from ribose and uracil, not deoxyribose and thymine — but their shape and job are completely different. Learn the differences as genuinely comparative pairs, since that's the only way "contrast" questions score.

📖

What the specification says: "The structure of molecules of messenger RNA (mRNA) and of transfer RNA (tRNA)."

1
2
3

mRNA is like a photocopied shopping list, sent out of the office (the nucleus) to the workers — a long, straight strip of writing, three letters at a time. tRNA is more like a fleet of little delivery robots, all roughly the same size, each with a claw shaped to pick up exactly one type of grocery item, and a barcode reader on the other end that matches it to the right spot on the list.

  • Codon = a 3-letter word on the shopping list (mRNA)
  • Anticodon = the barcode reader on a delivery robot (tRNA)

Both molecules are single strands of linked nucleotides, and both use ribose sugar and uracil instead of DNA's deoxyribose and thymine. mRNA is a long, straight chain that carries the genetic message as a string of three-base "codons," and different mRNA molecules are different lengths depending on the protein being made. tRNA, by contrast, folds back on itself into a clover-leaf shape held together by hydrogen bonding between its own bases; it carries an amino acid at one end and a three-base "anticodon" at the other, and every tRNA molecule is roughly the same short length.

MoleculeStructure to quote
mRNALinear, single-stranded polynucleotide; no amino-acid binding site; carries the code as a sequence of codons (base triplets); different mRNAs have different lengths.
tRNAClover-leaf shaped (folded by internal base pairing/hydrogen bonding); has an amino-acid binding site at one end and an anticodon triplet at the other; all tRNAs are a similar, short length.
SimilarityBoth are single-stranded polynucleotides containing ribose and uracil (not thymine) — useful if a question asks for the similarity rather than the contrast.
📌

Fully comparative points, for a "contrast" question: hydrogen bonding/base pairing — mRNA has none, tRNA does (it folds on itself); overall shape — mRNA is linear, tRNA is clover-leaf; amino-acid binding site — mRNA has none, tRNA does; length — mRNAs vary, all tRNAs are similar/short; three-base unit carried — mRNA has codons, tRNA has an anticodon.

▶ 🚩 Trap — a fact about only one molecule

Every statement in a "contrast" answer must be genuinely comparative. A true fact about mRNA alone — with no stated comparison to tRNA, or vice versa — scores nothing, even if the fact itself is entirely correct.

Section 3 · spec content point

✍️ Transcription: producing mRNA from DNA

This is one unbroken causal chain — helicase, template strand, complementary base pairing, then RNA polymerase — that ends differently depending on whether the cell is prokaryotic or eukaryotic.

📖

What the specification says: "Transcription as the production of mRNA from DNA. The role of RNA polymerase in joining mRNA nucleotides. In prokaryotes, transcription results directly in the production of mRNA from DNA. In eukaryotes, transcription results in the production of pre-mRNA; this is then spliced to form mRNA."

1
2
3

Think of DNA as a zip that gets unzipped (that's helicase). A photocopier (RNA polymerase) reads just one side of the unzipped strand and types out a copy — but in a slightly different alphabet, swapping every "T" for a "U". In an advanced office (a eukaryotic cell), the first copy printed is only a rough draft, full of paragraphs that don't matter. An editor cuts those paragraphs out and staples the useful ones together before the final copy leaves the office. A simpler office (a bacterium, a prokaryotic cell) skips the editing stage entirely — its first printed copy is already the finished version.

An enzyme called DNA helicase breaks the hydrogen bonds holding the two DNA strands together, so they separate. Only one of the two strands — the template strand — is actually used as the pattern. Free RNA nucleotides floating in the nucleus pair up with the exposed template bases by complementary base pairing (using uracil in place of thymine), and RNA polymerase then joins these RNA nucleotides together into a continuous strand. In a bacterium, that strand is already finished mRNA. In a eukaryotic cell, this first strand is only "pre-mRNA" — it still contains non-coding sections that have to be cut out and the coding sections joined together (splicing) before it becomes mature mRNA.

  1. DNA helicase breaks the hydrogen bonds between the bases on the two DNA strands, so the strands separate ("unzip").
  2. Only one strand — the template strand — is used as the pattern; it is read 3′ → 5′, so the new mRNA is built 5′ → 3′, antiparallel to it.
  3. Free RNA nucleotides are attracted to the exposed template bases and form complementary base pairs: A→U, T→A, C→G, G→C (RNA uses uracil, never thymine).
  4. RNA polymerase catalyses the joining of adjacent RNA nucleotides, forming phosphodiester bonds between them.
  5. This produces pre-mRNA.
  6. In eukaryotes, splicing then removes the introns and joins the exons to form mature mRNA (see the diagram below). In prokaryotes, there is no splicing step — the strand made in step 4 is already finished mRNA, because prokaryotic DNA has no introns.
RNA polymerase moves along the template strand (read 3′→5′), building mRNA 5′→3′ — identical to the coding strand except U replaces T.
▶ 🚩 The single costliest error on this spec point

"DNA polymerase" is the wrong enzyme for transcription — it must be RNA polymerase. And RNA polymerase's role is always to catalyse phosphodiester bond formation between RNA nucleotides — never "forms hydrogen bonds between nucleotides/bases." Hydrogen bonds are what helicase breaks, earlier in the process, to open the DNA double helix — a completely different enzyme, a different bond, and a different stage.

Splicing: turning pre-mRNA into mature mRNA (eukaryotes only)

In a eukaryotic cell, the strand RNA polymerase first produces is only pre-mRNA — it still contains non-coding sections called introns. Splicing cuts these introns out and joins the coding sections, called exons, together before the finished, mature mRNA ever leaves the nucleus.

Introns (non-coding) are removed; exons (coding) are joined into one continuous mature mRNA before it leaves the nucleus. DNA itself never leaves the nucleus — only mRNA carries the code out.
Point of comparisonProkaryotesEukaryotes
Pre-mRNANot produced — transcription gives finished mRNA directlyProduced first, before splicing
SplicingNever occursOccurs, in the nucleus, before mRNA leaves
IntronsNot present in the DNAPresent in the DNA; removed from the pre-mRNA
🔬

Synoptic note: prokaryotes also lack the transcription factors of the eukaryotic type — part of why a bacterium can struggle to express a gene taken from a eukaryotic organism (relevant to gene technologies later in the course).

▶ 🚩 Trap — reversing which cells have introns

Don't reverse this: it is prokaryotic DNA that lacks introns, not eukaryotic. A common error is believing eukaryotic DNA specifically — or plant DNA specifically — is the type without introns. It's the opposite: all eukaryotic DNA (plant and animal alike) contains introns and needs splicing. And never describe introns as "made of DNA" — by the time splicing happens, introns are being removed from (RNA) pre-mRNA, not from DNA. Also keep "exons code for protein" precise: the credited idea is that the base sequence within exons codes for the amino acid sequence, not that exons "are" the protein.

Application: blocking transcription

If a molecule is complementary to a stretch of the DNA — so it can form base pairs with it — it can physically prevent or reduce RNA polymerase binding or activity, or stop free RNA nucleotides binding, which reduces or stops transcription of that gene altogether.

Section 4 · spec content point

🏗️ Translation: building a polypeptide at the ribosome

Codon, anticodon and amino acid, brought together at the ribosome — and the role ATP plays in making each link of the growing chain.

📖

What the specification says: "Translation as the production of polypeptides from the sequence of codons carried by mRNA. The roles of ribosomes, tRNA and ATP."

1
2
3

Picture a ribosome as a little train moving along a mRNA track, stopping at each three-letter station (a codon). At every station, a delivery truck (a tRNA) pulls up carrying one specific passenger (an amino acid) — but the truck can only stop at a station if its own keycode (the anticodon) matches the station's codeword exactly. Each new passenger holds hands with the last one to form a growing chain (the polypeptide). The train keeps going, station after station, until it reaches a stop sign with no matching truck (a stop codon) — a guard (a release factor) then lets the finished chain go.

  • Polypeptide = the growing chain of linked amino acids
  • Peptide bond = the "handshake" linking two amino acids together

mRNA attaches to a ribosome, which finds and starts at the "start" codon, AUG. A tRNA molecule then brings a specific amino acid to the ribosome, and its anticodon pairs up — by complementary base pairing — with the codon on the mRNA. The ribosome then shifts along to the next codon, and the process repeats: another matching tRNA arrives, and the new amino acid joins onto the growing chain by forming a peptide bond, using energy released from ATP. Each tRNA is released once its amino acid has been added. This continues, codon by codon, until a "stop" codon is reached — no tRNA matches a stop codon, so a release factor steps in instead and lets go of the finished polypeptide.

  1. mRNA associates with a ribosome (it may attach to the rough endoplasmic reticulum).
  2. The ribosome moves to, or finds, the start codon (AUG).
  3. A tRNA brings/carries a specific amino acid to the ribosome.
  4. The anticodon on the tRNA is complementary to the codon on the mRNA.
  5. The ribosome moves along to the next codon (it fits around two codons at once, so it can hold two tRNAs simultaneously).
  6. Amino acids join by peptide bonds, formed in a condensation reaction, using energy from ATP; the tRNA is released once its amino acid has been transferred.
  7. This repeats, codon by codon, until a stop codon (UAA/UAG/UGA) is reached — no tRNA has a complementary anticodon for a stop codon, so a release factor binds instead and the completed polypeptide is released.
Codon and anticodon are always complementary, never identical — codon AUG pairs with anticodon UAC (carrying Met); each tRNA carries one specific amino acid, and peptide bonds form as the ribosome moves 5′→3′ along the mRNA.
📌

The specific role of ATP: it releases/provides energy, so that peptide bonds can form between amino acids, and so that a used tRNA can be released from the ribosome once its amino acid has been transferred. Never write that ATP "produces" energy — it releases energy already stored in its phosphate bonds.

How this sets the structure of the finished protein (synoptic link)

LevelWhat defines it
PrimaryThe order/sequence of amino acids, joined by peptide bonds formed by condensation.
SecondaryFolding of the polypeptide chain due to hydrogen bonding (α-helix / β-pleated sheet).
Tertiary3-D folding due to hydrogen, ionic and disulfide bonds/interactions — creates the active site in enzymes, or complementary/specific shapes in antibodies, carrier proteins and receptors.
QuaternaryTwo or more polypeptide chains (a prosthetic group is also accepted here).

This question type fuses 3.4.2 directly with the separate proteins content point — the same combined demand is set across the course with an identical set of credited points, so it is worth learning here too.

▶ 🚩 Trap — mixing up which molecule carries what

Never say the ribosome reads "DNA" — it reads mRNA. Don't muddle which molecule carries the codon (mRNA) versus the anticodon (tRNA). And never describe amino acids joining by "hydrogen bonds" — it's a peptide bond, formed by condensation.

Section 5 · spec content point

🔑 Using the genetic code

You will never be asked to recall a codon table from memory — every question supplies one, or supplies enough sequence information to work from. The actual skill being tested is applying the U-for-T rule correctly, in the correct order, to whichever molecule the question names.

📖

What the specification says: "Students will not be required to recall in written papers specific codons and the amino acids for which they code." (The related "students should be able to" objective — relating base sequences to amino acid sequences using supplied data, and interpreting experimental data on nucleic acids — is the practical skill this note is scoping.)

1
2
3

Imagine a translation dictionary between three different languages — DNA-speak, mRNA-speak and protein-speak. In the exam, you're always handed that dictionary — you never have to memorise the phrasebook by heart. The only skill is reading the right column, in the right order, without accidentally mixing up which language you started in.

You'll always be given a genetic-code table in the exam, so nothing here needs memorising — the skill is reading the question carefully enough to know exactly which sequence you've been given (a DNA template strand, a DNA coding strand, or an mRNA sequence) before converting it, and then reading off the amino acids in the correct order. There's also a size gotcha: because eukaryotic genes contain introns that get removed before translation, you can't just take the whole gene's DNA base count and divide by three — only the exon (coding) sequence in the mature mRNA actually determines protein length. And if a base in that sequence changes, it can change the amino acid coded for, which can ripple all the way through to changing how a protein folds and works.

GivenHow to get the next one
DNA template strandmRNA codon = complementary, with U in place of T (e.g. DNA 3′-TAC-5′ → mRNA 5′-AUG-3′)
DNA coding (sense) strandmRNA is the same sequence, with U in place of T
mRNA codontRNA anticodon = complementary (mRNA AUG → tRNA UAC)
mRNA codonAmino acid — read from the genetic-code table supplied
📌

Read the question carefully: AQA always specifies whether the sequence given is the template strand, the coding strand or the mRNA — get this wrong and every base after it is wrong too. The final answer must be given in the correct reading order.

Calculating numbers of bases, triplets and amino acids

Number of amino acids = number of mRNA bases ÷ 3. In a eukaryotic gene, the DNA has more bases than the mature mRNA, because introns are removed by splicing — so gene length cannot simply be divided by three; the polypeptide's length is determined by the exons only.

Explaining the effect of a base-sequence change

A changed base sequence can code for a different amino acid — a different primary structure. Follow the chain on: a different amino acid sequence can mean different hydrogen, ionic or disulfide bonds forming, giving a different tertiary structure; for an enzyme, this can mean the active site is no longer complementary to the substrate, so enzyme-substrate complexes can no longer form.

▶ 🚩 Trap — vague or incomplete reasoning

"Different amino acids produced/formed" on its own is not enough — the idea of a changed sequence is essential, not just that different amino acids appear somewhere. A bare reference to "different bonds" with no mention of the sequence link earns nothing either.

Interpreting experimental data on nucleic acids

  • Radioactively labelled bases tracked over time → show where mRNA is made and where it goes (nucleus → cytoplasm).
  • An RNA polymerase inhibitor added → mRNA production stops, but protein synthesis carries on briefly — evidence that mRNA is short-lived.
  • Pre-mRNA length compared with mature mRNA length → a shorter mature mRNA is evidence that splicing has occurred.

Antisense RNA and siRNA: blocking translation without touching transcription

  1. Antisense mRNA is complementary in sequence to the sense (functional) mRNA.
  2. It binds/base pairs with the sense mRNA, so double-stranded RNA forms.
  3. Ribosomes cannot bind to this double-stranded region.
  4. This prevents/reduces translation, and so reduces production of the protein — transcription itself is unaffected.

Section 6 · exam technique

🎯 Common mistakes & precise wording

This spec point is testable at high difficulty not because the biology is conceptually hard, but because so much of it rides on exact, molecule-specific terminology. These are the slips that come up again and again nationally.

Vague or wrong versionWhat to say instead
"RNA polymerase forms hydrogen bonds between nucleotides"RNA polymerase catalyses phosphodiester bond formation between RNA nucleotides — hydrogen bonds are broken by helicase, a different enzyme, earlier in the process
"DNA polymerase" joins the mRNA nucleotidesIt is RNA polymerase that joins mRNA nucleotides in transcription
Using "codon" for any three-base sequenceMatch the term to the molecule every time — DNA base triplet, mRNA codon, tRNA anticodon
"Amino acids join by hydrogen bonds"Amino acids join by peptide bonds, formed in a condensation reaction, using energy from ATP
"ATP produces energy" at translationATP releases/provides energy already stored in its phosphate bonds — it is not itself "produced" at this step
"Eukaryotic DNA doesn't have introns" (or "plant DNA doesn't")It's the reverse: prokaryotic DNA lacks introns; all eukaryotic DNA, plant and animal, contains them
"Introns are made of DNA"Introns are removed from (RNA) pre-mRNA during splicing, not from DNA
"Exons code for protein" left unqualifiedThe base sequence within exons codes for the amino acid sequence — exons aren't themselves "the protein"
"More gene copies means simply more enzyme"State the intermediate step: more gene copies → more transcription/mRNA made → more enzyme produced → faster reaction
Ribosome composition left vagueA ribosome is made of rRNA and protein — simple recall that's still very often answered incorrectly
▶ Why this spec point is rated so difficult

The biology itself isn't conceptually hard — it's a content-heavy point built almost entirely on terminology discipline. The two-enzymes/two-bonds trap (RNA polymerase forms phosphodiester bonds, never hydrogen bonds) and the triplet/codon/anticodon trio, correctly matched to DNA, mRNA and tRNA respectively, between them account for a large share of the marks lost nationally — not because students don't understand the process, but because the vocabulary has to be pinned to the correct molecule every single time it's used.

Section 7 · exam technique

📝 Using this in longer written answers

3.4.2 works well as the "mechanism" paragraph inside a longer essay-style answer — the concrete molecular machinery that converts a DNA base sequence into a functioning protein. The trick is closing the paragraph with a sentence that ties the mechanism back to whatever the title is actually about.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around controlled molecular movement

During translation, the ribosome moves precisely codon by codon along the mRNA, controlled by complementary codon-anticodon base pairing, which ensures the correct amino acid is added at each step. ATP hydrolysis provides the energy for peptide bond formation, controlling the timing of each addition to the chain. This is a tightly controlled, molecular-scale movement that has to be essentially error-free — an uncontrolled or incorrect step would change the resulting protein's structure, and therefore its function.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around the importance of base sequence

In transcription, RNA polymerase joins RNA nucleotides that are complementary to the DNA template strand (A→U, T→A, C→G, G→C), forming phosphodiester bonds to produce (pre-)mRNA. In translation, mRNA codons are matched by complementary tRNA anticodons, bringing specific amino acids together to be joined by peptide bonds in the exact order the sequence specifies. This is the mechanism by which a base sequence is converted into biological function — directly specifying a protein's primary structure, and, through that, its tertiary structure and its function.

Whichever essay title this content is used for, treat it as the mechanism paragraph: name the process precisely, then add one sentence linking it back to the theme in the title. It reads well straight after a paragraph on genes and the triplet code, and immediately before a paragraph on what happens when that sequence changes.

Memory hook — two machines, three names, two bonds. Transcription (in the nucleus: DNA → pre-mRNA → spliced mature mRNA) and translation (at the ribosome: mRNA → polypeptide) are the two machines. 5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Genome & proteome

  • Genome = complete set of genes in a cell/organism (or all the DNA in a cell/organism) — always at cell/organism level, never species/population.
  • Proteome = full range of proteins a cell can produce (at a given time), or what the genome/DNA can code for — "number of proteins" alone is never enough.

2 · mRNA & tRNA structure

  • Both single-stranded, ribose + uracil (not thymine).
  • mRNA: linear, no amino-acid site, carries codons, variable length.
  • tRNA: clover-leaf (internal H-bonding), has an amino-acid binding site + anticodon, all similar/short length.
  • Contrast answers must be genuinely comparative — a fact about one molecule alone scores nothing.

3 · Transcription

  • Helicase breaks H-bonds to separate DNA strands; the template strand (read 3′→5′) is used as the pattern.
  • Free RNA nucleotides base-pair complementarily: A→U, T→A, C→G, G→C.
  • RNA polymerase joins RNA nucleotides by phosphodiester bonds — never "hydrogen bonds," never "DNA polymerase."
  • Prokaryotes: mRNA made directly. Eukaryotes: pre-mRNA made, then spliced (introns out, exons joined) into mature mRNA.

4 · Translation

  • mRNA associates with a ribosome; the ribosome finds the start codon AUG.
  • tRNA anticodon pairs complementarily with the mRNA codon, delivering a specific amino acid.
  • Amino acids join by peptide bonds (condensation), using ATP; the ribosome moves codon by codon.
  • Continues until a stop codon (no matching tRNA) — a release factor ends translation.
  • ATP releases/provides energy for peptide bond formation and tRNA release — never "produces energy."

5 · Using the genetic code

  • You're always given a genetic-code table — check whether you're told the template strand, coding strand, or mRNA before converting.
  • Number of amino acids = mRNA bases ÷ 3 — never divide the whole eukaryotic gene length, since introns are removed first.
  • A base-sequence change alters the amino acid sequence (primary structure) → can alter tertiary structure → can alter an enzyme's active site.
  • Antisense RNA binds sense mRNA (forms double-stranded RNA) → blocks ribosome binding → reduces translation, not transcription.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Genome & proteome

❓ A textbook sentence reads: "The proteome of a species is huge." Explain why this would not be given credit, and rewrite it correctly.
Genome and proteome must both be defined at the level of a single cell or organism, not a species or population, so this sentence is at the wrong level of organisation. Corrected: "The proteome of a cell/organism is the full range of proteins that cell/organism is able to produce, at a given time."
❓ Give the fully credited definition of genome, including one accepted alternative wording.
The complete set of genes in a cell/organism. Also accepted: all the DNA in a cell/organism; all the genes/alleles/genetic material/genetic code in a cell/organism/person; or the total number of DNA bases in a cell/organism.
❓ A student defines proteome as simply "the number of proteins." Explain why this scores no marks, and give a version that would.
"Number of proteins" alone gives no qualifying detail about range or type, so it isn't credited. Fully credited version: "the range of proteins a cell is able to produce, at a given time, or the range of proteins the genome/DNA is able to code for."

mRNA & tRNA structure

❓ Give two genuinely comparative statements contrasting the structure of mRNA and tRNA.
Any two of: mRNA has no internal hydrogen bonding/base pairing and is linear, whereas tRNA folds into a clover-leaf shape held by internal hydrogen bonding; mRNA has no amino-acid binding site, tRNA does; mRNA carries codons, tRNA carries a single anticodon; different mRNAs vary in length, while all tRNAs are a similar, short length.
❓ A student's entire answer to a "contrast" question is: "mRNA is a straight chain." Explain why this alone would not score, and correct it.
A fact stated about only one molecule, with no comparison to the other, scores nothing — even if true. Corrected: "mRNA is a straight/linear chain, whereas tRNA is folded into a clover-leaf shape."
❓ State one structural feature mRNA and tRNA share.
Both are single-stranded polynucleotides built from ribose sugar and containing uracil rather than thymine.

Transcription

❓ Describe, in order, how mRNA is produced from a DNA template strand in a eukaryotic cell.
DNA helicase breaks the hydrogen bonds between the two DNA strands, so they separate; only the template strand is used as the pattern; free RNA nucleotides form complementary base pairs with the exposed template bases (A→U, T→A, C→G, G→C); RNA polymerase catalyses the formation of phosphodiester bonds between adjacent RNA nucleotides, joining them into a strand; this produces pre-mRNA, which is then spliced — introns removed, exons joined — to produce mature mRNA.
❓ A student writes: "DNA polymerase joins the mRNA nucleotides together by forming hydrogen bonds." Identify both errors and correct them.
The enzyme should be RNA polymerase, not DNA polymerase; and the bonds formed between adjacent RNA nucleotides are phosphodiester bonds, not hydrogen bonds — hydrogen bonds are broken by helicase earlier in the process, a different enzyme entirely.
❓ State two ways transcription in a eukaryotic cell differs from transcription in a prokaryotic cell.
Pre-mRNA is produced only in the eukaryotic cell; splicing occurs only in the eukaryotic cell (or: introns are removed only in the eukaryotic cell, since introns are not present in prokaryotic DNA).
❓ Suggest how a molecule that is complementary to part of a gene's DNA sequence could prevent that gene from being transcribed.
The molecule forms base pairs with the DNA; this prevents or reduces RNA polymerase binding/activity, or prevents free RNA nucleotides binding, reducing or stopping transcription of that gene.

Translation

❓ Describe the sequence of events at the ribosome that produces a polypeptide from an mRNA molecule.
mRNA associates with a ribosome; the ribosome moves to/finds the start codon (AUG); a tRNA carrying a specific amino acid binds, its anticodon forming complementary base pairs with the codon on the mRNA; the ribosome moves along to the next codon, holding two tRNAs/codons at once; amino acids are joined by peptide bonds in a condensation reaction, using energy from ATP; the tRNA is released once its amino acid has been transferred; this repeats until a stop codon is reached, and the completed polypeptide is released.
❓ State the specific role of ATP in translation.
ATP releases/provides energy; this allows peptide bonds to form between amino acids, and allows the tRNA to be released from the ribosome once its amino acid has joined the chain.
❓ Explain why a stop codon does not code for an amino acid.
No tRNA has an anticodon complementary to a stop codon, so no amino acid can be brought to the ribosome; instead, a release factor binds and the completed polypeptide is released.

Using the genetic code

❓ A DNA template strand reads 3′-TAC GGA ATT-5′. Give the corresponding mRNA sequence, with correct 5′/3′ orientation.
5′-AUG CCU UAA-3′, applying A→U, T→A, C→G, G→C in order along the template strand.
❓ Explain why the number of bases in a eukaryotic gene cannot simply be divided by three to find the number of amino acids in its polypeptide.
A eukaryotic gene's DNA contains introns as well as exons, and introns are removed during splicing before translation; only the exon (coding) sequence in the mature mRNA determines the polypeptide, so the mRNA base count — not the whole gene's DNA base count — should be divided by three.
❓ A base substitution changes one codon in an mRNA sequence. Explain the possible chain of effects on an enzyme's function.
A different amino acid may be coded for at that position, giving a different primary structure/amino acid sequence; this can change which hydrogen, ionic or disulfide bonds form, giving a different tertiary structure; if this alters the shape of the active site, it is no longer complementary to the substrate, so enzyme-substrate complexes can no longer form.
❓ Explain how antisense RNA can reduce production of a specific protein without affecting transcription of its gene.
The antisense RNA has a sequence complementary to the sense (functional) mRNA; it binds/base pairs with the sense mRNA, forming double-stranded RNA; this prevents ribosomes from binding to the mRNA; translation is therefore prevented or reduced, so less protein is produced, while transcription of the gene is unaffected.
