Inorganic Ions

Four named ions, four named jobs: hydrogen ions and pH, iron ions in haemoglobin, sodium ions in co-transport, and phosphate ions in DNA and ATP. A short spec point that only pays out when you can name the mechanism behind each ion, not just the association.

⚠️

The single rule that costs the most marks on this spec point: AQA names exactly four ions and wants a specific role plus a specific location for each — hydrogen ions and pH, iron ions as a component of haemoglobin, sodium ions in the co-transport of glucose and amino acids, phosphate ions as components of DNA and of ATP. This is rarely a genuine knowledge gap — students usually know the association ("iron in haemoglobin binds oxygen") but not the mechanism (how the sodium gradient used in co-transport is actually built and maintained). A vague "ions are important for cells" answer scores nothing here; every mark is tied to a named ion doing a named job.

Section 1 · spec content point

🧪 What is an inorganic ion?

Before the four named ions, the spec opens with a context statement — what an inorganic ion actually is, why some are abundant and others are barely detectable, and how a plant or cell actually gets hold of one in the first place.

📖

What the specification says: "Inorganic ions occur in solution in the cytoplasm and body fluids of organisms, some in high concentrations and others in very low concentrations."

1
2
3

Think of a cell like a busy warehouse, and ions like different types of staff badges floating around inside it. Some badges — sodium, chloride — are everywhere, doing routine jobs. Other badges — iron, magnesium — are rare, there's hardly any of them about, but they belong to specialist staff the warehouse genuinely can't function without. To let new badge-holders in from outside (from the soil, for a plant), the warehouse has to actively pull them in, which costs energy.

  • Ion — a tiny charged particle, carrying either a + or − charge
  • Concentration — how crowded something is; "high" = lots of it, "low" = hardly any
  • Active transport — moving something into a cell using energy, even against the flow it would naturally take

Ions are electrically charged particles dissolved in the watery parts of cells and body fluids. Some, like sodium and chloride, are around in large amounts; others, like iron or magnesium, are only present in tiny traces but are still vital, because what a particular ion can do depends on its properties — its charge, its size, whether it can change oxidation state — not on how much of it there is. Plants take these ions up through their roots using active transport, meaning the plant has to spend energy (ATP, made during respiration) to move ions in, often against their concentration gradient.

An inorganic ion is a charged particle that does not contain carbon, dissolved throughout the cytoplasm and body fluids of an organism. Some ions are present in comparatively high concentrations — sodium and chloride ions are the obvious examples — while others, such as the iron ion locked inside every haemoglobin molecule or the magnesium ion at the centre of chlorophyll, are present in tiny amounts yet are still absolutely essential. The idea worth carrying into every other section on this page is that the role each ion plays always comes back to its properties: its charge, its size, and — for a few ions such as iron — its ability to change oxidation state.

Concentration in the organismExample ion(s)Still essential?
Relatively highSodium, chlorideYes — e.g. sodium drives co-transport (Section 4)
Very lowIron (in haemoglobin), magnesium (in chlorophyll)Yes — a shortage still has serious consequences
📌

How ions are taken up by roots: uptake against a concentration gradient is achieved by active transport, and active transport requires ATP, supplied by aerobic respiration. This two-step chain — active transport, powered by ATP from respiration — earns the full marks on its own; nothing else needs to be added.

▶ 🚩 Trap — don't bring bacteria into this answer

When asked how ions are absorbed by roots, do not mention bacteria, mycorrhizal fungi, or nitrogen fixation — the credited answer is the clean two-step chain above (active transport; requires ATP/aerobic respiration), and any reference to bacteria in this specific answer is rejected, not just ignored.

Section 2 · spec content point

💧 Hydrogen ions — pH, enzymes & chemiosmosis

One ion, two completely different jobs: setting the pH that enzymes are so sensitive to, and powering ATP synthesis directly via chemiosmosis.

📖

What the specification says: "Each type of ion has a specific role, depending on its properties. Students should be able to recognise the role of ions in the following topics: hydrogen ions and pH…"

1
2
3

Picture an enzyme as a hand shaped perfectly to hold one specific key (its substrate). Hydrogen ions are like tiny magnets floating around — too many of them (an acidic environment) start pulling that hand out of shape, so the key doesn't fit anymore. Once the hand's shape changes, it can't do its job — the enzyme has "denatured." Separately, hydrogen ions can also be put to work: pile enough of them up on one side of a membrane and let them rush through one particular protein turnstile (ATP synthase), and that rush is what builds ATP.

  • pH — a scale measuring how acidic or alkaline something is; a lower number means more acidic, more H⁺
  • Denatured — when a protein's 3D shape is permanently changed, so it stops working
  • Chemiosmosis — using the flow of H⁺ ions across a membrane to generate ATP

The number of hydrogen ions in a solution decides how acidic it is — more hydrogen ions means a lower, more acidic pH. Enzymes are extremely sensitive to this: changing the pH changes the electrical charges on parts of the enzyme's protein chain, which pulls apart the bonds holding its 3D shape together. Once that shape — including the active site — changes, the enzyme stops working; this is called denaturing. Hydrogen ions also power ATP production directly: a build-up of H⁺ on one side of a membrane (in chloroplasts during photosynthesis, and in mitochondria during respiration) flows back through a protein called ATP synthase, and that flow is what generates ATP.

The concentration of hydrogen ions in a solution is what determines its pH, and the relationship is inverse: the more hydrogen ions present, the lower — more acidic — the pH, following pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]. This matters directly for protein structure, because pH governs the charges carried by the R groups of amino acids. Shift the pH away from an enzyme's optimum and those charge changes break the ionic and hydrogen bonds holding the tertiary structure together; the active site loses its precise shape, is no longer complementary to its substrate, and the enzyme is denatured.

Hydrogen ions also sit right at the centre of chemiosmosis. A proton gradient is built up across the thylakoid membrane during the light-dependent reactions of photosynthesis, and across the inner mitochondrial membrane during respiration; in both cases it's the flow of H⁺ back down that gradient, through ATP synthase, that actually generates ATP.

A rise in [H⁺] lowers pH; the resulting charge change on amino-acid R groups breaks ionic/hydrogen bonds, alters tertiary structure, and denatures the enzyme.
🔁

Synoptic thread: rising CO₂ concentration in the blood produces more carbonic acid and therefore more H⁺, lowering blood pH. Chemoreceptors detect this fall in pH and raise heart rate and breathing rate; the same fall in pH also causes the Bohr effect, shifting haemoglobin's oxygen-binding behaviour so oxygen is released more readily to respiring tissue (Section 3).

Section 3 · spec content point

🩸 Iron ions — haemoglobin's reversible oxygen binding

The whole mark here hangs on one property — reversibility — not on the fine chemistry of the iron itself.

📖

What the specification says: "…iron ions as a component of haemoglobin…"

1
2
3

Picture haemoglobin as a taxi with four seats, and each seat has its own "sticky hand" (a haem group with iron in it) that can grab one oxygen passenger. The clever bit is that the hand's grip is reversible — like Velcro rather than superglue — so it grabs oxygen easily where there's plenty around (the lungs) and lets go easily where oxygen is running low (the muscles and organs that need it).

  • Haem group — the iron-containing part of haemoglobin that actually holds onto oxygen
  • Reversible binding — a bond that can form and then break again, rather than being permanent
  • Polypeptide — a chain of amino acids; haemoglobin is built from four of them

Haemoglobin is made of four separate protein chains, and each one has its own iron-containing part (a haem group) that can grab hold of one oxygen molecule — so one haemoglobin molecule can carry four oxygen molecules in total. The key feature of iron here is that its bond with oxygen is reversible: it picks oxygen up easily where oxygen is plentiful (the lungs) and lets it go easily where oxygen is scarce (respiring tissues). You don't need to know or state the exact oxidation state of the iron — just that binding oxygen, and letting it go again, is what makes haemoglobin useful.

Haemoglobin is built from four polypeptide chains, and each one carries its own iron-containing haem group. Each haem group binds exactly one oxygen molecule, so a single haemoglobin molecule can carry up to four oxygen molecules at once. The property that makes this useful is that iron binds oxygen reversibly — the same bond that forms easily in the oxygen-rich environment of the lungs breaks just as readily in the lower-oxygen environment of respiring tissue, so haemoglobin can load oxygen in one place and unload it in another.

Binding is reversible — the same property that lets oxygen load onto iron in the high-oxygen lungs also lets it unload at the lower-oxygen tissues. Each of the four polypeptide subunits contributes one haem group, so one haemoglobin molecule carries four O₂.
📌

Credited wording: "haemoglobin binds/associates with oxygen" or "haemoglobin transports/loads oxygen" is what's accepted for the role of iron ions. You do not need to name the oxidation state — reference to Fe²⁺ or Fe³⁺ is ignored, not required.

▶ ⚠️ Unfamiliar-context warning

This point has been set in an unfamiliar context — iron ion transport across membranes via ferroportin channel proteins — where the underlying reasoning (reversible binding, or a change of oxidation state) is what's rewarded, not the channel-protein detail itself. Don't be thrown by an unfamiliar noun in a question; find the ion property the question is actually testing.

Section 4 · spec content point

🔄 Sodium ions — the co-transport mechanism

The pump creates the gradient; co-transport spends it. Step through the sequence below — this is the exact order examiners want narrated.

📖

What the specification says: "…sodium ions in the co-transport of glucose and amino acids…"

1
2
3

Think of the sodium–potassium pump as a bouncer with a machine that costs money (ATP) to run: it forces sodium ions out of the club (the cell) and lets a couple of potassium ions in, building up a big crowd of sodium waiting outside the door. Once that crowd has built up, opening a second door lets the sodium rush back in on its own — no machine needed this time — and as it rushes through, it drags a friend (a glucose or amino acid molecule) in through the same door with it, even though that friend wouldn't normally be able to get in by itself.

  • Concentration gradient — a difference in how crowded a substance is between two places
  • Active transport — moving a substance using energy (ATP), often against its gradient
  • Facilitated diffusion — a substance moving down its gradient through a specific protein, no energy needed directly

A pump in the cell membrane (the sodium–potassium pump) uses energy from ATP to push sodium ions out of the cell — three out for every two potassium ions let back in. This builds up a strong sodium gradient, with much more sodium outside the cell than inside. A different protein then lets sodium flow back in down that gradient, and as it flows in, it drags a glucose or amino acid molecule in alongside it — even against that molecule's own gradient. The pump step costs energy directly (active transport); the second step doesn't use energy directly at all — it's powered indirectly by the gradient the pump built, so it counts as facilitated diffusion.

Sodium ions are actively transported out of an epithelial cell by the sodium–potassium pump, which uses ATP directly to move three sodium ions out for every two potassium ions moved in — both against their own concentration gradients. The result is a sodium concentration gradient across the membrane, with far less sodium inside the cell than outside it. A separate co-transport protein then allows sodium to diffuse back into the cell down that gradient, and as it does so it carries a glucose or amino acid molecule in with it — even against that molecule's own concentration gradient. Keep the two steps distinct: the pump step is active transport, because it uses ATP directly, but the co-transport step itself is facilitated diffusion, powered only indirectly by the gradient the pump created upstream.

Click a numbered dot, or use Next / Back above, to step through the sequence — always narrate in this order: pump first, gradient second, co-transport third.

The same underlying sodium mechanism shows up in more than one context beyond co-transport. In the kidney and the blood, sodium ions lower the water potential of the surrounding fluid, drawing water back in by osmosis. In neurones, the sodium–potassium pump is what establishes the resting potential in the first place, and it's the subsequent influx of sodium through voltage-gated channels that causes depolarisation and produces the action potential.

▶ 🚩 Trap — never call co-transport "active transport"

Co-transport itself is facilitated diffusion — it is powered indirectly by the gradient the sodium–potassium pump created upstream, not by ATP used directly at that step. Calling co-transport "active transport" is one of the most repeated slips on this spec point.

Section 5 · spec content point

🧬 Phosphate ions — DNA and ATP

One ion, three separate jobs — backbone bond, energy currency, and membrane structure — and the exam checks all three.

📖

What the specification says: "…and phosphate ions as components of DNA and of ATP."

1
2
3

Phosphate is like one very useful connector piece that shows up in three completely different builds. In DNA, it's the link that joins the "beads" (nucleotides) together into a chain. In ATP, it's one of three connector pieces stacked together — snap the last one off, and that releases a burst of energy the cell can spend, a bit like letting go of a stretched elastic band. And in the fatty molecules that make up cell membranes, the phosphate piece is the "water-loving" end, which is exactly why those molecules arrange themselves into the double-layered wall that surrounds every cell.

  • Phosphodiester bond — the bond that links nucleotides together via a phosphate group
  • Hydrolysis — breaking a bond apart using water, which releases energy in ATP's case
  • Hydrophilic — "water-loving"; dissolves in or is attracted to water
  • Phosphorylation — adding a phosphate group onto a molecule, usually making it more reactive

Phosphate does three jobs, and each one comes up in exam questions. In DNA/RNA it links nucleotides together to build the sugar–phosphate backbone. In ATP, breaking off the last of three phosphate groups releases energy the cell can use, and the released phosphate can go on to phosphorylate — and so activate — other molecules. And in phospholipids, phosphate is the water-loving head that makes the membrane bilayer form in the first place. Plants also need phosphate from the soil to build all three of these things, so a plant without enough phosphate simply can't grow properly.

In DNA and RNA, phosphate ions join adjacent nucleotides together through phosphodiester bonds, forming the sugar–phosphate backbone that runs along the outside of each strand. In ATP, three phosphate groups are joined in a chain; hydrolysing the bond holding the terminal phosphate group in place releases energy — never say this energy is "produced," since hydrolysis releases energy that was already stored in the bond — and the inorganic phosphate that's released can go on to phosphorylate other compounds, usually making them more reactive. Phosphate also forms the hydrophilic head of a phospholipid, which is precisely why phospholipids arrange themselves into a bilayer in water; an answer that mentions phospholipids without connecting that back to membrane structure will not be credited.

The same ion also underpins plant growth: phosphate is absorbed by roots — often with help from mycorrhizal fungi — and used to build DNA, ATP and phospholipids, so a shortage of phosphate directly limits how much a plant can grow.

Same ion, three contexts: joining nucleotides via the phosphodiester bond in DNA/RNA; forming the three phosphate groups of ATP, whose hydrolysis releases energy and phosphorylates other molecules; and forming the hydrophilic head of the phospholipid bilayer.
"Name a phosphate-containing compound"Accepted answers
Nucleic acids & energy carriersRNA, DNA, ATP/ADP, NADP, AMP
Membranes / metabolismmembranes/phospholipids, phosphorylation, RuBP, triose phosphate, GP

If neither DNA nor RNA is named directly, one mark is still allowed for "nucleotide," "nucleic acid," "phosphodiester bonds," or "sugar-phosphate backbone."

▶ 🚩 Trap — "energy produced" and "phospholipids" without membranes

Never write "energy produced" for the ATP mark — ATP hydrolysis releases energy, it does not produce it. And writing "phospholipids" without any reference to membranes is ignored rather than credited — always tie the phosphate/phospholipid point back to membrane structure.

Section 6 · exam technique

🎯 The capped 5-mark question — all four ions at once

This spec point is regularly tested with one question asking for the role of "named inorganic ions" across the board. The highest-scoring strategy is one clean sentence per ion, not an essay on your favourite one.

IronCompulsory — you must have this mark to reach 5 at allHaemoglobin binds/transports oxygen

PhosphateCapped at 3Phosphodiester bond/backbone; used in ATP; phosphorylation; osmosis/water potential (accept this or the phospholipid point, not both toward the cap)
✍️

A clean 5/5 spread reads like this: "Haemoglobin transports oxygen using the iron in its haem groups. That's five clean marking points from three short sentences — nothing spent on detail beyond what's asked.

Never write "energy produced" for the ATP mark. Naming the Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ oxidation state is also unnecessary — it costs time without earning credit in this question.

Section 7 · exam technique

⚠️ Common mistakes & precise wording

This is one of the least conceptually demanding spec points in the whole of 3.1 — the difficulty is almost entirely in precise wording, not in understanding.

Vague versionWhat to say instead
"Energy is produced" from ATP hydrolysisEnergy is released from the phosphate bond — it was already stored there
"Phospholipids" on their ownAlways tie it back to membrane structure — phosphate is the hydrophilic head
Co-transport described as active transportCo-transport is facilitated diffusion, powered indirectly by the pump's gradient
"Ions are important for cells"Name the specific ion, its specific role, and its specific location
Sodium–potassium pump direction forgotten3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in — both against their concentration gradients, using ATP
Naming Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ unpromptedNot required — the credited property is reversible binding, not the oxidation number
Mentioning bacteria in root ion-uptake answersStick to: active transport, requiring ATP from aerobic respiration
▶ Where the actual difficulty is

Examiner reports consistently note that students know the association for each ion — "iron in haemoglobin binds oxygen" — but not the underlying mechanism, especially how the sodium gradient used in co-transport is built and maintained. The recurring A2-level slip is the sodium–potassium pump direction: which ion moves in and which moves out. The fix isn't new understanding — it's learning one mechanism sentence per ion and reciting it precisely.

Section 8 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

3.1.8 works well as a supporting paragraph in longer essay-style answers on more than one theme — the trick is naming the ion explicitly rather than describing the process alone.

Whenever a longer answer or essay title touches on ions, metabolism, or transport, this spec point supplies a ready-made opening: inorganic ions are dissolved throughout the cytoplasm and body fluids of an organism, sometimes in very different concentrations, and each one plays a specific role that comes down to its own particular properties — charge, size, or the ability to change oxidation state. From there, naming the ion responsible for a process — rather than describing the process alone — is what earns credit: hydrogen ions for enzyme control and chemiosmosis, iron ions for oxygen transport, sodium ions for co-transport and nerve signalling, phosphate ions for the very molecules, DNA and ATP, that store and release metabolic energy.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around bonding

Ions contribute to biological function through the bonds their properties allow: phosphate forms covalent phosphodiester bonds that build the sugar-phosphate backbone of DNA, iron forms a reversible binding interaction with oxygen inside each haem group, and hydrogen ions disrupt the ionic and hydrogen bonds that hold an enzyme's tertiary structure together when pH shifts. The same underlying idea — that a charged particle's specific properties dictate exactly which bond it can form — runs through every one of these examples.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around active transport

Ion-driven active transport supplies the raw materials cells need to build larger molecules: the sodium–potassium pump actively maintains the sodium gradient that co-transport proteins use to bring glucose and amino acids into epithelial cells, while root cells use active transport, powered by ATP from aerobic respiration, to take up phosphate ions needed to build DNA, ATP and phospholipids. In both cases, ion movement driven directly by ATP is what makes a second, indirect transport process possible.

  • 3.3.3 Digestion and absorption — co-transport of glucose/amino acids in the ileum.

  • 3.6.2.1 / 3.6.2.2 Nerve impulses and synaptic transmission — sodium, potassium and calcium ions.
Section 9

🧠 Memory hook & final checklist

🧠

Memory hook — H·Fe·Na·P, in exam order. Hydrogen sets pH. Iron feels oxygen (haemoglobin). Sodium needs a gradient (co-transport). Phosphate is the backbone and the battery (DNA and ATP). One ion, one role, one location — that's the whole spec point.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · What is an inorganic ion?

  • An ion is a charged particle, no carbon, dissolved in cytoplasm/body fluids.
  • High concentration: sodium, chloride. Very low but essential: iron (haemoglobin), magnesium (chlorophyll).
  • Role depends on properties: charge, size, ability to change oxidation state.
  • Root uptake = active transport, needs ATP from aerobic respiration — never mention bacteria.

2 · Hydrogen ions — pH & chemiosmosis

  • [H⁺] sets pH: pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]; more H⁺ = lower pH.
  • pH change → charges on R groups change → ionic/hydrogen bonds break → tertiary structure altered → enzyme denatured.
  • H⁺ drives chemiosmosis: proton gradient across thylakoid/inner mitochondrial membrane → ATP synthase → ATP.
  • Synoptic: rising CO₂ → more H⁺ → lower blood pH → chemoreceptors raise heart/breathing rate; also causes the Bohr effect.

3 · Iron ions — haemoglobin

  • 4 polypeptides, 4 haem groups (Fe-containing), 4 O₂ per haemoglobin.
  • Reversible binding: loads in lungs (high O₂), unloads at tissues (low O₂).
  • Credited wording: binds/associates/transports/loads oxygen. Oxidation state (Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺) not required.
  • Unfamiliar contexts still test the same property: reversible binding / change of oxidation state.

4 · Sodium ions — co-transport

  • Na⁺/K⁺ pump: active transport, ATP, 3 Na⁺ out / 2 K⁺ in → creates the gradient.
  • Co-transport protein: Na⁺ diffuses back in down the gradient, drags glucose/amino acid with it — facilitated diffusion, NOT active transport.
  • Same mechanism elsewhere: lowers water potential → water reabsorption (kidney); sets resting potential + depolarisation (neurones).
  • Always narrate in order: pump first, then gradient, then co-transport.

5 · Phosphate ions — DNA & ATP

  • DNA/RNA: joins nucleotides via phosphodiester bond, forms the sugar-phosphate backbone.
  • ATP: 3 phosphate groups; hydrolysis of terminal bond releases (never "produces") energy; released Pᵢ phosphorylates other molecules.
  • Phospholipids: phosphate = hydrophilic head — always tie back to membrane structure.
  • Plant growth: phosphate uptake (roots, mycorrhizae) needed for DNA, ATP, phospholipids.
🧠

H·Fe·Na·P. Hydrogen sets pH. Iron feels oxygen. Sodium needs a gradient. Phosphate is the backbone and the battery. One ion, one role, one location.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

What is an inorganic ion?

❓ Explain how sodium ions and iron ions differ in their typical concentration in the body, and explain why iron is still described as "essential" despite this.
Sodium ions occur in relatively high concentration in the cytoplasm and body fluids; iron ions occur in very low concentration, largely as a component of haemoglobin. Iron remains essential because the role of an ion depends on its properties, not its abundance — even a very low concentration of iron is sufficient to allow haemoglobin to transport oxygen, so a shortage still has serious physiological consequences.
❓ Outline how inorganic ions are absorbed by root hair cells from the soil.
Ion uptake by root hair cells occurs by active transport; active transport requires ATP, supplied by aerobic respiration in the root cells. (Any reference to bacteria or nitrogen-fixing organisms is not creditable in this context.)
❓ A student writes "ions are important for keeping cells working properly." Explain why this statement alone would not be awarded marks, and rewrite it so that it would be creditable for iron ions specifically.
A general statement that ions are "important" does not identify a named ion, a specific role, or a location, so it earns no marks. A creditable version names all three: iron ions are a component of haemoglobin, where they bind/transport oxygen.

Hydrogen ions — pH & chemiosmosis

❓ Explain, in terms of pH, why a fall in the pH of the cytoplasm can cause an enzyme to stop working.
A fall in pH means an increase in hydrogen ion concentration. This changes the charges on the R groups of the enzyme's amino acids, breaking the ionic and hydrogen bonds that hold its tertiary structure in place; the active site is no longer complementary to the substrate, and the enzyme is denatured.
❓ State the mathematical relationship between hydrogen ion concentration and pH, and use it to explain why a solution with a higher [H⁺] has a lower pH.
pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]. Because pH is the negative logarithm of hydrogen ion concentration, an increase in [H⁺] produces a decrease in pH — the two are inversely related.
❓ Explain the role of hydrogen ions in the synthesis of ATP by chemiosmosis.
A hydrogen ion (proton) gradient is built up across a membrane — the thylakoid membrane in photosynthesis, or the inner mitochondrial membrane in respiration. Hydrogen ions diffuse back down this gradient through ATP synthase, and this flow of ions provides the energy used to synthesise ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate.

Iron ions — haemoglobin

❓ Explain the property of iron ions that allows haemoglobin to both load oxygen in the lungs and unload it at respiring tissues.
Iron ions bind oxygen reversibly. This reversible binding allows oxygen to associate with the haem group where oxygen concentration is high (the lungs), and to dissociate from it where oxygen concentration is low (respiring tissue).
❓ A haemoglobin molecule is described as having "four oxygen-binding sites." Explain the structural basis for this statement.
Haemoglobin is composed of four polypeptide chains, and each chain contains one iron-containing haem group; each haem group can bind one oxygen molecule, giving four oxygen-binding sites, and so four oxygen molecules, per haemoglobin molecule.
❓ In an unfamiliar context, a transport protein is shown to release iron ions that then bind reversibly to oxygen elsewhere in the body. Suggest which property of the iron ion is being tested.
The property being tested is the iron ion's ability to bind oxygen reversibly (or, more generally, its ability to change oxidation state) — the same underlying property credited for haemoglobin's role, applied to an unfamiliar transport context.

Sodium ions — co-transport

❓ Explain how the sodium–potassium pump enables the co-transport of glucose into an epithelial cell, even though co-transport is not itself a form of active transport.
The sodium–potassium pump actively transports sodium ions out of the epithelial cell, using ATP, maintaining a low intracellular sodium concentration and so a sodium concentration gradient. Sodium ions then diffuse back into the cell through a co-transport protein, down this gradient; as they do so, they carry glucose into the cell against its own concentration gradient. The pump step uses ATP directly (active transport); the co-transport step itself is facilitated diffusion, powered indirectly by the gradient the pump created.
❓ State the number of sodium and potassium ions moved, and the direction of movement, in one cycle of the sodium–potassium pump.
Three sodium ions are pumped out of the cell for every two potassium ions pumped into the cell, both moved against their own concentration gradients, using ATP.
❓ Explain how sodium ions contribute to the reabsorption of water in the kidney.
Sodium ions are actively transported into the blood/medulla, lowering the water potential of the surrounding fluid; water then moves into this region by osmosis, down its own water potential gradient, resulting in water reabsorption.

Phosphate ions — DNA & ATP

❓ Explain the role of phosphate ions in the structure of DNA.
Phosphate ions join adjacent nucleotides together through phosphodiester bonds, forming the sugar-phosphate backbone that runs along the outside of each DNA (or RNA) strand.
❓ Explain what happens when the terminal phosphate bond of ATP is hydrolysed, and why "energy is released" is preferred to "energy is produced."
Hydrolysis of the terminal phosphate bond of ATP produces ADP and inorganic phosphate, releasing energy that was already stored in that bond. "Released" is the credited wording because the energy pre-exists in the bond; "produced" is not accepted, as no new energy is created.
❓ A phospholipid bilayer forms spontaneously in water. Explain the part played by the phosphate ion in this process.
The phosphate group forms the hydrophilic ("water-loving") head of the phospholipid molecule, while the fatty acid tails are hydrophobic. In water, the hydrophilic phosphate heads orientate towards the water on both sides of the membrane, and the hydrophobic tails face inwards away from water — this arrangement is what causes phospholipids to form a bilayer.
