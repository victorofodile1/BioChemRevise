Principles of Homeostasis and Negative Feedback

The single rule that protects the most marks on this spec point: a negative feedback answer is marked as a named, ordered chain — stimulus (deviation) → receptor → coordinator → effector → a response that opposes/reverses the change. Never just write "the body returns to normal" — you must name the receptor, coordinator and effector, and state the specific mechanism for the given context, every single time.

Section 1 · spec content point

🧭 What homeostasis actually is

This is the definition every later 3.6.4 answer is marked against, plus the five reusable components — set point, receptor, coordinator, effector, feedback — that get relabelled onto every worked example from here on. Learn the vocabulary here once; nothing about it changes later.

📖

What the specification says: "Homeostasis in mammals involves physiological control systems that maintain the internal environment within restricted limits."

1
2
3

Your body is a bit like a house with a thermostat. The house isn't always exactly one temperature — it drifts up and down slightly — but the thermostat notices when it drifts too far, and switches the heating or the air conditioning on to pull it back. Homeostasis is your body doing that, for lots of different things at once: how warm you are, how acidic your blood is, how much sugar is in it.

  • Set point = the temperature you set the thermostat to
  • Receptor = the thermostat's sensor, noticing it's got too hot or too cold
  • Coordinator = the thermostat's control unit, deciding what to do about it
  • Effector = the heater or the air conditioning unit, actually doing something

Homeostasis means keeping conditions inside the body steady, even though the world outside keeps changing. "Steady" doesn't mean perfectly fixed — a variable like body temperature or blood glucose is always drifting slightly around a normal value, called the set point, rather than sitting on one exact number forever. Whenever it drifts too far, a receptor notices and passes that information to a coordinator — usually part of the brain or a hormone-producing gland — which tells an effector, a muscle or a gland, to do something that pushes the variable back towards normal. Once it's back, a feedback signal tells the receptor the job's done.

Define homeostasis, in full

  • Homeostasis is the maintenance of a stable internal environment within restricted limits, despite changes in the external environment, achieved by physiological control systems using negative feedback.
  • A physiological variable — core temperature, blood glucose concentration, blood water potential — fluctuates around a set point; it is never held perfectly constant.
  • What's actually being kept stable is the composition of the tissue fluid and blood: its temperature, pH, glucose concentration and water potential.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "constant" instead of "restricted limits"

Never describe the internal environment as "constant" — the precise, credited wording is that it is held within restricted limits, fluctuating around a set point. Equally, never describe a deviation with no named receptor or effector: both must be explicitly stated, never left to be inferred from a described change.

Section 2 · spec content point

🌡️ Why cells need a stable temperature and pH

This section re-applies the enzyme mechanism from earlier in the course to explain exactly why deviating outside restricted limits actually matters at the cellular level — it isn't just an abstract "the body likes to be stable" statement.

📖

What the specification says: "The importance of maintaining a stable core temperature and stable blood pH in relation to enzyme activity."

1
2
3

Enzymes are like tiny locks, and the substances they work on are the keys. If it's too cold, everything moves too slowly for the key to reach the lock very often, so not much happens. If it's too hot, the lock itself starts to warp and buckle out of shape — and once that's happened, the right key doesn't fit any more, no matter how many times it tries. That's why your body fights hard to avoid getting too hot or too cold — not because heat itself is dangerous, but because it wrecks the shape of the locks your cells depend on. Blood acidity does the same kind of damage to the lock's shape if it swings too far either way.

Cells rely on enzymes to catalyse their metabolic reactions, and enzymes only work well within a narrow range of temperature and pH. If it's too cold, molecules simply don't have enough energy to collide with each other often enough, so reactions happen more slowly. If it's too hot, the bonds holding the enzyme's 3D shape together start to break, so the enzyme's shape changes permanently and it stops working — this is denaturation. A change in pH causes the same kind of damage, because it disturbs the charges holding that 3D shape together. That's why the body actively defends a stable temperature and pH, rather than just tolerating whatever the surroundings happen to be.

Explain why core temperature and blood pH must be kept within restricted limits

  • Cells and their enzymes function optimally only within a narrow range of conditions, so metabolic reactions proceed efficiently.
  • Below the optimum: reduced kinetic energy means fewer successful enzyme–substrate collisions, so fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form and reaction rate falls.
  • Above the optimum: hydrogen and ionic bonds break, changing the enzyme's tertiary structure so the active site is no longer complementary to the substrate — the enzyme denatures.
  • A change in pH alters the charges on the amino acid R-groups, breaking ionic and hydrogen bonds and changing the tertiary structure in exactly the same way. Blood pH is normally held close to 7.35–7.45.
📌

Why this matters for homeostasis: deviating outside the tolerable range can denature enzymes or otherwise impair cell function — this is exactly why the body actively defends these conditions using negative feedback, rather than merely tolerating drift.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "the enzyme is killed"

Never write "the enzyme is killed" — enzymes are proteins, not living things, so they are denatured, not killed. And never leave "the active site changes shape" unexplained: state why — because hydrogen and/or ionic bonds break, changing the tertiary structure, so the active site is no longer complementary to the substrate. Neither statement earns credit without that bond-breaking, tertiary-structure mechanism behind it.

Section 3 · spec content point

🍬 Why blood glucose concentration must be stable

Two genuinely distinct, named reasons — this is the bridge into 3.6.4.2's worked insulin/glucagon example, but the theory itself is examined here on its own.

📖

What the specification says: "The importance of maintaining a stable blood glucose concentration in terms of availability of respiratory substrate and of the water potential of blood."

1
2
3

Glucose matters for two totally different reasons. First, it's fuel — your cells burn it to get energy, and your brain barely has any other fuel source, so if it runs low, your brain runs low on power too. Second, glucose is dissolved in your blood, a bit like sugar dissolved in a glass of squash — the more sugar you dissolve in it, the more it "pulls" on water nearby. Too much dissolved glucose pulls water out of your cells and they shrivel up; too little, and water pushes into your cells instead, and they can swell and even burst.

Blood glucose has to stay stable for two separate reasons, and an exam question expects both of them named, not the same idea said twice. Firstly, glucose is the body's main respiratory substrate — cells, especially brain cells, need a steady supply to keep producing ATP. Secondly, because glucose is dissolved in the blood, it affects the blood's water potential: too much glucose lowers the water potential, so water leaves cells by osmosis and they shrink; too little glucose raises the water potential, so water enters cells by osmosis and they may burst.

Give two reasons blood glucose concentration must be kept stable

  • Respiratory substrate. Glucose is the main respiratory substrate for ATP production — this is especially critical for brain cells, which rely almost exclusively on it.
  • Water potential of the blood. Glucose is a solute that affects the water potential of the blood. Too much glucose lowers (makes more negative) the water potential of the blood, so water leaves cells by osmosis and they shrink; too little glucose raises the water potential, so water enters cells by osmosis and they may burst.
▶ 🚩 Trap — two versions of "energy" only scores one mark

A "give two reasons" question checks that both named justifications — respiratory substrate and water potential — are present, not the same idea restated twice. Writing two slightly different sentences that both boil down to "cells need energy" will only ever earn one of the two marks available.

Section 4 · spec content point

🔁 Negative feedback — the full loop

This is the defining principle of the whole spec point — the named, ordered chain examiners actually mark, drawn out as a closed loop that can correct a deviation in either direction, plus how to tell it apart from its much rarer opposite: positive feedback.

📖

What the specification says: "Negative feedback restores systems to their original level."

1
2
3

Negative feedback is like a see-saw pushing itself back level whenever it tilts. Something changes (the see-saw tilts), a sensor notices, and a response pushes back the other way until it's level again — then the pushing stops. Positive feedback is the opposite, and it's rare: instead of pushing back, the response pushes the same way, making the tilt worse and worse, like a microphone squealing louder and louder next to a speaker, until something outside the system stops it completely — the mic gets switched off, or in the body's case, a baby is actually born.

  • Negative feedback = opposes the change, restores normal, keeps things stable
  • Positive feedback = makes the change bigger, moves things further from normal, ends only when some outside event stops it

A negative feedback loop starts when something drifts away from its set point — the stimulus. A receptor notices this and passes the information to a coordinator, which instructs an effector to produce a response. That response opposes the original change, pushing the variable back towards its set point; once the receptor detects that it's back to normal, the response switches off — the control system itself doesn't switch off, it just stops correcting until the next deviation happens. Positive feedback works completely differently: the response makes the original change bigger, not smaller, driving the variable further away rather than back towards normal. Because that's the opposite of stability, positive feedback is rare in homeostasis — it only shows up where the biological goal is to finish an event quickly, like childbirth, rather than to hold a steady state.

Explain what is meant by negative feedback, as a full loop

  • A stimulus (a deviation from the set point) is detected by a receptor, which informs a coordinator — often the brain or an endocrine gland.
  • The coordinator instructs an effector to produce a corrective response that reverses the original change and restores the system to its original level.
  • The receptor then detects the return to normal, and the corrective response is switched off — the system is not shut down completely, it simply stops correcting once the set point is restored, ready to detect the next deviation in either direction.
A single mechanism can only actively correct one direction — this is why two effector pathways exist, one opposing a rise (4a) and one opposing a fall (4b). Both feed back into the same closed loop.
💡

"Negative" means opposing, not switching off. The response is negative relative to the original change — it counteracts the deviation and restores the variable to around its set point. Once the receptor detects the return to normal, the corrective response is switched off; the control system itself keeps running, ready to detect the next deviation in either direction.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "the body returns to normal" is too generic to credit

Never write "the body returns to normal" with no named receptor, coordinator, effector or mechanism — this is marked as too generic, however biologically "true" it sounds. A full-credit answer always names all three roles and states explicitly that the response opposes the deviation.

Distinguishing negative from positive feedback

A change is still detected by a receptor, exactly as in negative feedback — but in positive feedback, the response makes the change bigger, not smaller. The response amplifies the original change, driving the variable further away from the set point, not back towards it.

Point of comparisonNegative feedbackPositive feedback
Effect on the deviationReverses / opposes itAmplifies it
OutcomeReturns to the set pointMoves further from the set point
RoleMaintains stability — homeostasisRapidly completes a process; not homeostatic
How it endsCorrective response switches off once the set point is restoredRuns to completion, stopped by the triggering event itself — not by an opposing effector
Named examplesBlood glucose, temperature, water potential, heart rateOxytocin and childbirth; oestrogen stimulating more LH before ovulation

Worked example — oxytocin and childbirth

  • The baby's head stretches the cervix.
  • Stretch receptors send impulses to the brain, stimulating more oxytocin release from the pituitary gland.
  • Oxytocin makes the uterine (myometrium) muscle contract more strongly.
  • Stronger contractions push the baby harder against the cervix — stretching it even more, so even more oxytocin is released.
  • Contractions escalate until birth occurs — the triggering event ends the loop. Contrast this with negative feedback, which would need a second, opposing mechanism to stop it.

A second named example: rising oestrogen before ovulation stimulates more LH release, rather than inhibiting it — amplifying the change instead of reversing it.

🔍

AO3 skill — interpreting an unfamiliar feedback example in data. The specification explicitly names this as a skill you must be able to apply: unfamiliar contexts (appetite suppression, progesterone inhibiting FSH/LH) require an explicit, stated justification of why the scenario fits negative or positive feedback — "how do these results show…", not "do these results show…". A described trend or correlation alone, without that explicit statement, is not credited. Candidates who have only memorised the standard taught examples (blood glucose, water potential) tend to struggle to transfer the principle to a genuinely new scenario — this is a strong discriminator between grades.

▶ 🚩 Trap — muddling positive and negative feedback in an unfamiliar context

Positive and negative feedback are frequently confused in unfamiliar contexts (e.g. childbirth, blood clotting, appetite suppression, reproductive hormones). Always state explicitly whether the response is pulling the variable back towards the set point (negative) or pushing it further away until some other event intervenes (positive) — never leave this to be inferred from a described trend.

Section 5 · spec content point

⚖️ Two mechanisms, twice the control

The conceptual capstone tying the spec point together: why having a separate negative-feedback mechanism for each direction of departure genuinely gives better control than relying on just one.

📖

What the specification says: "The possession of separate mechanisms involving negative feedback controls departures in different directions from the original state, giving a greater degree of control."

1
2
3

Imagine a car with only an accelerator and no brake. You could speed up whenever you liked, but to slow down you'd just have to take your foot off and coast — slow, and not very precise. A car with both an accelerator and a brake can be actively sped up and actively slowed down, whenever needed. The body does the same thing: it doesn't just have one lever for each variable, it has a pair — one that pushes a level up, and a separate one that pushes it back down — so it can react quickly whichever way things drift.

If a variable only had one control mechanism, that mechanism could actively correct a deviation in just one direction — a departure the other way would have to rely on the response simply switching off, which is slow and imprecise. Having two separate negative-feedback mechanisms, one for a rise and a completely different one for a fall, means both directions are actively corrected rather than one of them just being left to drift back on its own. That's why, for example, blood glucose has both insulin (lowers it) and glucagon (raises it), rather than just one hormone doing both jobs.

Explain why having separate mechanisms gives greater control

  • A single mechanism can only actively correct one direction, relying on passive switching-off for the other.
  • Two mechanisms actively correct both directions — the phrase worth reusing is that this "gives a greater degree of control."
VariableMechanism that RAISES itMechanism that LOWERS it
Blood glucoseGlucagonInsulin
Heart rateSympathetic stimulationParasympathetic stimulation
Body temperatureShivering, vasoconstrictionSweating, vasodilation

When negative feedback fails

Negative feedback can be overwhelmed by an extreme or prolonged deviation, or by a mechanism failure — for example in diabetes, where insulin is not produced, or where receptors fail to respond to it. When that happens, the corrective mechanism can no longer restore the set point, and the internal environment moves outside its restricted limits. This is frequently tested alongside diabetes elsewhere in 3.6.4 as a worked example of negative feedback failing.

📌

Command word to watch for: "Explain why separate mechanisms give greater control" is asking for the causal reasoning above — a single mechanism can only push one way — not just a restatement of the antagonistic-pair examples on their own.

A complete answer must name the receptor, state that it informs a coordinator (brain or endocrine gland), name the effector, and state explicitly that the response opposes the original deviation — a described trend alone, without that explicit "why," is not credited.

Section 7 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

This spec point supplies the general framework almost every essay-style question in 3.6 is secretly built on — treat every later paragraph as "cashing in" the vocabulary named on this page.

▶ Model opening paragraph for an essay on homeostasis or feedback

"Homeostasis is the maintenance of a stable internal environment within restricted limits, not a perfectly constant one, since any physiological variable fluctuates around a set point. Every control system follows the same chain: a receptor detects a deviation and informs a coordinator, which instructs an effector to produce a response that reverses the deviation; once the receptor detects a return to normal, the response is switched off, which is negative feedback. Possessing separate negative-feedback mechanisms for departures in each direction — such as insulin and glucagon, or vasodilation and vasoconstriction — gives a greater degree of control than a single mechanism ever could."

▶ How to close a paragraph that contrasts with positive feedback

"Positive feedback, by contrast, amplifies rather than restores a deviation, so it is rare in maintaining a stable internal environment; instead it is used where the biological goal is to drive a process rapidly to completion, such as during childbirth, or when rising oestrogen stimulates further LH release before ovulation."

Across essay titles on homeostasis, negative feedback, or feedback in biology generally, this spec point supplies the general framework — blood glucose (3.6.4.2), blood water potential (3.6.4.3) and heart rate (3.6.1.3) are each just a worked application of the same receptor–coordinator–effector–negative-feedback chain, operating on different timescales. Explicitly name the receptor, coordinator and effector every time you apply it, and state that the response opposes (rather than merely follows) the deviation.

Rule of thumb: any question anywhere in 3.6.4 or 3.6.1.3 about the nervous or hormonal control of an internal variable is, underneath, testing the same receptor → coordinator → effector → negative-feedback chain named on this page.

🧠

Memory hook — negative opposes, positive amplifies. Negative feedback: stimulus (deviation) → receptor → coordinator (brain or endocrine gland) → effector (muscle or gland) → a response that opposes the change and restores the set point — and because separate mechanisms exist for each direction (insulin/glucagon; sympathetic/parasympathetic), control works both above and below the set point. Positive feedback instead amplifies the original change, driving the variable further away until an external event (birth, ovulation) ends the loop — which is exactly why it is rare in maintaining a stable internal environment.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · What homeostasis actually is

  • Homeostasis = stable internal environment within restricted limits — never "constant." A variable fluctuates around a set point.
  • Five components, always: set point, receptor, coordinator, effector, negative feedback.
  • Always name the receptor and the effector explicitly — never just describe the change.

2 · Stable temperature & pH — enzymes

  • Too cold → less kinetic energy → fewer enzyme–substrate collisions → slower reactions.
  • Too hot / wrong pH → hydrogen/ionic bonds break → tertiary structure changes → active site no longer complementary → denatured (never "killed").
  • pH changes charges on R-groups — same bond-breaking mechanism as heat.

3 · Why blood glucose must be stable

  • Respiratory substrate — main fuel for ATP production, especially for brain cells.
  • Water potential — too much glucose lowers water potential (cells shrink); too little raises it (cells may burst).
  • Both reasons must be named — two versions of "energy" only scores one mark.

4 · Negative feedback — the full loop

  • Stimulus (deviation) → receptor → coordinator → effector → response that opposes the change → set point restored → response switches off.
  • Never write "the body returns to normal" with no named chain.
  • Positive feedback amplifies instead — runs to completion, stopped by the event itself (childbirth; oestrogen → more LH).
  • In unfamiliar data, you must state explicitly why an example is negative or positive — never leave it implied.

5 · Two mechanisms, twice the control

  • One mechanism only actively corrects one direction; two mechanisms actively correct both — "gives a greater degree of control."
  • Insulin/glucagon; sympathetic/parasympathetic; vasoconstriction+shivering/vasodilation+sweating.
  • Negative feedback can be overwhelmed by extreme/prolonged deviation, or mechanism failure (e.g. diabetes).

Negative opposes, positive amplifies. Stimulus → receptor → coordinator → effector → opposing response → set point restored → switches off. Two mechanisms per variable, one for each direction. Positive feedback amplifies until an outside event ends it — rare, because it can't maintain stability.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

What homeostasis actually is

❓ Define homeostasis, and explain why the word "constant" would not be an accurate way to describe it.
Homeostasis is the maintenance of a stable internal environment within restricted limits, despite changes in the external environment. "Constant" is inaccurate because a physiological variable fluctuates around a set point rather than staying at one fixed value; it is the range of fluctuation that is restricted, not the value itself.
❓ A student answers a homeostasis question by describing "a change happening, and then the body sorting it out." Identify what is missing from this answer and rewrite it using the correct terminology.
Missing: a named receptor, coordinator and effector. Rewritten: a stimulus (deviation from the set point) is detected by a receptor, which informs a coordinator; the coordinator instructs an effector to bring about a corrective response.
❓ Name the five components of a generalised control system and state the role of each.
Set point — the normal level the variable fluctuates around. Receptor — detects the deviation and informs the coordinator. Coordinator — a region of the brain or an endocrine gland that processes the information and sends instructions on. Effector — a muscle or gland that brings about the corrective response. Negative feedback — informs the receptor that the level has returned to normal, so the response can be switched off.

Stable temperature & pH — enzymes

❓ Explain, in terms of enzyme activity, why a core temperature significantly below 37 °C would slow down metabolic reactions.
A lower temperature means enzyme and substrate molecules have less kinetic energy, so they collide less frequently; fewer successful collisions mean fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form per unit time, so the rate of enzyme-catalysed, and therefore metabolic, reactions falls.
❓ A student writes "high fever denatures enzymes because the heat kills them." Rewrite this using credited biological wording.
Excess heat breaks hydrogen and ionic bonds within the enzyme's tertiary structure, changing its shape so the active site is no longer complementary to the substrate; this is denaturation, not "killing," since enzymes are non-living proteins.
❓ Explain why a fall in blood pH can have the same effect on enzyme activity as a rise in temperature.
A change in pH alters the charges on the R-groups of amino acids within the enzyme, breaking ionic and hydrogen bonds; this changes the tertiary structure so the active site is no longer complementary to the substrate, reducing the rate of enzyme–substrate complex formation in the same way that excessive heat does.

Why blood glucose must be stable

❓ Give two reasons why it is important that blood glucose concentration is maintained within restricted limits.
1. Glucose is the main respiratory substrate used to produce ATP, and brain cells in particular rely almost exclusively on a continuous glucose supply. 2. Glucose is a solute that affects the water potential of the blood; too high a concentration lowers blood water potential, causing cells to lose water by osmosis and shrink, while too low a concentration raises it, causing cells to gain water and potentially burst.
❓ Explain why a sustained rise in blood glucose concentration could cause cells elsewhere in the body to shrink.
A higher glucose concentration lowers (makes more negative) the water potential of the blood plasma; water then moves out of surrounding cells, into the blood plasma, by osmosis, down the water potential gradient, causing those cells to lose water and shrink.
❓ A candidate answers "blood glucose must stay stable so cells have enough energy," and gains only one mark out of two. Explain why.
Only the respiratory-substrate reason has been given; the second, separately credited reason — the effect of glucose concentration on the water potential of the blood — has not been mentioned, so the second mark is not awarded.

Negative feedback — the full loop

❓ Explain what is meant by negative feedback, using the general receptor–coordinator–effector chain.
A stimulus is a deviation from the set point; this is detected by a receptor, which informs a coordinator (often the brain or an endocrine gland); the coordinator instructs an effector to produce a response that opposes the original deviation, restoring the variable to its set point; the receptor then detects this return to normal and the corrective response is switched off.
❓ A graph shows that as a hormone X increases, the release of hormone Y also increases, and this continues until a specific event occurs. Explain how this shows positive, rather than negative, feedback.
This is positive feedback because a rise in one variable causes a further rise in the other, rather than a corrective fall — the change is amplified rather than opposed, and the variable moves further from any set point rather than back towards it, continuing until the triggering event ends the loop.
❓ Explain why, during childbirth, the increasing strength of uterine contractions is described as positive feedback rather than negative feedback.
Stretching of the cervix stimulates the release of more oxytocin, which causes stronger uterine contractions; these stronger contractions stretch the cervix even further, amplifying the original stimulus rather than opposing it, so the change escalates rather than being reversed — this is the defining feature of positive, not negative, feedback.

Two mechanisms, twice the control

❓ Explain why the possession of separate mechanisms for raising and lowering blood glucose concentration gives a greater degree of control than a single mechanism would.
A single mechanism could only actively correct a deviation in one direction, relying on the response simply switching off to allow the opposite departure to be corrected, which is slow and imprecise; having two separate negative-feedback mechanisms — glucagon to raise blood glucose and insulin to lower it — means departures in both directions are actively corrected, giving a greater degree of control.
❓ Suggest why a person with type 1 diabetes cannot maintain blood glucose concentration within restricted limits using negative feedback alone.
In type 1 diabetes, insulin is not produced, so the mechanism that actively lowers blood glucose concentration is unavailable; the negative feedback system is therefore overwhelmed, since only one of the two normal corrective mechanisms is functioning, and blood glucose concentration can rise outside its restricted limits.
❓ Give one further named example, other than blood glucose, of a variable controlled by two separate negative-feedback mechanisms, and identify both.
Heart rate: sympathetic stimulation raises heart rate; parasympathetic (vagus nerve) stimulation lowers it. (Body temperature is equally acceptable: shivering and vasoconstriction raise it; sweating and vasodilation lower it.)
