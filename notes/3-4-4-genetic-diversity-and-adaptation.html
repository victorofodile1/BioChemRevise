Genetic Diversity and Adaptation

How genetic diversity gives natural selection something to act on, the fixed five-step chain that answers almost every selection question on this spec point, the two named types of selection — directional and stabilising — and how an adaptation gets classified as anatomical, physiological or behavioural.

⚠️

The single rule that protects the most marks on this spec point: antibiotics do not cause resistance — they select for it. The resistance allele already exists in some bacteria, by chance mutation, before the antibiotic is ever present; exposure is the selection pressure that kills non-resistant bacteria while resistant ones survive and reproduce. Never describe bacteria as "becoming immune" or "developing immunity", and never write "gene" where the answer needs allele.

Section 1 · spec content point

🧬 Genetic diversity — the definition

One precise sentence everything else on this page depends on. Get the exact wording right and every later section becomes easier to write accurately.

📖

What the specification says: "Genetic diversity as the number of different alleles of genes in a population."

1
2
3

Imagine a whole class of students, and everyone's hair colour is a different "version" — blonde, brown, black, ginger, and every shade between. Genetic diversity is just a way of counting how many different versions of a feature exist across a whole group, not in one single person. A group with lots of different versions has more genetic diversity than a group where almost everyone looks the same.

  • A "version" of a gene = an allele
  • A whole group of the same species living together = a population
  • Genetic diversity is a fact about the whole group, never about one individual on its own

Genetic diversity is the number of different alleles of genes present across a population. It is not the number of genes — most members of a species share the same set of genes — it is how many different versions (alleles) of those genes exist once you look at every individual in the population together. A population where every individual carries identical alleles has zero genetic diversity for that gene; a population with many different alleles in circulation has high genetic diversity.

TermWhat it actually means
GeneA section of DNA coding for a particular characteristic — shared by the whole species
AlleleA different version of that gene, arising from mutation
Genetic diversityHow many different alleles exist across a population, not within one organism

Genetic diversity is defined precisely as the number of different alleles of genes in a population. Two words in that definition carry almost all of the marks: it is measured in alleles, not genes — writing "the number of genes in a population" describes something completely different and scores nothing. And it is a property of a population — a group of interbreeding organisms of the same species living in the same area — never of a single individual, who can only ever carry at most two alleles of any one gene.

Correct wordingWhy the alternative is wrong
"…the number of different alleles…""Genes" describes the shared blueprint every member of the species has — it doesn't vary, so it can't measure diversity
"…of genes in a population."A single organism has at most two alleles per gene, so "diversity in an individual" is not a meaningful measurement

Where this diversity actually comes from is covered in 3.4.3, not here: random mutation is the source of brand-new alleles, and meiosis (independent assortment and crossing over) plus random fertilisation then shuffle existing alleles into new combinations across a population. 3.4.4 picks up the story from there — this spec point is about what happens to that diversity once it already exists, not about how it arises.

📌

Quick self-test: if a question asks you to define genetic diversity, a complete answer needs both halves of the sentence — the "alleles, not genes" half, and the "population, not individual" half — written out, not just implied.

Section 2 · spec content point

🔑 Why genetic diversity enables natural selection

A short but conceptually loaded clause — this is the logical bridge between "diversity exists" and everything the rest of this spec point builds on top of it.

📖

What the specification says: "Genetic diversity is a factor enabling natural selection to occur."

1
2
3

Picture a game of "who survives the storm" where every single competitor is identical in every way — same strength, same speed, same everything. Nobody would have any advantage, so the storm couldn't pick a winner based on anything. Now picture the same game where everyone is slightly different: some faster, some stronger, some better at climbing. Now the storm actually can favour some competitors over others. Genetic diversity is what makes that difference possible — no differences, no way for nature to "choose."

Natural selection works by favouring individuals whose characteristics give them a survival or reproductive edge over others in the same population. That only makes sense if individuals actually differ from one another in the first place. If every individual carried exactly the same alleles, there would be nothing for the environment to select between — everyone would have an identical chance of surviving and reproducing, and the population's allele frequencies could never shift in response to a changing environment. Genetic diversity is therefore not just a nice-to-have background fact; it's the raw material selection needs in order to have any effect at all.

Natural selection can only act on variation that already exists within a population. If every individual carried identical alleles for a given gene, there would be no difference at all in survival or reproductive success between individuals for selection to act on — the population would be entirely unable to adapt if its environment changed, because there would be no alternative allele available for selection to favour.

  • Genetic diversity supplies the variation that selection pressures act on.
  • Without different alleles already present, there is nothing for a selection pressure to differentiate between.
  • A population with higher genetic diversity is more likely to contain at least some individuals carrying an allele that happens to suit a new or changing environment — so it is more likely to survive that change as a population.
  • A population with very low genetic diversity (e.g. after a population bottleneck) is at greater risk, because there may be no allele in circulation capable of coping with a new selection pressure.
🔗

The logical chain worth stating explicitly in an answer: mutation and meiosis/random fertilisation (3.4.3) create genetic diversity → genetic diversity means individuals differ in the alleles they carry → selection pressures can therefore favour some alleles over others → over time, allele frequencies in the population change. Skipping straight from "mutation happens" to "selection happens" without stating this middle link is a common way marks are lost on "why is diversity important" questions.

▶ 🚩 Trap — treating diversity and selection as unrelated facts

A weak answer states the definition of genetic diversity and the five-step selection chain (Section 3) as two separate, unconnected facts. A strong answer explicitly says why one enables the other: selection requires differential survival/reproduction, and differential survival/reproduction requires individuals to actually differ — which is exactly what genetic diversity provides.

Section 3 · spec content point

🔗 The principles of natural selection — the five-step chain

This fixed sequence answers almost every natural-selection question on the paper, whatever organism or allele the question describes. Learn the order, not just the ideas — and never let the chain stop before the final step.

📖

What the specification says: "The principles of natural selection in the evolution of populations. Random mutation can result in new alleles of a gene. Many mutations are harmful but, in certain environments, the new allele of a gene might benefit its possessor, leading to increased reproductive success. The advantageous allele is inherited by members of the next generation. As a result, over many generations, the new allele increases in frequency in the population."

1
2
3

Think of it as a five-domino chain that always falls in the same order. First, a random accident creates a brand-new "version" of a trait — nobody chose it, it just happened. Second, that new version happens to be helpful in the world the animal lives in right now. Third, because it's helpful, that animal is more likely to survive long enough to have babies. Fourth, its babies inherit the same helpful version. Fifth, because this keeps happening generation after generation, that version becomes more and more common in the whole population over a long time.

The chain always runs in the same five-step order: a random mutation creates a new allele → in a particular environment, that allele happens to give its owner an advantage → individuals with the advantage are more likely to survive and reproduce → they pass the allele on to their offspring → over many generations, that allele becomes more common in the population. The step people forget most is the last one — the chain has to finish by saying the allele's frequency rises over many generations, not just that individuals "survive and reproduce."

  1. Mutation creates a new allele, at random
  2. In the current environment, the allele happens to be advantageous
  3. Individuals carrying it are more likely to survive and reproduce
  4. They pass the allele on to their offspring
  5. Over many generations, the allele's frequency in the population increases
  1. Step 1 — the allele arose by random mutation.
  2. Step 2 — individuals with the allele have a selective advantage in the stated environment.
  3. Step 3 — so they are more likely to survive and reproduce.
  4. Step 4 — they pass on the allele to the next generation.
  5. Step 5 — so the frequency of the allele increases in the population, over many generations.

Accept the converse throughout for a disadvantageous allele (advantage becomes disadvantage, frequency rises becomes frequency falls). Accept "proportion" or "percentage" for frequency, and accept "pass on the allele/characteristic" in place of "reproduce."

▶ 🚩 The mark students drop most often

A chain that stops at "individuals survive and reproduce" without going on to state that the allele's frequency rises over many generations is the single most common way the final mark is lost on this question. Always finish the chain — the outcome is a population-level change in allele frequency, not just an individual-level survival event.

▶ 🚩 Three wording traps that cost marks

Writing "gene" where the answer needs "allele" is usually penalised once per answer — selection acts on a specific allele of a gene, not the gene as a whole. Never suggest that the environment causes the mutation — mutation is random and happens independently of any selection pressure; the environment only determines whether a mutation that has already occurred turns out to be advantageous. And don't write "the number of alleles decreases/increases" — it is the frequency or proportion of the allele that changes, not a simple count.

Why a favourable allele takes many generations to spread

  • Initially, only one or a small few individuals carry the new, favourable mutation.
  • Because it gives an advantage, individuals with the allele tend to have more offspring than individuals without it.
  • It still takes many generations of this repeated advantage before the allele becomes the most common version of that gene in the population.

Correcting the misconceptions this spec point tests directly

  • Mutations are spontaneous and random — they are not produced deliberately or "on demand."
  • The environment can only affect the rate at which mutations occur (e.g. via a mutagen) — it never decides which mutation happens or in what direction.
  • Different species do not normally interbreed, or do not produce fertile offspring, so a mutation or allele arising in one species cannot be passed directly to another species.
▶ 🚩 No teleological language

Organisms do not "try", "want", "need" or "develop" a characteristic because it would be useful to them. Adaptations arise by chance mutation first, and are only then selected for if they happen to help — never the other way round. Any phrasing that implies an organism is aiming for an outcome will be marked down.

Section 4 · spec content point

📊 Directional and stabilising selection

Only two types of selection are named on this spec point. A huge range of invented terms show up nationally for this — none of them score. Learn exactly these two names and what each does to a population's distribution.

📖

What the specification says: "Directional selection, exemplified by antibiotic resistance in bacteria, and stabilising selection, exemplified by human birth weights."

1
2
3

Imagine a big line-up of people arranged from "shortest" to "tallest." Directional selection is like a rule that says "everyone below a certain height is out" — over time, the whole line-up shuffles taller and taller. Stabilising selection is like a rule that says "the very shortest AND the very tallest are both out, only the middle heights stay" — the line-up doesn't shift anywhere, it just gets squeezed into a narrower band around the middle.

  • Directional = the whole group shifts one way (like antibiotic resistance in bacteria)
  • Stabilising = the middle survives, both extremes are removed (like human birth weight)

In directional selection, individuals at one extreme of a range of phenotypes are favoured — usually because the environment has changed — so the population's average value shifts steadily in that direction over generations. Antibiotic resistance in bacteria is the standard example: once antibiotics are present, only resistant bacteria survive, so resistance becomes more and more common. In stabilising selection, individuals close to the average are favoured and both extremes are selected against, so the average stays exactly where it was but the spread of values narrows. Human birth weight is the standard example: babies that are too small or too large both have poorer outcomes, so most babies cluster around a middle weight.

TypeWhat is selectedEffect on the population
DirectionalIndividuals with a phenotype at one extreme of the range are favoured — usually when the environment changesThe modal (most common) value shifts in one direction over generations
StabilisingIndividuals around the mean are favoured; both extremes are selected against — when the environment is stableThe mean stays the same and the range narrows — genetic diversity is reduced

Antibiotic resistance in bacteria is the compulsory directional example; human birth weight is the compulsory stabilising one — very low birth weight means poorer survival, very high birth weight means birth complications, so the mode sits safely in the middle.

▶ 🚩 Only two names score — everything else is worth nothing

Naming the type of selection as "planned", "artificial", "systematic", "selective advantageous", "differential" or "individual" selection earns no credit at all — only directional and stabilising are named on this spec point. Disruptive selection, which favours both extremes over the mean, is not part of 3.4.4 and is not creditable here.

before exposure after exposure (survivors) removed by antibiotic
Before exposure the population spans a wide range of resistance. Antibiotic exposure kills low-resistance bacteria, so the whole curve's mean shifts toward higher resistance — genetic diversity falls, but the surviving population is harder to treat.

Explaining antibiotic resistance in full

  • Some bacteria already carry alleles for resistance, before any antibiotic is present.
  • Exposure to the antibiotic is the selection pressure — non-resistant bacteria die, while resistant bacteria survive and reproduce.
  • Because antibiotics are used heavily in some settings (e.g. hospitals) and because patients can have weakened immune systems, the resistance allele can end up at a high frequency in the bacterial population.
📌

Why bacteria evolve resistance so fast: a very short generation time, very large population sizes, and horizontal gene transfer of resistance plasmids via conjugation mean an advantageous allele can spread within days — and even move between different bacterial species, not just down through generations of the same one.

▶ 🚩 The immunity trap

Any reference to bacteria becoming "immune" is penalised. Antibiotics never cause the production of the resistance allele — the allele already exists in the population before exposure; antibiotics simply select for the bacteria that already carry it.

Applying the chain to an unfamiliar scenario — worked examples

The hardest version of this spec point is not reciting the principles in the abstract — it's re-deriving the same five-step chain for a specific, unfamiliar organism and allele. Always name the organism and the allele before walking the chain.

🐟

Fish predation and spot colour (directional selection): fish with more spots are more likely to be spotted and eaten by a predator, so alleles for more spots are less likely to be passed on — meaning the frequency of the "more spots" allele decreases in the population over generations. (Accept the converse throughout for the opposite scenario.)

🧫

A molecular-level enzyme allele (e.g. alcohol tolerance in flies): a different allele produces a protein with a different primary structure, so the enzyme folds into a different tertiary structure — including the shape of its active site — meaning enzyme-substrate complexes are more likely to form. Flies carrying this allele have a selective advantage in the presence of alcohol, so they are more likely to reproduce, pass the allele on, and its frequency increases in the population.

Stabilising selection — human birth weight

before selection after selection selected against, both extremes
Both extremes are selected against — very low birth weight (poorer survival) and very high birth weight (birth complications). The mean stays exactly where it was, but the after-selection curve is visibly narrower than the before curve — reduced genetic diversity, not a shift.
🧠

Directional vs stabilising — the one-line contrast to memorise: directional selection shifts the mean because one extreme is killed off; stabilising selection narrows the spread because both extremes are killed off and the middle survives. Selection never invents new alleles — mutation does that, randomly and independently of the selection pressure; selection only changes how common an allele that already exists becomes.

Section 5 · spec content point

🦎 Adaptation — anatomical, physiological, behavioural

Natural selection's visible outcome. Every named adaptation on this course fits into exactly one of three categories — questions often want the category named as well as explained.

📖

What the specification says: "Natural selection results in species that are better adapted to their environment. These adaptations may be anatomical, physiological or behavioural."

1
2
3

An adaptation is just any feature that helps a living thing survive and thrive where it lives. There are three kinds: a body-part kind (like thick fur — you can see or touch it), a chemistry kind (like a special blood that grabs oxygen really well — happening inside, invisible), and a behaviour kind (like migrating somewhere warmer — something the animal actually does).

An adaptation is a feature that makes a species better suited to its environment, and every adaptation falls into one of three categories. Anatomical adaptations are structural — a physical feature you could point to, like thick fur or a long loop of Henle. Physiological adaptations are about internal chemistry or processes — like a blood pigment with an especially high affinity for oxygen. Behavioural adaptations are about what the organism actually does — like migrating, hibernating, or a courtship display. The same organism usually has adaptations of more than one type at once.

TypeWhat it meansExamples
AnatomicalA structural featureThick fur; long loop of Henle; large surface area of alveoli; needle-shaped leaves
PhysiologicalA feature of internal chemistry or processesHaemoglobin with a high affinity for oxygen; production of a venom or antibiotic; ability to enter torpor
BehaviouralThe way the organism actsCourtship displays; migration; hibernation; taxis and kinesis
▶ 🚩 Read the command word carefully

Questions often ask you to name the type as well as explain it. "Explain why this adaptation evolved" needs both the classification (anatomical/physiological/behavioural) and the full selective-advantage chain from Section 3 — naming the classification alone is not a complete answer.

🔁

The reasoning to always attach: whatever the adaptation, the underlying explanation is still the five-step chain — the anatomical, physiological or behavioural feature arose by mutation, gave a selective advantage in that species' environment, and so increased in frequency over many generations through survival and reproduction. Classifying the type is the easy first step; explaining why it evolved is where the marks for a fuller answer are found.

Section 6 · spec content point · required practical

🧪 Required practical 6 — aseptic technique & antimicrobial substances

The practical method, its controls and variables, why a logarithmic scale suits this data, and how to evaluate a selection/adaptation investigation critically.

📖

What the specification says: "Required practical 6: Use of aseptic techniques to investigate the effect of antimicrobial substances on microbial growth." (Maths skill 2.5, tied to this practical: students could be asked to use a logarithmic scale when dealing with data relating to large numbers of bacteria in a culture.)

1
2
3

"Aseptic technique" just means working extra cleanly so no unwanted germs sneak into your experiment and spoil the results. You clean everything, work near a flame that pulls stray germs upward and away, and only open lids a tiny amount, quickly. To measure how well a substance kills germs, you look at the clear circle it leaves behind on the dish — a bigger clear circle means it worked better.

This practical investigates how well a substance stops microbial growth, using strict aseptic technique so that only the substance being tested — not stray contamination — affects the result. Discs soaked in different concentrations of the substance are placed on an agar plate already coated with bacteria; wherever the substance works, no bacteria grow, leaving a clear zone around the disc. A bigger clear zone means the substance was more effective at that concentration. Because bacterial numbers grow exponentially, a logarithmic scale is used so that both very small and very large numbers can be shown clearly on the same graph.

The aseptic technique, step by step

  1. Sterilise the equipment and the agar (e.g. by autoclaving), and disinfect the working surface before starting.
  2. Work near a Bunsen flame — the upward draught it creates carries airborne microorganisms away from the working area.
  3. Flame the neck of the culture bottle and sterilise the inoculating loop until red hot, then allow it to cool before it touches anything.
  4. Lift the Petri dish lid at an angle, only as far as necessary, and never put the lid down on the bench.
  5. Tape the lid to the dish, but do not seal it completely — fully sealed, anaerobic conditions could allow harmful anaerobic bacteria to grow undetected.
  6. Incubate the plate upside down, at no more than 25 °C, so that human pathogens are unlikely to grow and condensation does not drip down onto the colonies.
  7. Dispose of used plates by autoclaving them, then wash hands thoroughly.

Measuring and comparing the effect

  • Measure the diameter, radius or area of the clear (inhibition) zone around each disc — area is calculated as πr².
  • A common detail worth including: determine the mean diameter across repeats, or use graph paper to find the clear-zone area directly.
  • Control: a disc soaked in sterile water, or in the solvent only, at every concentration tested — this shows that any inhibition seen is due to the antimicrobial substance itself, not the disc or the solvent it was dissolved in.
  • Variables to control: the same volume of liquid on each disc; the same disc size; the same bacterial species and starting concentration; the same depth of agar; and the same temperature and incubation time throughout.
📌

A design criticism worth knowing: "no measurements at intermediate values" — if only two concentrations were tested, more concentrations should be used across the range to properly characterise the relationship between concentration and effect.

Using a logarithmic scale

Bacterial numbers rise exponentially, so a linear axis cannot show both the small early values and the very large later ones clearly on the same graph. A logarithmic scale compresses the range so that all the data fit, and — usefully — an exponential growth phase appears as a straight line on log axes, making the growth rate easy to read off directly.

▶ 🚩 Reading a log axis correctly

Each division on a logarithmic axis represents a ×10 increase, not an equal addition. Always convert values back out of logs before directly comparing two readings, or before calculating a difference or a rate from the graph.

Evaluating selection/adaptation data

Typical valid criticisms of this kind of investigation include: the data come from only one species; no statistical test was performed to confirm the difference is significant; the size of the container may itself affect growth; the organisms tested were all the same age, whereas a natural population is not; only mean mass or clear-zone size was measured, with no measure of number, yield or reproductive success; and the manipulation used in the lab is unlikely to be exactly replicated under natural conditions.

▶ 🚩 Balance is required

An evaluation is capped on how many "against" points can be credited — a genuinely balanced answer makes points supporting the conclusion as well as points against it, rather than a one-sided list of criticisms.

Section 7 · exam technique

🎯 Common mistakes & precise wording

The underlying biology on this spec point — mutation, selective advantage, allele frequency — is simple in outline, but almost every mark depends on exact causal wording. These are the slips that come up again and again nationally.

▶ Where this point sits in difficulty terms

Memorisation load is moderate — one definition, one five-step chain, two named types, three adaptation categories, and one required practical. But application depth and wording-precision risk are both high: a wide range of invented terms for the two named selection types are seen nationally, none of which score, and the antibiotic-resistance misconception (that antibiotics cause mutation, or that bacteria "become immune") is a persistent, recurring error. Students also frequently describe the consequence of directional selection rather than the actual selection pressure driving it.

Section 8 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

3.4.4 supplies the central "variation is the raw material" paragraph for several 25-mark essay titles, and works as a standalone 6-mark model paragraph on antibiotic resistance.

▶ Essay paragraph — "the importance of natural selection to living organisms"

Genetic diversity — the number of different alleles of genes in a population — is a prerequisite for natural selection, since selection can only act on variation that already exists between individuals. Random mutation continually generates new alleles; where a new allele happens to be advantageous in the current environment, individuals carrying it are more likely to survive and reproduce, so they pass it on to their offspring, and its frequency in the population rises over many generations. Because different environments favour different phenotypes, this same process produces two contrasting patterns: directional selection, which shifts a population's characteristics toward one extreme when the environment changes, and stabilising selection, which keeps a population's characteristics clustered around a stable optimum when the environment does not. Over long timescales, the accumulated result is a population increasingly well adapted — anatomically, physiologically or behaviourally — to the environment it lives in.

▶ 6-mark model paragraph — antibiotic resistance and difficulty of treatment

Some bacteria already carry an allele, often located on a plasmid, that confers resistance to a particular antibiotic, before that antibiotic is ever used. When a population of bacteria is exposed to the antibiotic, it acts as a strong selection pressure: non-resistant bacteria are killed, while resistant bacteria survive. Surviving bacteria reproduce by binary fission — not mitosis — passing the resistance allele on to their offspring, which is vertical transmission. Because resistant bacteria continue reproducing while non-resistant ones are eliminated, the frequency of the resistance allele rises with every generation. Resistance can also spread horizontally: since the allele is often plasmid-borne, one bacterium can transfer a copy directly to another during conjugation, via a hollow pilus — even between different bacterial species — which is part of why resistance can become widespread unusually quickly.

🚩

Two traps worth flagging in any answer like the one above: do not describe bacteria as reproducing by "mitosis" — they reproduce by binary fission, and this is penalised if linked to reproduction or vertical transmission — and do not describe resistance using the language of "immunity."

Essay technique: use 3.4.4 as the "variation is the raw material" paragraph — open by stating that genetic diversity (from 3.4.3's mutation, meiosis and random fertilisation) is a prerequisite for selection, walk the five-step chain once in full, contrast directional and stabilising selection using the two named examples, then close by linking to adaptation (anatomical, physiological, behavioural) as the visible, long-term outcome of allele-frequency change.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Genetic diversity — the definition

  • Genetic diversity = the number of different alleles of genes in a population — never "genes," never "in an individual."
  • Comes from mutation (new alleles) and meiosis/random fertilisation (new combinations) — that's 3.4.3, not this spec point.

2 · Why diversity enables selection

  • No variation → nothing for selection to act on → no differential survival/reproduction possible.
  • Higher diversity = a population is more likely to contain an allele suited to a changed environment.

3 · The five-step selection chain

  • Mutation → advantage (in that environment) → survive & reproduce → pass on allele → frequency increases over many generations.
  • Never skip the final step — the most commonly dropped mark.
  • "Gene" instead of "allele" is usually penalised once; never say the environment causes mutation; it's frequency that changes, not allele count.

4 · Directional & stabilising selection

  • Directional = one extreme favoured, mean shifts. Example: antibiotic resistance in bacteria.
  • Stabilising = both extremes selected against, mean stays, spread narrows. Example: human birth weight.
  • Only these two names score — "planned/artificial/systematic" etc. score nothing.
  • Antibiotics select for pre-existing resistance — never "cause" it or make bacteria "immune."

5 · Adaptation — three types

  • Anatomical = structural (thick fur, loop of Henle). Physiological = internal chemistry (high-affinity haemoglobin). Behavioural = what it does (migration, hibernation).
  • "Explain why it evolved" needs the classification and the five-step chain, not just the name.

6 · RP6 — aseptic technique

  • Sterilise/autoclave, flame near Bunsen, flame the loop, lift lid at an angle, tape (don't seal) the lid, incubate upside down at ≤25 °C.
  • Control = disc with sterile water/solvent only, at every concentration. Measure clear-zone diameter/area (πr²).
  • Log scale needed because bacterial growth is exponential — each division is ×10, not +constant.
  • Evaluation must be balanced — the mark scheme caps "against" points.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Genetic diversity — the definition

❓ Define genetic diversity.
The number of different alleles of genes in a population.
❓ A student writes "genetic diversity is how many genes an organism has." Identify and correct the two errors.
First error: it should be the number of different alleles, not genes — genes are shared across the species and don't vary in number in this sense. Second error: genetic diversity is a property of a population, not of a single organism, which can carry at most two alleles of any one gene.
❓ State two sources of genetic diversity in a population that are covered elsewhere on the specification.
Random mutation, which creates new alleles; and meiosis together with random fertilisation, which create new combinations of existing alleles.

Why diversity enables selection

❓ Explain why genetic diversity is necessary for natural selection to occur.
Natural selection can only act on variation that already exists between individuals. If every individual in a population carried identical alleles, there would be no difference in survival or reproductive success for selection to act on, so the population could not adapt if the environment changed.
❓ Suggest why a population with very low genetic diversity might be at greater risk following a sudden environmental change.
With very few different alleles present, the population may not contain any individuals carrying an allele suited to the new conditions, so there is nothing for selection to favour and the population may be unable to adapt, increasing the risk of the whole population declining or becoming extinct.

The five-step selection chain

❓ Explain how natural selection can lead to a change in a population over time.
A new allele arises by random mutation. In a particular environment, individuals carrying that allele have a selective advantage, so they are more likely to survive and reproduce. They pass the allele on to their offspring, so, over many generations, the frequency of the allele increases in the population.
❓ A student's answer states "the allele is useful, so bacteria develop it to survive." Identify what is wrong with this statement and rewrite it correctly.
The statement wrongly implies the mutation happens because it is useful (teleological language) — mutation is spontaneous and random, and occurs independently of whether it will turn out to be useful. Corrected: a random mutation produces the allele; only afterwards, if the allele happens to be advantageous in that environment, are individuals carrying it more likely to survive, reproduce and pass it on.
❓ Explain why it typically takes many generations for a favourable allele to become common in a population.
Initially only one or a few individuals carry the favourable mutation. Because they have a selective advantage, individuals with the allele tend to produce more offspring than individuals without it, but it still takes repeated generations of this advantage before the allele becomes the most common version of that gene in the population.

Directional & stabilising selection

❓ Name and describe the type of selection acting when a population of insects, previously varying widely in wing length, comes to have almost entirely long wings after a change in habitat.
Directional selection. Individuals with a phenotype at one extreme of the range (long wings) are favoured, usually because the environment has changed, so the modal wing length shifts in that direction over generations.
❓ Explain, using the example of human birth weight, why stabilising selection reduces genetic diversity without changing the population mean.
Both very low birth weight (associated with poorer survival) and very high birth weight (associated with birth complications) are selected against, while babies around the average weight are favoured. Because both extremes are removed, the mean stays the same, but the range of birth weights narrows, reducing genetic diversity.
❓ Explain fully why a population of bacteria exposed to an antibiotic becomes increasingly resistant over successive generations.
Some bacteria already carry alleles for resistance before the antibiotic is present. Exposure to the antibiotic is the selection pressure: non-resistant bacteria die, while resistant bacteria survive and reproduce. Because antibiotics are used repeatedly and resistant bacteria continue reproducing, the frequency of the resistance allele in the bacterial population increases.

Adaptation — three types

❓ A species of desert mammal has a very long loop of Henle. Name the type of adaptation this is, and explain why it evolved.
An anatomical adaptation — it is a structural feature. A random mutation gave rise to individuals with a longer loop of Henle; in a dry environment this gave a selective advantage by allowing more efficient water reabsorption, so these individuals were more likely to survive and reproduce, passing the allele on, so its frequency increased in the population over many generations.
❓ Classify each of the following as anatomical, physiological or behavioural: (a) haemoglobin with an unusually high oxygen affinity, (b) hibernation, (c) needle-shaped leaves.
(a) Physiological — a feature of internal chemistry. (b) Behavioural — a pattern of what the organism does. (c) Anatomical — a structural feature.

RP6 — aseptic technique

❓ Explain why a Petri dish lid should be taped but not sealed completely during this practical.
Taping keeps the lid from being knocked off accidentally, but sealing it completely would create anaerobic conditions inside the dish, which could allow harmful anaerobic bacteria to grow undetected.
❓ A student tests only two concentrations of an antimicrobial substance. Suggest one improvement to the experimental design.
Test a wider range of concentrations, including intermediate values, so the relationship between concentration and the size of the zone of inhibition can be established more precisely.
❓ Explain why a logarithmic scale is often used when graphing bacterial population growth over time.
Bacterial numbers increase exponentially, so a linear scale cannot clearly show both the small early values and the very large later values on the same axis. A logarithmic scale compresses the range so all the data can be shown, and an exponential growth phase appears as a straight line.
