Synaptic Transmission

How an electrical impulse in one neurone is converted into a chemical message, sent across a microscopic gap, and turned back into an electrical signal in the next cell — plus how weak signals can add up to a strong one, how a synapse can be told to stay quiet, and how to reason through a never-before-seen drug scenario using the same underlying chain.

⚠️

The one habit that protects the most marks on this topic: never let calcium disappear from your answer. "The presynaptic membrane depolarises, so the vesicles release acetylcholine" jumps straight past the single most important link in the whole chain — voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels opening, and Ca²⁺ actually entering the knob, is what triggers everything downstream. As a general rule: whenever a channel "opens" in your answer, don't stop there — say which ion moves, in which direction, and what effect that has on the membrane. And always name acetylcholinesterase specifically; "an enzyme" is never precise enough for full credit.

Section 1 · spec content point

🔬 The detailed structure of a synapse and of a neuromuscular junction

Labelling questions on this content point all reward the same habit: give a structure's name and its job in the same sentence. These eight structures are worth being able to spot instantly on any diagram or electron micrograph.

📖

What the specification says: "The detailed structure of a synapse and of a neuromuscular junction."

1
2
3

Two neurones almost touch, but not quite — there's a tiny gap between them called a synapse. One side keeps little bags of a chemical messenger ready to fire. When a nerve signal arrives, the bags burst open and squirt the chemical across the gap. The other side has special catching-spots shaped to fit only that one chemical, and once it lands there, it wakes the next cell up. Afterwards, a tiny clean-up crew chops the chemical into pieces so the catching-spots are free again for next time.

  • Sending side = the presynaptic side
  • Receiving side = the postsynaptic side
  • The chemical messenger = acetylcholine (ACh, for short)
  • The gap itself = the synaptic cleft
  • The clean-up crew = acetylcholinesterase

A synapse is the junction between two neurones, or between a motor neurone and a muscle fibre. The presynaptic knob — the bulb at the very tip of the sending neurone — stores acetylcholine in synaptic vesicles and is packed with mitochondria to power the whole release process. When an impulse arrives, calcium channels in the presynaptic membrane open, so the vesicles fuse with the membrane and dump their acetylcholine into the synaptic cleft, a gap roughly 20 nanometres wide. Acetylcholine crosses that gap and locks onto receptor proteins on the postsynaptic membrane — proteins the presynaptic side simply doesn't have. Finally, an enzyme called acetylcholinesterase breaks the acetylcholine apart so the signal switches off rather than firing forever.

▶ 🚩 Trap — vague location words

Saying a neurotransmitter "binds to the membrane" is too vague to earn credit — always specify the postsynaptic membrane, since the presynaptic membrane carries no receptors to bind to at all. In the same way, "an enzyme breaks down the neurotransmitter" should always become "acetylcholinesterase hydrolyses acetylcholine" — naming the enzyme, and ideally both of its products, is what separates a strong answer from a vague one.

Section 2 · spec content point

🔗 The sequence of events across a cholinergic synapse

This is one unbroken causal chain, not eight separate facts sitting side by side — an arriving impulse sets off a domino run that ends with the messenger chemical being cleared away again. Learn it start to finish, then practise pulling out whichever single link a shorter question is actually asking about.

📖

What the specification says: "The sequence of events involved in transmission across a cholinergic synapse in sufficient detail to explain: unidirectionality; temporal and spatial summation; inhibition by inhibitory synapses." (This section builds the sequence itself — Section 3 covers unidirectionality, summation and inhibition, which come from the same specification content point.)

1
2
3

Picture a chain of dominoes. A signal arrives and it knocks open a tiny door for calcium to rush through. The calcium sets off little bags of chemical, which burst open. The chemical floats across a gap and clicks into a matching socket on the other side. That click opens a different door on the other side, letting sodium rush in. If enough sodium rushes in, a brand-new signal fires off in the next cell. Last of all, a clean-up helper snips the chemical apart so everything resets, ready to happen all over again.

Here's the chain without every fine detail: an impulse depolarises the presynaptic membrane, which opens voltage-gated calcium channels. Calcium diffuses in and makes vesicles fuse with the membrane, releasing acetylcholine by exocytosis. Acetylcholine diffuses across the cleft and binds receptors on the postsynaptic membrane, opening sodium channels. Sodium diffuses in, depolarising the postsynaptic membrane — and if this reaches threshold, a new impulse fires. Finally, acetylcholinesterase breaks the acetylcholine down into choline and ethanoic acid, both of which get reabsorbed and rebuilt into more acetylcholine.

  1. Impulse arrives, presynaptic membrane depolarises
  2. Calcium channels open, Ca²⁺ diffuses in
  3. Vesicles fuse with the membrane, ACh is released
  4. ACh diffuses across the cleft
  5. ACh binds receptors, sodium channels open
  6. Na⁺ diffuses in, postsynaptic membrane depolarises
  7. If threshold is reached, a new impulse fires
  8. Acetylcholinesterase breaks ACh down; the synapse resets

▶ 🚩 The single costliest omission

Marks disappear most often through incomplete chains — writing "sodium channels open" and stopping there, instead of continuing to say that sodium ions then diffuse in and that this depolarises the postsynaptic membrane. Calcium suffers the same problem: "calcium enters," full stop, with no mention of voltage-gated channels or facilitated diffusion, is a much weaker answer than "calcium ions enter the synaptic knob by facilitated diffusion through voltage-gated channels." Every "channel opens" step needs its ion movement and its consequence stated, every single time.

▶ Precise phrasing worth reusing

Three phrases are worth learning almost word for word, because they sit at exactly the level of detail a full-mark answer needs: "calcium ions enter the synaptic knob" (not just "calcium"), "acetylcholine attaches to receptors on the postsynaptic membrane" (not "the membrane" generally), and "sodium ions enter, causing depolarisation" (always name the ion). A single reference to "ions" can sometimes cover both the channel-opening and the entry step together, but no reference to ions at all is a weak answer regardless of everything else written.

Why a synapse needs mitochondria, and why it's slower than a nerve impulse

Mitochondria provide the ATP needed to move vesicles into position, actively transport ions, and resynthesise or reabsorb the neurotransmitter afterwards. Synaptic transmission is also measurably slower than an impulse travelling along an axon, because diffusion across the cleft itself takes time — this "synaptic delay" is one reason a measured reaction time always comes out longer than nerve-conduction time alone would predict.

Section 3 · spec content point

🎚️ Unidirectionality, summation & inhibition

Three ideas the sequence above needs to be able to explain, not just describe: why the signal only ever travels forward, how several weak signals can add up to a strong one, and how a synapse can actively resist firing altogether.

📖

What the specification says: "The sequence of events involved in transmission across a cholinergic synapse in sufficient detail to explain: unidirectionality; temporal and spatial summation; inhibition by inhibitory synapses." (Same specification content point as Section 2 — this half covers the three named ideas.)

1
2
3

The signal can only ever go one way, like a one-way street, because only one side can throw the chemical and only the other side can catch it. If one small nudge isn't enough to wake the next cell up, several small nudges can team up — either from a few different neighbours firing at once, or the same neighbour firing again and again, really fast — until together they're big enough. But some synapses do the opposite: they actively tell the next cell to stay quiet, making it harder, not easier, to fire.

Transmission is unidirectional because only the presynaptic membrane can release neurotransmitter, and only the postsynaptic membrane carries receptors for it. Spatial summation is several different neurones each releasing a small amount onto the same neurone at the same time; temporal summation is one neurone releasing repeatedly in quick succession — both add small signals together until they cross the threshold needed to fire. An inhibitory synapse does the opposite: it opens chloride and/or potassium channels instead of sodium channels, making the inside of the membrane more negative (hyperpolarised), so it becomes harder, not easier, to trigger a response.

Why transmission only ever travels one way

  • Only the presynaptic membrane contains synaptic vesicles and is capable of releasing neurotransmitter.
  • Only the postsynaptic membrane carries the complementary receptor proteins that neurotransmitter can actually bind to.
  • Neither side is equipped to do the other's job — so there is simply no mechanism for the impulse to travel backwards.

Temporal and spatial summation

TypeMechanism
Spatial summationSeveral different presynaptic neurones each deliver a sub-threshold amount onto the same postsynaptic neurone, at the same time.
Temporal summationA single presynaptic neurone fires repeatedly, in rapid succession.

In both cases the individual EPSPs combine, and if the resulting depolarisation reaches threshold, an action potential fires — a single release usually would not be enough on its own.

Inhibition by an inhibitory synapse

At an inhibitory synapse, neurotransmitter binding opens ligand-gated chloride channels (Cl⁻ diffuses in) and/or potassium channels (K⁺ diffuses out) — never sodium channels. This pushes the postsynaptic membrane more negative — hyperpolarisation, producing an IPSP — which moves it further from threshold, so a stronger simultaneous excitatory input is then needed before an action potential can fire.

▶ 🚩 Trap — inhibitory synapses get under-explained

Inhibitory synapses are less familiar than excitatory ones, and answers show it: many correctly write "hyperpolarisation" and then stop, without going on to explain that more sodium-ion entry, or a stronger simultaneous stimulus, would then be needed to reach threshold as a result. Never treat excitatory and inhibitory synapses as the same channel simply behaving differently — they use genuinely different channels (Na⁺ versus Cl⁻/K⁺).

⚖️

It's always the sum that decides. Whether the postsynaptic neurone actually fires depends on the total of every EPSP and IPSP arriving at the trigger zone at that moment — spatial and temporal summation of excitatory input, minus whatever inhibitory input is happening at the same time. Reach threshold, and an action potential fires; let inhibition dominate, and it doesn't, even with some excitatory input present.

Section 4 · spec content point

⚖️ Comparing a cholinergic synapse with a neuromuscular junction

Same chemistry, different ending. A "compare" answer needs both sides addressed on every point — describing only one side, however accurately, will not score fully.

📖

What the specification says: "A comparison of transmission across a cholinergic synapse and across a neuromuscular junction."

1
2
3

A synapse and a neuromuscular junction are like cousins — same chemical messenger, same calcium-triggered release, same clean-up crew. But a normal synapse hands the baton to another neurone, and that neurone might not always run with it. A neuromuscular junction always hands the baton to a muscle, and the muscle almost always runs with it — contracting pretty much every single time.

Both use acetylcholine, released by calcium-triggered exocytosis, and both use acetylcholinesterase to switch the signal off again. The difference is what happens next. At a cholinergic synapse the postsynaptic cell is another neurone, the effect can be excitatory or inhibitory, and whether the impulse carries on depends on summation. At a neuromuscular junction the postsynaptic cell is always a muscle fibre, the effect is always excitatory, and because there are so many more receptors clustered there, the outcome — muscle contraction — happens virtually every time.

Point of comparisonCholinergic synapse (neurone → neurone)Neuromuscular junction (motor neurone → muscle)
Postsynaptic cellAnother neuroneA skeletal muscle fibre (the sarcolemma)
Excitatory or inhibitory?Can be either excitatory (EPSP) or inhibitory (IPSP)Always excitatory only
Outcome once threshold is reachedA fresh action potential in the postsynaptic neuroneMuscle contraction, via the sliding-filament mechanism
Does the signal reliably continue?Not guaranteed — depends on summationVirtually guaranteed — many more receptors, and far more neurotransmitter released, per impulse
🤝

Shared similarities (worth stating for any "compare" question too): both use acetylcholine as the neurotransmitter, both release it by calcium-triggered exocytosis, and both terminate the signal using acetylcholinesterase.

▶ 🚩 Trap — a one-sided "compare" answer

A compare answer that only describes one side's structure or behaviour, without the paired similarity and difference points above, will never score fully — the command word "compare" always wants both sides addressed, not a description of just one.

Section 5 · spec content point

💊 Predicting the effects of drugs on a synapse

No drug names or real mechanisms ever need to be memorised for this content point — every mark comes from applying the sequence from Section 2 to whatever unfamiliar scenario the question describes, then reasoning forward to the consequence.

📖

What the specification says: "Students should be able to use information provided to predict and explain the effects of specific drugs on a synapse. (Recall of the names and mode of action of individual drugs will not be required.)"

1
2
3

Imagine three ways to mess with the messenger system: jam the receiving socket so the chemical can't dock at all; disable the clean-up crew so the chemical never gets cleared away; or sneak in extra chemical, or a copycat, so more signal gets through than usual. Each one changes how much "wake up" message actually reaches the next cell.

Every drug question here is really the same puzzle wearing a different costume: find the one link in the sequence the description is pointing at, then say what happens next. Block the receptor, and less or no acetylcholine can bind, so less or no depolarisation happens. Stop acetylcholinesterase working, and acetylcholine isn't cleared away, so depolarisation carries on for longer than normal. Add extra acetylcholine, or something that mimics it, and depolarisation becomes bigger or lasts longer. You're never expected to know the drug's actual name — everything needed is in the description given.

Drug acts by…Predicted effect, reasoned from the sequence
Blocking the receptorLess/no neurotransmitter can bind → less/no depolarisation → fewer/no postsynaptic action potentials
Stopping acetylcholinesterase workingLess ACh is broken down, so it stays bound to receptors for longer → continued/prolonged depolarisation (at a neuromuscular junction, the muscle fibre cannot relax)
Mimicking or boosting neurotransmitter releaseBinds receptors itself, or triggers release of more neurotransmitter → greater/prolonged depolarisation
🧭

How to tackle any drug scenario: pin down precisely where in the sequence the description places the drug's action, name the specific receptor type and ion movement affected, then reason forward to the consequence. A general "it affects the synapse" answer scores nothing, no matter how well the underlying mechanism is otherwise known.

▶ 🧪 Try one yourself — click to reveal the reasoning

Scenario: a newly discovered compound permanently occupies the acetylcholine receptor site on the postsynaptic membrane, but does not open the associated ion channel. Predict its effect.

Reasoning: the receptor is occupied but never activated, so acetylcholine released from the presynaptic knob can no longer bind and open the ligand-gated sodium channels. Less/no sodium enters, so less/no depolarisation of the postsynaptic membrane occurs, meaning fewer or no postsynaptic action potentials are produced — the same outcome as any other receptor-blocking scenario, reasoned purely from where in the chain the compound intervenes.

🔎

This same reasoning connects directly to reflex-arc reaction-time questions elsewhere in the course: a measured reaction time is longer than nerve-conduction time alone would predict, because diffusion across the cleft, receptor binding and channel opening all take additional, measurable time.

Section 6 · exam technique

🎯 Precision & common mistakes

This spec point is demanding not because the biology itself is conceptually hard, but because so much of the credit rides on exact wording. These are the slips that come up again and again.

Vague versionWhat to say instead
"Calcium enters"Voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open, and Ca²⁺ diffuses in (facilitated diffusion)
"Sodium channels open" as a complete answer…and Na⁺ diffuses in, depolarising the postsynaptic membrane
"An enzyme" breaks down the neurotransmitterAcetylcholinesterase hydrolyses it — name the enzyme and, where possible, its two products

"Affects the synapse" for a drug questionName the specific receptor type and ion movement, even though the drug itself is never named

▶ Why this content point feels harder than it looks

This is entirely second-year-level material, built from a detailed named-structure list, a full multi-step causal chain, a four-point comparison against the neuromuscular junction, and three distinct drug-scenario response types — a heavy memorisation load paired with a genuine application skill, since the drug-prediction questions have to be reasoned from unfamiliar information rather than recalled from memory. Almost all of the actual difficulty comes down to reproducing the sequence accurately and picking the right sub-set of it for the marks on offer.

Section 7 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

This content makes a strong supporting paragraph inside a longer essay-style answer, on more than one theme — the trick is closing each paragraph with a sentence that ties the mechanism back to whatever the essay title is actually about, rather than repeating a generic summary.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around enzyme function

An enzyme's job isn't always to build something — sometimes it has to switch a process off. Acetylcholinesterase, sitting in the synaptic cleft, hydrolyses acetylcholine into choline and ethanoic acid almost as soon as it arrives, which keeps the postsynaptic response brief and lets the membrane repolarise ready for the next impulse. Without that enzyme working, receptor binding would continue unchecked and the postsynaptic membrane would stay permanently depolarised — so this is a case where an enzyme's importance lies in ending a biological signal, not starting one.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around molecular specificity

Complementary shape is what makes synaptic transmission specific rather than random. Acetylcholine only binds to receptor proteins on the postsynaptic membrane because their shape matches its own precisely, and that binding is what opens ligand-gated sodium channels. Because only the postsynaptic membrane carries those complementary receptors — the presynaptic membrane has none — a synapse can only ever pass its signal in one direction. In other words, a single well-matched shape both makes the chemical signal specific and explains directly why it is unidirectional.

Whatever the essay theme, treat this content as the "mechanism" paragraph: state the process precisely — naming acetylcholinesterase, or the complementary receptor, depending on what the title is actually asking about — then finish with one sentence linking it explicitly back to that theme.

Section 8 · recap

🧷 Recap, links & final checklist

Where synaptic transmission reappears later

  • The reflex arc — synaptic delay is a named reason why a measured reaction time exceeds simple nerve-conduction time alone.
  • Nerve impulses — this content picks up exactly where that topic leaves off, reusing the same depolarisation and voltage-gated-channel vocabulary, now triggering Ca²⁺ entry rather than Na⁺/K⁺ alone.
  • Skeletal muscles — the neuromuscular junction reuses acetylcholine, calcium-triggered exocytosis and acetylcholinesterase, but is always excitatory and triggers the sliding-filament mechanism instead of a further action potential.
  • Homeostasis and hormonal control — contrasts a fast, short-range, wired synaptic signal against a slower, long-range, blood-borne hormonal one, both ultimately converting a chemical signal into a cellular response through complementary receptors.
🧠

Memory hook — no calcium, no fusion. Every step from depolarisation through to acetylcholine release hinges on that one link: Ca²⁺ channels open, Ca²⁺ floods in, vesicles fuse. After that: diffuse, bind, Na⁺ in, EPSP — then acetylcholinesterase breaks it down, and it gets rebuilt using ATP. A single release is rarely enough on its own — it takes summation, either spatially from several neurones at once, or temporally from one neurone firing repeatedly, to reach threshold. Any inhibitory input (Cl⁻ in, K⁺ out, never Na⁺) pulls the balance the other way. Whether the postsynaptic neurone actually fires is always a balance, never a single isolated event.

Self-check before moving on

  • I can name and give the function of every structure in a synapse (knob, mitochondria, vesicles, Ca²⁺ channels, cleft, postsynaptic membrane, receptors, acetylcholinesterase).
  • I can reproduce the full transmission sequence from memory, without skipping the calcium step, and without truncating any "channel opens" step before stating the ion movement and its consequence.
  • I can explain unidirectionality (vesicles only presynaptic, receptors only postsynaptic), and explain spatial summation (different neurones, same time) distinctly from temporal summation (one neurone, repeated firing).

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Synapse & NMJ structure

  • Know all eight by name + function: knob, mitochondria, vesicles, voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels, synaptic cleft (~20 nm), postsynaptic membrane, receptor proteins, acetylcholinesterase (AChE).
  • AChE hydrolyses ACh into choline + ethanoic acid — name the enzyme, never just "an enzyme."
  • Only the postsynaptic membrane carries receptors — the presynaptic membrane never does.

2 · The signal-crossing sequence

  • AP arrives → Ca²⁺ channels open → Ca²⁺ in (facilitated diffusion) → vesicles fuse (exocytosis) → ACh diffuses across the cleft → binds postsynaptic receptors → ligand-gated Na⁺ channels open → Na⁺ in → depolarisation (EPSP) → if threshold reached, AP fires → AChE hydrolyses ACh → products reabsorbed and reused with ATP.
  • Never skip the calcium step — it's the single most heavily credited link.
  • Every "channel opens" statement needs the ion movement and the consequence, not just "opens."

3 · Direction, summation & inhibition

  • One-way: only the presynaptic membrane releases neurotransmitter; only the postsynaptic membrane has receptors.
  • Spatial summation = different neurones, same postsynaptic cell, same time. Temporal = one neurone, repeated firing.
  • Inhibitory = ligand-gated Cl⁻ in and/or K⁺ out → hyperpolarised → IPSP → moves away from threshold. Never sodium.

4 · Cholinergic synapse vs NMJ

  • Shared: acetylcholine, calcium-triggered exocytosis, acetylcholinesterase.
  • NMJ: postsynaptic cell = muscle fibre (sarcolemma), always excitatory, virtually always triggers contraction (far more receptors).
  • Cholinergic synapse: postsynaptic cell = neurone, excitatory OR inhibitory, may or may not continue (depends on summation).

5 · Reasoning about unfamiliar drugs

  • Blocks receptor → less/no ACh binds → less/no depolarisation → fewer/no postsynaptic APs.
  • Stops AChE working → ACh not broken down → prolonged depolarisation (muscle can't relax).
  • Mimics/boosts neurotransmitter → greater/prolonged depolarisation.
  • Always name the specific receptor + ion — never just "it affects the synapse."
🧠

No calcium, no fusion. Ca²⁺ in → vesicles fuse → ACh diffuses, binds, Na⁺ in, EPSP → AChE breaks it down, rebuilt. One release rarely reaches threshold alone — it takes summation. Inhibition always pulls the other way.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise, dense wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Synapse & NMJ structure

❓ An electron micrograph shows a synapse with several structures labelled A–E. Describe the function of each, given: A = presynaptic knob, B = a vesicle, C = a channel protein in the presynaptic membrane, D = the gap between the two membranes, E = an enzyme in that gap.
A, presynaptic knob — contains mitochondria and vesicles needed for transmission. B, synaptic vesicle — stores acetylcholine and fuses with the presynaptic membrane by exocytosis to release it. C, voltage-gated calcium channel — opens when the presynaptic membrane depolarises, allowing calcium ion entry. D, synaptic cleft — the ~20 nm gap across which acetylcholine diffuses down its concentration gradient. E, acetylcholinesterase — hydrolyses acetylcholine into choline and ethanoic acid.
❓ A student's answer states that "the chemical attaches to the membrane and this makes the next cell fire." Rewrite this using precise, full-credit terminology.
Acetylcholine binds to specific, complementary receptor proteins on the postsynaptic membrane, opening ligand-gated sodium channels; sodium ions then diffuse in, depolarising the postsynaptic membrane, and if threshold is reached, an action potential is produced.
❓ State two structural features that are found only on the postsynaptic side of a synapse, and explain the significance of each.
Complementary receptor proteins — these bind acetylcholine and open ligand-gated sodium channels, and their absence from the presynaptic membrane is why transmission is unidirectional. A high density of receptors at a neuromuscular junction specifically — this is why transmission there is virtually always successful.

The signal-crossing sequence

❓ Describe the sequence of events, from the arrival of an action potential, that leads to the release of acetylcholine from a presynaptic knob.
An action potential arrives at the presynaptic knob and depolarises the presynaptic membrane; this opens voltage-gated calcium channels; calcium ions diffuse into the knob by facilitated diffusion, down their electrochemical gradient; the resulting rise in calcium ion concentration causes synaptic vesicles to move to and fuse with the presynaptic membrane by exocytosis, releasing acetylcholine into the synaptic cleft.
❓ Explain how the binding of acetylcholine to the postsynaptic membrane can lead to a new action potential.
Acetylcholine diffuses across the synaptic cleft and binds to specific, complementary receptor proteins on the postsynaptic membrane; this opens ligand-gated sodium channels; sodium ions diffuse in, depolarising the postsynaptic membrane; if this depolarisation reaches threshold, an action potential is produced.
❓ Reaction-time data shows that measured reaction time is consistently longer than nerve-conduction time alone would predict. Use your knowledge of synaptic transmission to explain why.
Diffusion of calcium ions into the presynaptic knob, exocytosis of vesicles, diffusion of acetylcholine across the synaptic cleft, and its binding to receptors all take measurable additional time before the postsynaptic membrane can depolarise; this synaptic delay adds to the time taken for the impulse to travel along the axon itself.

Direction, summation & inhibition

❓ Explain why transmission across a synapse can only ever occur in one direction.
Only the presynaptic membrane contains synaptic vesicles and is able to release acetylcholine; only the postsynaptic membrane carries the complementary receptor proteins that acetylcholine can bind to; the impulse therefore has no route by which to travel backwards across the synapse.
❓ Distinguish between temporal and spatial summation, and explain what they achieve.
Temporal summation is the repeated release of neurotransmitter from a single presynaptic neurone in quick succession; spatial summation is the simultaneous release of neurotransmitter from several different presynaptic neurones onto the same postsynaptic neurone. In both cases, individual excitatory postsynaptic potentials combine, and if the combined depolarisation reaches threshold, an action potential is produced.
❓ A newly identified toxin is found to open ligand-gated potassium channels on a postsynaptic membrane. Predict and explain the effect this would have on the likelihood of an action potential being produced.
Potassium ions diffuse out of the postsynaptic neurone, making the inside more negative — hyperpolarisation, producing an inhibitory postsynaptic potential; this moves the membrane potential further from threshold, so a stronger simultaneous excitatory input would be required for an action potential to be produced.

Cholinergic synapse vs NMJ

❓ Compare transmission at a cholinergic synapse with transmission at a neuromuscular junction.
At both, acetylcholine is released by calcium-triggered exocytosis and broken down by acetylcholinesterase. At a cholinergic synapse the postsynaptic cell is another neurone and transmission may be excitatory or inhibitory, so the impulse may or may not continue onward; at a neuromuscular junction the postsynaptic cell is a muscle fibre (the sarcolemma), transmission is always excitatory, and because far more receptors are present, an action potential in the muscle — and so contraction — is produced virtually every time.
❓ Give one similarity and one difference between a cholinergic synapse and a neuromuscular junction.
Similarity: both release acetylcholine by calcium-triggered exocytosis (or: both use acetylcholinesterase to terminate the signal). Difference: a cholinergic synapse can be inhibitory as well as excitatory, whereas a neuromuscular junction is always excitatory.

Reasoning about unfamiliar drugs

❓ A drug is found to bind permanently to acetylcholine receptors on the postsynaptic membrane without opening the associated ion channel. Predict and explain the effect on synaptic transmission.
Less or no acetylcholine can bind to the now-occupied receptors; this results in less or no opening of ligand-gated sodium channels; so less or no depolarisation of the postsynaptic membrane occurs, meaning fewer or no postsynaptic action potentials are produced.
❓ A drug prevents the breakdown of acetylcholine in the synaptic cleft. Predict the effect on transmission at a neuromuscular junction.
Less acetylcholine is broken down, so it remains bound to receptors for longer; sodium ions continue to enter, so depolarisation continues; the muscle fibre therefore remains stimulated and cannot relax.
❓ A drug causes additional acetylcholine to be released from the presynaptic knob for every action potential that arrives. Predict the effect on the postsynaptic membrane.
More acetylcholine binds to receptors on the postsynaptic membrane; more ligand-gated sodium channels open; more sodium ions diffuse in, producing a greater or more prolonged depolarisation of the postsynaptic membrane.
