Structure of Prokaryotic Cells and of Viruses

A short spec point with a high confusion rate: two structure checklists — features in every bacterium versus features in only some — and three look-alike word pairs (capsid/capsule, DNA/RNA, attachment protein/receptor) between them account for most of the marks lost here.

⚠️

The single rule that costs the most marks on this spec point: keep two separate lists and never let them merge. Every bacterium has a murein cell wall, 70S ribosomes and free circular DNA — a capsule, plasmid or flagellum is only found in some bacteria. Asked for structures found in "every" bacterium, naming a some-bacteria-only structure scores nothing. Keep capsule (bacterial) and capsid (viral) just as strictly separate — they are two unrelated structures on two unrelated organisms.

Section 1 · spec content point

🔬 Features found in every prokaryotic cell

Prokaryotic cells are much smaller than eukaryotic cells, and this section is the fixed list of four structural differences that apply to every single bacterium, with no exceptions.

📖

What the specification says: "Prokaryotic cells are much smaller than eukaryotic cells. They also differ from eukaryotic cells in having: • cytoplasm that lacks membrane-bound organelles • smaller ribosomes • no nucleus; instead they have a single circular DNA molecule that is free in the cytoplasm and is not associated with proteins • a cell wall that contains murein, a glycoprotein."

1
2
3

Imagine a bacterium as a tiny factory with just one giant open room — no separate offices, no boardroom, nothing walled off inside. Every bacterium, no exceptions, is built this way: it has an outer wall made from a special material found nowhere else, its tiny machines (which build proteins) are a bit smaller than the ones in your own cells, and instead of a boxed-off control room, its instruction book floats loose in a ring, right there in the open room with everything else.

  • Outer wall material = murein (not the same stuff as a plant's wall)
  • Tiny protein-building machines = ribosomes, just a smaller size
  • Instruction book = a single loop of DNA, floating free, no control room around it
  • One giant open room = cytoplasm with nothing walled off inside

A prokaryotic cell is far smaller overall than a eukaryotic cell, and differs from it in four fixed ways. Its cell wall is built from murein, a glycoprotein — not the cellulose of a plant wall or the chitin of a fungal wall. Its ribosomes are smaller than eukaryotic ribosomes. It has no nucleus: instead of DNA boxed inside a nuclear envelope, it has a single circular DNA molecule sitting free in the cytoplasm, without any proteins attached to it. And its cytoplasm has no membrane-bound organelles at all — no mitochondria, no Golgi body, no endoplasmic reticulum.

FeatureWhat it means here
Cell wallMurein, a glycoprotein — never cellulose or chitin
RibosomesSmaller than in a eukaryotic cell
No nucleusDNA is a free circular loop, not boxed in an envelope
CytoplasmNo membrane-bound organelles of any kind

The four universal structures — spec-exact detail

StructureSpec-exact description
Cell wallContains murein, a glycoprotein — a synonym sometimes accepted is peptidoglycan, but that's not the specification's own word. Not cellulose (found in plant/algal walls) and not chitin (fungal walls).
RibosomesSmaller than eukaryotic ribosomes — commonly labelled 70S, against the eukaryotic 80S. The numeric labels aren't spec-required wording, but are frequently credited and worth knowing precisely.
No nucleus / circular DNAA single circular DNA molecule, free in the cytoplasm, not associated with proteins (i.e. no histones). This loop is called the nucleoid, and it is still double-stranded, like all DNA.
CytoplasmLacks membrane-bound organelles — no mitochondria, no Golgi body, no endoplasmic reticulum, and (as above) no nucleus.

The size difference, and reading it from a scale bar

Prokaryotic cells are much smaller than eukaryotic cells — typically 0.1–10 µm, against roughly 10–100 µm for a eukaryotic cell. In a comparative-micrograph question this is often earned directly from a given magnification or scale bar rather than recalled from memory: actual size = image size ÷ magnification, converting units carefully (mm → µm is ×1000).

Comparing a eukaryotic and a prokaryotic cell — every sentence must be two-sided

Extended comparison questions, often built from real electron micrographs, need each point phrased as one explicit, comparative sentence naming both cell types — not two separate lists written side by side.

▶ 🚩 Trap — a one-sided comparison scores nothing

A statement like "the prokaryote has no nucleus," with nothing said about the eukaryotic cell, scores zero for that point in a compare-type question. Both cell types must be named in the same sentence: "cell A has a nucleus, whereas cell B has DNA free in the cytoplasm instead."

▶ Why murein and small ribosomes matter beyond this page

The murein cell wall and the 70S ribosome are exactly the two structures antibiotics are able to target that a human cell doesn't have — which is why antibiotics work against bacteria but are completely ineffective against a human's own cells. Keep this link in mind: it's a very common way this content resurfaces in questions about drug action later in the course.

Section 2 · spec content point

🧫 Features found in only some prokaryotes

Beyond the four universal structures, many — but not all — bacteria carry extra structures. Confusing this list with Section 1's is the single most common way marks are lost on this spec point.

📖

What the specification says: "In addition, many prokaryotic cells have: • one or more plasmids • a capsule surrounding the cell • one or more flagella." The specification also notes, immediately after these two structural lists: "Details of these structural differences are not required." — in other words, you need to be able to name and place each structure correctly, but not explain the finer mechanics of how each one is built.

1
2
3

Some bacteria come with extra gear on top of the basic four — like some people carry a backpack and others don't. A raincoat around the outside (a capsule), some spare tool bags scattered inside (plasmids), and a tail for swimming (a flagellum). None of this extra gear is compulsory — plenty of bacteria get by perfectly well without any of it.

On top of the four universal structures, some bacteria — not all — also carry one or more plasmids (small circular loops of DNA, separate from the main DNA loop), a capsule surrounding the outside of the cell wall, and one or more flagella for movement. Because these are "some bacteria only" structures, they must never be given as the answer if a question specifically asks for structures found in every bacterium.

StructureWhat it means here
Plasmid(s)Small circular DNA loop(s), separate from the main DNA
CapsuleA layer surrounding the cell wall — not the same as a viral capsid
FlagellumOnly one needs to be named for full credit

The three optional structures — spec-exact detail

StructureSpec-exact description
Plasmid(s)One or more small circular DNA loops, separate from the main nucleoid — found in some bacteria only.
CapsuleSurrounds the outside of the cell wall. Not universal — protects against desiccation and phagocytosis where present, but many bacteria have no capsule at all.
Flagellum/flagellaOne or more; if a question asks for a "some bacteria only" structure, naming just one flagellum is enough for full marks.
Solid outline = present in every bacterium (cell wall, membrane, cytoplasm, circular DNA, 70S ribosomes). Dashed structures = capsule, plasmid(s) and flagellum, found in some bacteria only.

The other trap: capsid vs capsule

A bacterial capsule and a viral capsid are two completely different structures on two completely different types of organism — a slimy outer coat around some bacteria, versus the protein shell of every virus (covered in Section 3). Writing "capsid" for the bacterial capsule, or "capsule" for the viral protein coat, is treated as a wrong answer either way round, not a spelling slip.

▶ 🚩 The costliest national error on this spec point

If a question specifically asks for structures found in every bacterium, naming plasmid, capsule or flagellum scores nothing — these belong to the "some bacteria only" list. Sort every structure into its correct list before writing an answer down. If the question only says "found in a bacterium," without the word "every" or "all," then any structure from either list is a safe answer.

Section 3 · spec content point

🦠 Virus structure & replication

Every virus particle needs exactly three structures — nothing else is required by this spec point — plus a precise understanding of what "acellular and non-living" actually means, and how those three structures let a virus copy itself.

📖

What the specification says: "Viruses are acellular and non-living. The structure of virus particles to include genetic material, capsid and attachment protein."

1
2
3

Think of a virus as a locked delivery box with no engine of its own. Inside is the blueprint (its genetic material), the box itself is built from lots of identical little panels (the capsid), and sticking out of the box are a few tiny keys (attachment proteins) that only fit one specific lock (a receptor on one type of cell). The box can't move itself, can't power itself, and can't make copies of itself — it has to trick a living cell into doing all of that for it.

  • Blueprint = genetic material (DNA or RNA — never assume it's always one or the other)
  • Box built from panels = capsid, made of repeating pieces
  • Tiny keys = attachment protein(s), fitting one specific lock on a host cell

A virus particle is built from just three parts: its genetic material (DNA or RNA — different viruses have either, never assume all viruses share one type), a capsid — a protein coat built from repeating subunits — and one or more attachment proteins that stick out of the capsid and bind a matching receptor on the surface of one specific type of host cell. A virus is described as acellular because it has no cytoplasm, no organelles and no cell-surface membrane of its own — it isn't built like a cell at all — and as non-living because it cannot respire, grow or reproduce by itself; it can only be copied by hijacking a host cell's own machinery.

The three structures every virus needs — spec-exact detail

StructureSpec-exact description
Genetic materialDNA or RNA — different viruses carry either type; never assume all viruses share one.
CapsidA protein coat, built from repeating subunits called capsomeres. Never call this a "capsule" — that word belongs to bacteria (Section 2).
Attachment protein(s)Project from the capsid and bind a complementary receptor on a specific host cell. The attachment protein belongs to the virus; the receptor belongs to the host cell — they are not the same thing, and this distinction is examined repeatedly.
Every virus particle needs exactly three structures — genetic material, a capsid built from capsomeres, and attachment protein(s) — nothing else is required by this spec point.
▶ 🚩 Both directions are explicitly wrong

"Capsule" for the viral coat is rejected — the credited term is capsid. "Receptor" for the viral attachment protein is also rejected — the receptor sits on the host cell, not on the virus. Keep the pair capsid/capsule, and the pair attachment protein/receptor, each strictly sorted by which organism they belong to.

"Acellular" and "non-living" — two separate ideas, defined precisely

Acellular means having no cell-surface membrane enclosing cytoplasm or organelles — a virus simply isn't built like a cell. This is worth learning word-for-word, because paraphrases built around "cannot reproduce alone" or "smaller than a cell" are the single most common reason this mark is lost nationally. Non-living is the separate claim that a virus cannot respire, grow or reproduce independently — it can only be replicated inside a host cell, using the host's own ribosomes, enzymes and nucleotides. Because a virus has no murein cell wall, no ribosomes of its own and no metabolic pathways, antibiotics — which target exactly those bacterial structures — are completely ineffective against it.

▶ 🚩 Trap — order matters, and "mRNA" is not the general answer

This sequence is marked as an ordered list — a jumbled but complete answer loses marks, so state the steps in the order above. A frequent error is stating that mRNA, rather than the viral genetic material in general, is what enters the host cell — that's only accurate for some virus types, not the general answer this spec point expects.

Antigenic variability — a multi-step reasoning chain

A mutation in the viral genetic material changes the sequence of amino acids coded for, which alters the tertiary structure of the attachment protein (through a changed pattern of bonding within it). This allows the virus to bind to a new host-cell receptor that it previously could not bind to. Existing antibodies and memory cells were built to recognise the old attachment-protein shape, so they no longer recognise the mutated form — which is exactly why flu vaccines need repeat updating, and why reinfection with a mutated virus is possible.

▶ Unfamiliar-virus questions (e.g. HPV) — same checklist, new label

Whatever the virus, and however unfamiliar the diagram looks, the same three-structure checklist always applies: genetic material, capsid, attachment protein(s). A recurring error is muddling an unfamiliar virus with bacterial structure — crediting a feature such as a cell wall, flagellum or plasmid to a virus. Neither a bacterial capsule nor a bacterial flagellum is ever correct for a virus, no matter which virus is shown.

Section 4 · exam technique

🎯 Common mistakes & precise wording

This spec point is short in content but high in confusion risk between look-alike terms — most lost marks come from precision, not from a genuine gap in understanding.

Vague or wrong versionWhat to say instead
"Capsule, flagellum, plasmid" for structures in every bacteriumOnly the four universal structures count: murein cell wall, 70S ribosomes, circular protein-free DNA, cytoplasm lacking organelles
"Capsid" for the bacterial capsule, or "capsule" for the viral coatCapsule = bacterium; capsid = virus — never swap them
"Receptor" for the virus's own attachment proteinAttachment protein = on the virus; receptor = on the host cell
"Acellular" explained as "cannot reproduce alone" or "non-living"Acellular means no cell-surface membrane enclosing cytoplasm/organelles — a separate idea from "non-living"
"Ribosomes" given as a structure unique to prokaryotesAll cellular organisms have ribosomes — the creditable difference is that prokaryotic ribosomes are smaller (70S vs 80S)
Prokaryotic circular DNA described as single-strandedIt is double-stranded, exactly like all DNA — only its shape (circular, protein-free) is different
A one-sided micrograph comparison ("the prokaryote has no nucleus")Every comparison point needs both cell types named in the same sentence
"mRNA enters the host cell" as the general replication answerThe general answer is "the viral genetic material" — mRNA entry is only true for some virus types
Antibiotics "don't work on viruses because they hide inside cells"Antibiotics fail because viruses lack the bacterial structures (cell wall, 70S ribosomes) antibiotics actually target
▶ Why this content trips people up despite being short

The two structure checklists are answered poorly at first pass because plasmid, capsule and flagellum feel like the "interesting" or memorable structures — so they get written down even when a question restricts the answer to structures in every bacterium. "Acellular" is very poorly defined in practice, because students reach for "non-living" or "cannot reproduce" instead of the precise membrane/cytoplasm/organelle definition. At the more advanced end, the same core content is stretched into fully comparative micrograph analysis and the antigenic-variability reasoning chain (mutation → altered attachment-protein tertiary structure → new receptor binding), which is genuinely multi-step reasoning, not simple recall — this content underpins the micrograph and scale-bar interpretation skills used elsewhere in microscopy questions too.

Section 5 · exam technique

✍️ Using this content in longer written answers

This is a short, self-contained spec point, but it makes a reliable "structure relates to function" paragraph inside a longer essay-style answer — the trick is closing each paragraph with a sentence that ties the structure back to whatever the essay title is actually about.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around antibiotic action

The bacterial cell wall, built from murein rather than cellulose or chitin, and the smaller 70S ribosome are both structures a human cell simply does not have. This structural difference is exactly what allows antibiotics to target bacteria selectively — disrupting murein synthesis or binding to the 70S ribosome — while leaving the patient's own cells, with their different cell-surface chemistry and 80S ribosomes, unaffected.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around viral structure and immunity

A virus particle's minimal three-part structure — genetic material, a capsid built from capsomeres, and attachment proteins that bind a complementary host-cell receptor — is also its main vulnerability to change: a single mutation in the genetic material can alter the tertiary structure of the attachment protein, letting the virus evade antibodies raised against its previous shape. This structural simplicity is precisely why some vaccines, such as those against influenza, require regular updating.

Whichever essay theme this content supports, use it as a two-sentence structural anchor early in the paragraph, then explicitly "cash in" the structure later — the cell wall when discussing antibiotic targets, the lack of membrane-bound organelles when contrasting with eukaryotic complexity, and the attachment protein when discussing vaccination and antigenic variability.

Section 6

🧷 Memory hook, spec links & final checklist

Where 3.2.1.2 reappears later in the course

  • Microscopy — using these structures to identify cell type from a micrograph, and calculating actual size from a given magnification.
  • DNA structure and cell division — contrasting circular, protein-free prokaryotic DNA with the linear, histone-associated DNA of a eukaryotic cell.
  • Immunity — the attachment protein acting as the antigen antibodies target, and antigenic variability explaining vaccine failure and reinfection.
  • Drug action / antibiotics — the murein cell wall and 70S ribosomes as antibiotic targets a bacterium has and a human cell doesn't.
  • Gene expression & mutation — the underlying mechanism of antigenic variability, where a base-sequence change alters a protein's tertiary structure.
🧠

Memory hook. Bacteria — "wall, ribosomes, ring" for every bacterium: a murein cell wall, 70S ribosomes, and a circular DNA ring free in the cytoplasm; "cap, plasmid, tail" for some bacteria only: a capsule, a plasmid, or a flagellum. Viruses — "GCA": Genetic material, Capsid, Attachment protein, three structures and nothing more. Never let the bacterial "cap" and the viral "GCA" swap their spelling — capsule stays with bacteria, capsid stays with viruses.

Self-check before moving on

  • I can list the four structures found in every bacterium (murein cell wall, 70S ribosomes, circular protein-free DNA, cytoplasm lacking membrane-bound organelles), separately from the three found in some bacteria only (capsule, plasmid, flagellum).
  • I can describe a virus particle using exactly three structures — genetic material, capsid (built from capsomeres), attachment protein(s) — and state that viruses are acellular and non-living.
  • I can define "acellular" precisely (no cell-surface membrane enclosing cytoplasm/organelles) without confusing it with "non-living."
  • I can reproduce the five-step viral replication cycle in the correct order, and explain antigenic variability as: mutation → altered attachment-protein tertiary structure → binding to a new host receptor → evades existing immunity.
  • I never write "capsid" for the bacterial capsule, or "capsule"/"receptor" for any part of a virus, and I never treat a comparison of eukaryotic vs prokaryotic cells as a pair of one-sided lists.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Features in every prokaryotic cell

  • Four universal structures: murein cell wall (not cellulose/chitin), 70S ribosomes (smaller than 80S), circular DNA free in cytoplasm with no histones, cytoplasm with no membrane-bound organelles.
  • Prokaryotes are much smaller: ≈0.1–10 µm vs ≈10–100 µm for eukaryotes.
  • Comparisons must be two-sided in one sentence — never a one-sided list.
  • Circular DNA is still double-stranded — only its shape and lack of histones differ.

2 · Features in only some prokaryotes

  • Plasmid(s), capsule, flagellum/flagella — never given if a question asks for structures in every bacterium.
  • Only one flagellum needs naming for full credit.
  • Capsule (bacterium) ≠ capsid (virus) — always sorted by organism.

3 · Virus structure & replication

  • Exactly three structures: genetic material (DNA or RNA), capsid (capsomeres), attachment protein(s) binding a host receptor.
  • Acellular = no cell-surface membrane enclosing cytoplasm/organelles. Non-living = cannot respire/grow/reproduce alone.
  • Replication order: attachment → nucleic acid entry → genome replicated → viral protein made → assembly & release.
  • Antigenic variability: mutation → altered attachment-protein tertiary structure → new receptor binding → evades immunity.
🧠

Wall, ribosomes, ring / cap, plasmid, tail / GCA. Every bacterium: wall, ribosomes, ring. Some bacteria: cap, plasmid, tail. Every virus: Genetic material, Capsid, Attachment protein — and nothing more.

Features in every prokaryotic cell

❓ State two structural features that would be found in every bacterial cell, but not in a human cell.
Any two of: a cell wall containing murein, a glycoprotein (not cellulose or chitin); ribosomes that are smaller (70S) than eukaryotic ribosomes (80S); a single circular DNA molecule free in the cytoplasm, not associated with proteins, instead of a nucleus.
❓ A student describes bacterial DNA as "a single strand of DNA." Identify what is wrong with this description and correct it.
The description wrongly implies the DNA is single-stranded. Bacterial DNA is a single circular molecule that is still double-stranded, like all DNA — it differs from eukaryotic DNA in being circular, free in the cytoplasm, and not associated with (histone) proteins, not in the number of strands.
❓ Using labelled electron micrographs of a named eukaryotic cell and a named prokaryotic cell, give two comparative differences in their structure.
The eukaryotic cell has a nucleus, whereas the prokaryotic cell has DNA free in the cytoplasm instead; the eukaryotic cell has mitochondria present, whereas the prokaryotic cell has no mitochondria. (Any similarly phrased comparative pair, each naming both cell types in one sentence, would also be credited.)

Features in only some prokaryotes

❓ A question asks for structures found in every bacterium. A student answers "capsule, flagellum, cell wall." Explain why this answer would score at most one mark.
Capsule and flagellum are structures found in some bacteria only, not in every bacterium, so neither can be credited against this question. Only "cell wall" (provided it is qualified as murein/a glycoprotein) belongs to the universal list, so at most one mark is available from this answer.
❓ Distinguish between a bacterial capsule and a plasmid.
A capsule is a layer that surrounds the outside of the cell wall; a plasmid is a small circular loop of DNA, separate from the main circular DNA molecule, found inside the cytoplasm. Both are structures found in some bacteria only, not every bacterium.
❓ A diagram of a virus shows a protective outer coat. A student labels this "capsule." Explain why this would not be credited, and give the correct term.
"Capsule" refers to the layer surrounding some bacterial cells, not to any structure of a virus, so it is rejected here. The correct term for the virus's protein coat is capsid.

Virus structure & replication

❓ Describe the structure of a named virus particle.
Genetic material — DNA or RNA; a capsid — a protein coat built from repeating subunits called capsomeres; and one or more attachment proteins, which project from the capsid and bind to a complementary receptor on a specific host cell.
❓ Explain why viruses are described as both acellular and non-living, making clear that these are two separate ideas.
Acellular: a virus has no cytoplasm, no organelles and no cell-surface membrane of its own, so it is not built like a cell at all. Non-living: a virus cannot respire, grow or reproduce independently — it can only be replicated inside a host cell, using the host's own ribosomes, enzymes and nucleotides.
❓ Explain, in terms of protein structure, how a mutation in a virus's genetic material can allow it to infect a host cell it previously could not infect.
A mutation in the viral genetic material changes the sequence of amino acids coded for; this alters the tertiary structure of the attachment protein, through a changed pattern of bonding within it; the altered attachment protein can now bind to a different, complementary receptor on a new host cell that it could not previously bind to.
