⚠️

The single habit that protects the most marks on this spec point: never write "pentose" where the specific sugar is wanted — always name deoxyribose for DNA or ribose for RNA. Never abbreviate a base to a single letter — write thymine or uracil in full, not "T" or "U". And keep the two bond types apart: DNA polymerase forms phosphodiester bonds within a strand, never hydrogen bonds between strands — mixing these two up is one of the most repeated errors on this spec point.

Section 1 · spec content point

📜 What DNA and RNA actually do

Before any structural detail, this spec point rewards getting the job description of each molecule exactly right. Swap "holds" for "transfers", or the other way round, and the mark disappears even if everything else you write is correct.

📖

What the specification says: "Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) and ribonucleic acid (RNA) are important information-carrying molecules. In all living cells, DNA holds genetic information and RNA transfers genetic information from DNA to the ribosomes."

1
2
3

Think of DNA as the original master copy of an instruction manual, locked safely away in an archive (the nucleus) — nobody is ever allowed to take the original out. RNA is like a single photocopied page, carried out of the archive to wherever those instructions are actually needed. The photocopy doesn't get kept forever — it does its job and is thrown away, while the original stays locked up and untouched.

  • DNA = the original manual, kept safe
  • RNA = a working photocopy, carried out to be used
  • Genetic information = the instructions written in the manual
  • Ribosome = the workshop where the copied instructions actually get used

DNA is the molecule that permanently stores an organism's genetic information — it stays inside the cell as the master record and gets passed on whenever the cell divides. RNA has a different job: it transfers a working copy of that genetic information away from DNA, out to the ribosomes, where it's actually put to use. RNA doesn't hold onto the information long-term — it delivers it and is then broken down. Occasionally, a gene's "instructions" are for a piece of RNA itself, not for a protein — RNA can be the finished, functional product, not just a messenger carrying someone else's message.

Two precise verbs carry the entire mark here, and examiners do not treat them as interchangeable:

  • DNA holds the genetic information — it is the permanent, stored record for the life of the cell (and is passed on at cell division).
  • RNA transfers genetic information from DNA to the ribosomes — it carries a working copy outward; it does not store the original.
  • A gene does not have to code for a protein: it can code directly for a functional RNA molecule — such as rRNA or tRNA — rather than for a polypeptide. This point resurfaces in Section 2, since one of those functional RNA molecules becomes part of the ribosome itself.
🚩

Never write "RNA stores genetic information." The specification's precision matters here: DNA holds/stores; RNA only transfers. A "state" question wants exactly these two verbs, correctly assigned — nothing more elaborate is needed, and nothing less precise will earn the mark.

📌

Command word note. "State the roles of DNA and RNA" is a state question — it wants the precise holds/transfers distinction expressed in clear terms, not a description of structure layered on top. Save the structural detail for Sections 3–5.

Section 2 · spec content point

🏭 Ribosomes are formed from RNA and proteins

It's tempting to picture a ribosome purely as a protein-building machine made of protein. The specification is precise: ribosomes are themselves partly built from RNA.

📖

What the specification says: "Ribosomes are formed from RNA and proteins."

1
2
3

Imagine a workshop that reads photocopied instructions and builds furniture from them. Strangely, part of the workshop's own walls and machinery are built out of the very same paper-like material as the photocopies it reads. That's a ribosome: a machine built partly from RNA (a special kind called rRNA) and partly from protein, which reads RNA copies of instructions and builds proteins from them.

  • rRNA = the "ribosomal RNA" that helps build the ribosome itself
  • Ribosome = the machine where proteins get built, made of rRNA + protein together

Ribosomes are made of two ingredients combined: a type of RNA called ribosomal RNA (rRNA), and protein. This matters because it shows RNA isn't only a messenger — one kind of RNA becomes a permanent, structural part of the very machine that reads genetic messages and builds proteins from them.

Ribosomes are formed from RNA and proteins together — specifically, a type of RNA called ribosomal RNA (rRNA) combines with protein to build the ribosome itself.

  • This gives RNA a second, structural role beyond simply carrying a copy of the genetic code: one class of RNA molecule becomes a permanent component of the machine that reads that code.
  • It also means a "what are ribosomes made of" answer needs both components named — RNA and protein — not protein alone.
  • This links directly back to Section 1: RNA "transfers genetic information from DNA to the ribosomes" — and the ribosome that information arrives at is itself partly built from RNA, so RNA is a structural component of the very machine it delivers instructions to.
🚩

Trap — naming only protein. If asked what ribosomes are formed from, "protein" alone misses half the available credit; the specification names both RNA and protein, and both need to appear in the answer.

Section 3 · spec content point

🧩 Nucleotide structure — both versions, learnt cold

Every nucleotide, DNA or RNA, is built from exactly the same three-part pattern — only the sugar and one base ever change between the two versions.

📖

What the specification says: "Both DNA and RNA are polymers of nucleotides. Each nucleotide is formed from a pentose, a nitrogen-containing organic base and phosphate group: • The components of a DNA nucleotide are deoxyribose, a phosphate group and one of the organic bases adenine, cytosine, guanine or thymine. • The components of an RNA nucleotide are ribose, a phosphate group and one of the organic bases adenine, cytosine, guanine or uracil. • A condensation reaction between two nucleotides forms a phosphodiester bond."

1
2
3

A nucleotide is like a train carriage with exactly three parts bolted together: a coupling hook (phosphate), a body section (a sugar), and a little flag on top (a base). Lots of these carriages link up hook-to-body to form a long train — that's the whole DNA or RNA strand. Joining two carriages together squeezes out a tiny drop of water — that's the "condensation reaction" — and the new coupling made is called a phosphodiester bond.

  • Nucleotide = one "carriage": sugar + phosphate + base
  • Pentose = the sugar part (deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA)
  • Condensation reaction = joining two units together and losing a water molecule
  • Phosphodiester bond = the new coupling formed between two nucleotides
  • Monomer / polymer = one carriage / the whole train of repeating carriages

Every nucleotide, whether it belongs to DNA or RNA, is a three-part building block: a sugar, a phosphate group, and a nitrogen-containing base. The two versions differ in exactly two places — which sugar, and one of the four bases:

ComponentDNARNA
SugarDeoxyriboseRibose
Phosphate groupOneOne
BasesAdenine, cytosine, guanine, thymineAdenine, cytosine, guanine, uracil

Nucleotides join together by losing a water molecule (a condensation reaction) between the phosphate of one and the sugar of the next, forming a bond called a phosphodiester bond — this is what builds the long backbone of the strand.

Pentose sugarDeoxyriboseRibose

Purine/pyrimidine terminology is not required by this spec point — just the four named bases in each list, and which sugar goes with which.

A DNA nucleotide and an RNA nucleotide, each drawn as phosphate–sugar–base. Condensation between the phosphate of one nucleotide and the sugar of the next (green bond) forms a phosphodiester bond and releases water.

How nucleotides are joined

A condensation reaction — a water molecule is lost — occurs between the phosphate group of one nucleotide and the sugar (deoxyribose or ribose) of the next. The reaction is catalysed by DNA polymerase, and forms a phosphodiester bond, producing the sugar–phosphate backbone. Phonetic spellings of "phosphodiester" are perfectly acceptable — what's being marked is whether you know the bond, not your spelling.

▶ 🚩 Trap — what DNA polymerase actually joins

DNA polymerase joins nucleotides within one strand — it does not "join A–T / G–C", does not "join complementary nucleotides or bases", and does not "form hydrogen bonds". Any of those phrasings loses the mark, because DNA polymerase's job is purely to build the phosphodiester backbone of a single strand, not to link the two strands together.

📌

Two bonds, two jobs — never confuse them. Phosphodiester bonds join nucleotides within a strand (the backbone). Hydrogen bonds join the two strands, between complementary bases (Section 4). If a question mentions DNA polymerase, the correct bond is phosphodiester — never hydrogen.

▶ Contrasting a DNA nucleotide with ATP

A DNA nucleotide and ATP share the same basic nucleotide-style layout, but differ in three linked ways — both halves of each point are needed for credit:

  • ATP's sugar is ribose; a DNA nucleotide's sugar is deoxyribose.
  • ATP has three phosphate groups; a DNA nucleotide has one.
  • ATP's base is always adenine; a DNA nucleotide's base varies (adenine, cytosine, thymine or guanine are all accepted examples).

An annotated diagram is an accepted way to make any of these three points. Never give uracil as an example of a varying DNA base — uracil belongs to RNA only, never DNA.

▶ What makes a molecule a polymer

A polymer is defined by having a repeating unit — a chain of nucleotides, monomers, or repeating molecules, more than one acceptable wording is possible, but a genuine repeating pattern has to be shown. Simply writing "two" is not enough — two units on their own don't demonstrate a repeating pattern.

Section 4 · spec content point

🌀 The DNA double helix and base pairing

Structure and function meet here: two strands, twisted together, held by hydrogen bonds between bases that only ever pair one specific way.

📖

What the specification says: "A DNA molecule is a double helix with two polynucleotide chains held together by hydrogen bonds between specific complementary base pairs."

1
2
3

Picture a rope ladder that's been twisted around into a spiral — that's DNA's double helix. The two long side-ropes are the sugar–phosphate backbones. Each rung of the ladder is actually two bases holding hands across the middle — but they're picky about who they hold hands with: one type of base only ever holds hands with one particular partner, never any other.

  • Double helix = the twisted-ladder shape
  • Hydrogen bond = the "handshake" holding each pair of bases together
  • Complementary base pairing = the rule for who's allowed to hold hands with whom
  • Antiparallel = the two side-ropes run in opposite directions

DNA is two strands twisted together into a double helix, like a spiral ladder. The two strands are held to each other by hydrogen bonds between bases, and those bases only pair in fixed combinations: adenine always pairs with thymine, and cytosine always pairs with guanine. The sugar–phosphate backbone of each strand sits on the outside of the helix, with the bases pointing inward towards each other, and the two strands run in opposite directions (antiparallel). A useful side-fact for base-frequency questions: because A always pairs with T and C always pairs with G, the percentage of A in a DNA molecule always equals the percentage of T, and the percentage of C always equals the percentage of G.

Describing the structure of a DNA molecule

  • Two polynucleotide chains, wound into a double helix.
  • Held together by hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairs.
  • Adenine pairs with thymine (2 hydrogen bonds); cytosine pairs with guanine (3 hydrogen bonds).
  • The strands are antiparallel — they run in opposite directions.
  • The sugar–phosphate backbone sits on the outside, formed by phosphodiester bonds, with the bases pointing inward.
The two strands run antiparallel; A–T pairs form 2 hydrogen bonds, G–C pairs form 3 — the extra bond is why C–G-rich DNA is more thermally stable (see below).

Explaining how the structure suits the function

FeatureConsequence
Very long moleculeStores a large amount of information
Double helix / sugar–phosphate backboneProtects the bases and gives stability
Hydrogen bonds between basesIndividually weak, so the strands separate easily for replication and transcription — but strong in large numbers, so the molecule is stable overall
Complementary base pairingAllows accurate replication, so genetic information is passed on unchanged
Base sequenceCarries the code for the amino acid sequence of polypeptides
▶ 🧮 The named maths skill in practice — Chargaff's rule (MS 0.3)

Because A always pairs with T, and C always pairs with G, in a double-stranded DNA molecule: %A = %T and %C = %G, so %A + %G = %C + %T = 50%. Between the two strands, every base on strand 1 is matched by its complementary base on strand 2 at the same percentage — strand 2's %T equals strand 1's %A, strand 2's %G equals strand 1's %C, and so on.

Worked example. Strand 1 of a DNA molecule is analysed: A = 30%, T = 20%, C = 15%, G = 35%.

  • Step 1 — swap complementary values to get strand 2: T(2) = 30%, A(2) = 20%, G(2) = 15%, C(2) = 35%.
  • Step 2 — average the two strands to find the whole-molecule percentages: overall A = (30 + 20) ÷ 2 = 25% = overall T; overall G = (35 + 15) ÷ 2 = 25% = overall C.
  • Check: 25% + 25% = 50%, confirming %A + %G = 50% for the whole molecule.

Show every intermediate step in a calculation like this — credit is available for correct working even if the final answer ends up wrong.

Watch for, every time: whether the figure given is a percentage or a number of bases; whether it refers to one strand or the whole molecule; and whether the molecule is DNA or RNA — Chargaff's rule does not apply to single-stranded RNA.

▶ Reading a DNA diagram — boxing a nucleotide and counting bonds

To box a single nucleotide on a diagram, enclose exactly one phosphate, one sugar and one base — no more and no less; a box containing two sugars, two phosphates, or no base at all is wrong.

To count hydrogen bonds across a section of DNA: multiply the number of A–T pairs by 2 and the number of C–G pairs by 3, then add the two totals together — the answer written in words is accepted just as well as a number.

A section of DNA with a higher proportion of C–G pairs has more hydrogen bonds overall, so it is more stable and needs more energy (a higher temperature) to separate the two strands.

🚩

A one-sided contrast with RNA never scores fully. "DNA is double-stranded" alone earns nothing in a contrast question — both halves of the statement are required, e.g. "DNA is double-stranded and RNA is single-stranded." This "both halves" rule reappears throughout Section 5.

Section 5 · spec content point

📄 RNA: a single, relatively short strand

RNA is not a shrunken-down version of DNA with a strand missing — it never had a partner strand to begin with, and that single-strandedness is itself part of what the specification wants named.

📖

What the specification says: "An RNA molecule is a relatively short polynucleotide chain."

1
2
3

If DNA is a thick, bound book kept in a library, RNA is a single sticky note torn off a pad — written on one side only, and much shorter. It was never half of a bigger book with pages ripped out; it was always meant to be just one small, single-sided note.

  • Polynucleotide chain = a strand made of many joined nucleotides
  • Single-stranded = only one chain, no partner strand
  • Relatively short = much shorter than a DNA molecule

Unlike DNA's two long, twisted-together strands, an RNA molecule is just one polynucleotide chain, and it's relatively short. It was never double-stranded to begin with — it's not "half of a DNA molecule" that lost its partner. Because RNA only carries a working copy of information rather than storing it permanently, it doesn't need DNA's length or its double-stranded stability.

FeatureDNARNA
Pentose sugarDeoxyriboseRibose
BasesAdenine, cytosine, guanine, thymineAdenine, cytosine, guanine, uracil
StrandsTwo, wound into a double helix, held by hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairsOne — a single polynucleotide chain
LengthVery long — stores a large amount of informationRelatively short
🔎

RNA is not a shrunken DNA — it never had a second strand to begin with. This is exactly why tRNA is not double-stranded, a very common wrong assumption to avoid: every RNA molecule, whatever job it does, is fundamentally a single, relatively short chain.

⚠️

The "both halves" rule, again. In any "contrast" answer, both molecules must be named explicitly — "DNA is double-stranded and RNA is single-stranded" earns the mark; "DNA is double-stranded" alone does not, even though the RNA half might feel obvious from context.

Being short and single-stranded suits RNA's role directly: since Section 1 established that RNA only ever transfers a working copy rather than storing the original, it doesn't need DNA's permanence, length, or double-stranded stability — a single, disposable, relatively short chain is exactly proportionate to a temporary carrier's job.

Section 6 · beyond the spec-content list

🔬 Why DNA was doubted, and Chargaff's rule in practice

Two extra AQA skills tied closely to this spec point: an appreciation of why DNA's simplicity once counted against it, and the named maths skill for turning incomplete base data into a full answer.

Why DNA was initially rejected as the genetic material

DNA has only four different bases and a repetitive sugar–phosphate backbone, so it originally appeared far too simple to carry the enormous variety of information a genetic code would need. Proteins — built from twenty different amino acids, with vastly more possible sequences — seemed the more plausible candidate, and were also already known to be present in chromosomes, as histones.

💡

What changed the view. Experiments showing that DNA, not protein, transformed bacteria and carried viral genetic information, together with Chargaff's base ratios and X-ray diffraction data, made the double-helix model possible — and made DNA, not protein, the credible candidate for the genetic material.

The wider point this illustrates: a scientific claim is accepted once it is supported by evidence, and a plausible-sounding assumption can still turn out to be wrong. The same theme reappears later in the course, with the validation of the Watson–Crick double-helix model itself.

Chargaff's rule, worked in full (the named maths skill)

The full method and a worked example live in Section 4's "explain more simply" panel, alongside the double-helix structure it depends on. As a quick summary: in a double-stranded DNA molecule, %A always equals %T and %C always equals %G, so %A + %G = %C + %T = 50%; base percentages swap over between the two complementary strands. The rule only applies to double-stranded DNA — never to single-stranded RNA.

▶ Where this spec point sits in difficulty terms

The biology itself is not conceptually demanding, but this is a high-precision spec point: memorisation load is moderate (learning both nucleotide versions, the pairing rules and Chargaff's rule cold), while the risk of losing marks purely on wording is high. Diagram-labelling questions in particular reward exact reproduction — bonds drawn in the wrong place, or "pentose" used instead of the named sugar, are among the most repeated errors. "Compare"/"contrast" questions require answers written explicitly in terms of both molecules — a one-sided answer, however correct, scores nothing.

Section 8 · exam technique

✍️ Using this content in longer written answers

This spec point works well as an opening or supporting paragraph inside a longer essay-style answer — the trick is closing each paragraph with a sentence that ties the structural detail back to whatever the essay title is actually about, rather than repeating a generic summary.

▶ Example paragraph — opening a paragraph on the importance of RNA

"RNA is a polymer of nucleotides, each made of ribose, a phosphate group and one of the organic bases adenine, cytosine, guanine or uracil, forming a relatively short, single polynucleotide strand — unlike DNA's long, double-stranded helix. RNA does not store genetic information; it transfers it from DNA to the ribosomes, which are themselves formed from RNA and protein. This structural distinction underlies every one of RNA's later roles, from carrying the genetic code during transcription to acting as a direct target of gene regulation." Notice what this paragraph deliberately avoids: describing RNA as double-stranded, or as storing genetic information — both are precision traps worth watching for in essay writing, not just short-answer questions.

▶ Example paragraph — nitrogen-containing substances in biological systems

"The organic bases that make DNA and RNA 'information-carrying' molecules are, by definition, nitrogen-containing; and the hydrogen bonds joining complementary base pairs in DNA's double helix are bonds between nitrogen-containing bases. Nitrogen is therefore chemically essential to the storage and transfer of genetic information, not just to the structure of proteins." This paragraph works as one of several supporting points in a broader essay on nitrogen's biological importance, rather than as the essay's central focus.

Where 3.1.5.1 reappears later in the specification

  • DNA replication — semi-conservative replication depends directly on the double helix and complementary base pairing established here.

  • DNA, protein synthesis — transcription copies the base sequence into mRNA using the same base-pairing logic (with A pairing U instead of T); translation reads that sequence via tRNA.

Rule of thumb: whenever a later question needs a base to pair with its partner, or a bond within a nucleic acid to form or break, the vocabulary from this page — deoxyribose / ribose, phosphodiester, hydrogen bond, complementary base pairing — is the expected answer.

🧠

Memory hook. Deoxyribose has one fewer oxygen than ribose — and DNA has just one base "swapped out" compared with RNA (thymine for uracil; everything else is shared). For the bonds: A–T is the short, plain pairing — 2 hydrogen bonds; C–G is the fancier, three-ringed pairing — 3 hydrogen bonds. Say it as "A–T, easy as 1-2" and "C–G, easy as 1-2-3." And for the roles: DNA holds, RNA transfers — never the other way round.

5-Minute Cram

The highest-yield bullets from each spec-content section — read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · What DNA and RNA do

  • DNA holds genetic information; RNA transfers it from DNA to the ribosomes — never say RNA "stores".
  • A gene can code directly for a functional RNA molecule (rRNA or tRNA), not only for a polypeptide.

2 · Ribosomes: RNA + protein

  • Ribosomes are formed from RNA (rRNA) and protein — always name both.
  • RNA is a structural component of the very machine it delivers instructions to.

3 · Nucleotide structure

  • 3 components every time: pentose sugar + phosphate group + nitrogen-containing base.
  • DNA = deoxyribose + A/C/G/T; RNA = ribose + A/C/G/U.
  • Condensation reaction (loses water) between the phosphate of one nucleotide and the sugar of the next, catalysed by DNA polymerase, forms a phosphodiester bond — never a hydrogen bond.
  • ATP vs DNA nucleotide: ribose not deoxyribose, 3 phosphates not 1, always adenine not a varying base.

4 · The DNA double helix

  • Two polynucleotide chains, wound into a double helix, antiparallel.
  • Held by hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairs: A–T = 2 bonds, C–G = 3 bonds.
  • Sugar–phosphate backbone outside, bases point inward.
  • Chargaff's rule: %A = %T, %C = %G, so %A + %G = 50% — DNA only, never RNA.
  • More C–G pairs = more hydrogen bonds = more thermally stable, needing more energy to separate.

5 · RNA: single, short strand

  • RNA = one polynucleotide chain, relatively short — never assume double-stranded (tRNA included).
  • RNA never had a partner strand — it's not "half of DNA".
  • In any "contrast" answer, both molecules must be named explicitly.
🧠

No calcium needed here — just precision. Name the sugar, spell out the base, keep phosphodiester and hydrogen bonds apart, and always answer "contrast" questions with both molecules named.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

What DNA and RNA do

❓ State the roles of DNA and RNA in a cell.
DNA holds the genetic information. RNA transfers genetic information from DNA to the ribosomes. Ribosomes are formed from RNA (rRNA) and proteins.
❓ A student writes "RNA stores a temporary copy of the genetic code." Identify and correct the imprecise wording.
"Stores" is imprecise — DNA stores/holds genetic information; RNA transfers it. The corrected statement should read "RNA transfers a copy of the genetic information from DNA to the ribosomes."
❓ Explain why a gene does not always need to be translated into a protein to have a function.
A gene can code directly for a functional RNA molecule, such as rRNA or tRNA, rather than for a polypeptide — the RNA product itself is the functional molecule, with no protein translation step required.

Ribosomes: RNA + protein

❓ State what ribosomes are formed from.
Ribosomes are formed from RNA and proteins.
❓ A student answers "ribosomes are made of protein" when asked for their composition. Explain what credit this answer would lose and why.
The answer would lose half the available credit for omitting RNA — ribosomes are formed from both RNA (ribosomal RNA) and protein; naming protein alone is incomplete.

Nucleotide structure

❓ Name the three components of a nucleotide, and state how the DNA and RNA versions differ.
A nucleotide is made of a pentose sugar, a phosphate group and a nitrogen-containing organic base. A DNA nucleotide contains deoxyribose and one of adenine, cytosine, guanine or thymine; an RNA nucleotide contains ribose and one of adenine, cytosine, guanine or uracil.
❓ Describe how two nucleotides are joined together.
A condensation reaction, with the loss of a water molecule, occurs between the phosphate group of one nucleotide and the sugar of the next; the reaction is catalysed by DNA polymerase and forms a phosphodiester bond, producing the sugar–phosphate backbone.
❓ Contrast a DNA nucleotide with a molecule of ATP.
ATP contains ribose, whereas a DNA nucleotide contains deoxyribose; ATP has three phosphate groups, whereas a DNA nucleotide has one; ATP's base is always adenine, whereas a DNA nucleotide's base varies (adenine, cytosine, thymine or guanine).

The DNA double helix

❓ Describe the structure of a DNA molecule.
Two polynucleotide chains are wound into a double helix, held together by hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairs; adenine pairs with thymine by 2 hydrogen bonds, and cytosine pairs with guanine by 3 hydrogen bonds; the two strands are antiparallel, and the sugar–phosphate backbone lies on the outside, with the bases pointing inward.
❓ One DNA strand is found to contain 18% cytosine. Calculate the percentage of adenine in the whole molecule, showing your working.
%C = %G, so this strand also has 18% G; on the complementary strand, %G = 18% of this strand's %C, meaning %C(strand 2) = %G(strand 1) — working through the whole-molecule average, %C + %G = 36% overall, so %A + %T = 100% − 36% = 64%, and since %A = %T, %A = 32%.
❓ A DNA sample from one species has a higher proportion of C–G pairs than a sample from a second species. Predict and explain the effect on thermal stability.
The sample with more C–G pairs has more hydrogen bonds overall, because each C–G pair contributes 3 hydrogen bonds compared with 2 for an A–T pair; more hydrogen bonds means the strands are held together more strongly, so more energy (a higher temperature) is needed to separate them — it is more thermally stable.

RNA: single, short strand

❓ Contrast the structure of a DNA molecule with the structure of an RNA molecule.
DNA is double-stranded and RNA is single-stranded; DNA is a very long molecule and RNA is relatively short; DNA contains deoxyribose and thymine, whereas RNA contains ribose and uracil.
❓ A student assumes that tRNA, like DNA, must be double-stranded because it folds into a complex shape. Explain why this assumption is incorrect.
All RNA molecules, including tRNA, are single, relatively short polynucleotide chains — RNA never had a second, complementary strand. Any folding into a complex shape happens within the single strand, not because a second strand is present.
❓ Suggest why it is appropriate that RNA is single-stranded and relatively short, given its role in the cell.
RNA only transfers a working copy of genetic information rather than storing it permanently, so it does not need the length or double-stranded stability that DNA's long-term storage role requires — a shorter, single, more easily made and broken-down molecule is proportionate to a temporary carrier.
