General Properties of Proteins

How one monomer — the amino acid — builds every protein in the body: the peptide bond that joins them, the four levels of structure and exactly which bond is named at each one, why two proteins ever end up a different shape, and the biuret test used to detect a protein at all.

⚠️

The single habit that protects the most marks on this whole spec point: primary structure is the sequence / order of amino acids in the polypeptide — not merely "amino acids joined together." Examiner feedback on the AS-tier version of this recall question shows a large majority of students losing marks purely by leaving "sequence/order" out. The second most costly habit: writing "3°" as shorthand for tertiary structure — this is never accepted; every level name has to be written out in full at least once.

Section 1 · spec content point

🧩 Amino acids — the one monomer every protein is built from

Every protein — from a tiny enzyme to a huge structural fibre — is a polymer built from just one type of repeating monomer. This section is about that one building block, and the single variable that makes every protein different.

📖

What the specification says: "Amino acids are the monomers from which proteins are made. The general structure of an amino acid, where NH2 represents an amine group, COOH represents a carboxyl group and R represents a side chain. The twenty amino acids that are common in all organisms differ only in their side group."

1
2
3

Imagine a building brick with exactly four studs sticking out of it. Three of those studs are always exactly the same on every single brick you own. But the fourth stud is a swappable sticker — and there are twenty different stickers you could clip on. Change the sticker, and you get a different-looking brick. An amino acid is that brick, and a protein is a huge tower built by clicking thousands of these bricks together in a particular order.

  • The brick itself = the central carbon atom
  • Two of the fixed studs = the amine group and the carboxyl group
  • The third fixed stud = a plain hydrogen atom
  • The swappable sticker = the R group — this is the only bit that ever changes

An amino acid is built around one central carbon atom, which always carries the same four attachments: an amine group (NH₂), a carboxyl group (COOH), a single hydrogen atom, and an R group (side chain). The amine and carboxyl groups, and the hydrogen, are identical on every one of the twenty amino acids that are common to all living organisms — the R group is the only part of the molecule that is ever different. It is that one variable group that eventually gives every protein its own distinctive three-dimensional shape, because the R group is what interacts with other R groups later on to fold the chain.

Part of the moleculeIs it the same in every amino acid?
Amine group (NH₂)Yes — identical in all 20
Carboxyl group (COOH)Yes — identical in all 20
Hydrogen atomYes — identical in all 20
R group (side chain)No — this is the only part that varies

Draw and label the general structure exactly like this: a central (alpha) carbon bonded to four different groups — an amine group (NH₂), a carboxyl group (COOH), a hydrogen atom, and an R group (side chain).

The central carbon carries four different groups. Only the R group changes between the 20 amino acids — everything else here is fixed.
📌

The sentence to learn word-for-word: the 20 amino acids common to all organisms differ only in their side group — everything else about the molecule is identical between them.

▶ Naming the end group — a question shared with lipids

If asked to name the functional group drawn at the end of the molecule in a diagram, the answer is the carboxyl group — "carboxylic acid" is also accepted. This exact naming task is set for fatty acids in the lipids topic too, so it's worth revising both structures side by side.

Section 2 · spec content point

🔗 The peptide bond — forming dipeptides and polypeptides

This is the reaction that turns individual amino-acid bricks into a chain. The mechanism is always the same, however long the chain gets — only the name of the product changes.

📖

What the specification says: "A condensation reaction between two amino acids forms a peptide bond. Dipeptides are formed by the condensation of two amino acids. Polypeptides are formed by the condensation of many amino acids. A functional protein may contain one or more polypeptides."

1
2
3

Think of two building bricks clicking together. As they snap into place, a tiny drop of water squeezes out — that's the "condensation" part. Click two bricks together and you get a short two-brick piece; keep clicking more bricks on and you get a long tower. A finished protein is one tower, or sometimes several separate towers standing next to each other. Pulling the bricks back apart works the opposite way round — you have to add a drop of water back in to break the click.

Two amino acids join together by a condensation reaction — a reaction that releases a molecule of water — forming a peptide bond between them. Specifically, the amine group of one amino acid reacts with the carboxyl group of the next one. Joining exactly two amino acids this way makes a dipeptide; joining many makes a polypeptide. A complete, functioning protein might be built from just one polypeptide chain, or from several chains joined together. The reverse reaction, hydrolysis, uses a water molecule to break a peptide bond apart again — this is how digestive enzymes break proteins back down into amino acids.

Forming the bond

  • It is a condensation reaction — a molecule of water is lost.
  • The two reacting groups are named precisely: the amine (NH₂) group of one amino acid joins the carboxyl (COOH) group of another ("amino group" and "carboxylic/acid group" are also accepted wordings).
  • The result each time is a specific sequence / order of amino acids — this idea applies whether two amino acids are joining to form a dipeptide, or many are joining to form a polypeptide.
  • Because one amine always reacts with one carboxyl, the finished chain keeps a free amine group at one end and a free carboxyl group at the other — every amino acid is orientated the same way along the chain.
The amine group of one amino acid reacts with the carboxyl group of the other; a water molecule is lost, and the new peptide bond forms between them.
▶ 📌 Marking habit worth building now

Each of the three ideas above — condensation/loss of water, the specific amine + carboxyl groups involved, and the resulting sequence — is credited even if only shown clearly in a labelled diagram, so a fully labelled sketch of two amino acids joining can be worth as much as a written description. Always include the "loss of water" half of the answer — it is very commonly the part that gets left out.

Breaking the bond

Hydrolysis breaks a peptide bond apart — the exact reverse of condensation — using a molecule of water. In digestion, three types of enzyme act on peptide bonds:

  • Endopeptidases hydrolyse peptide bonds within the polypeptide chain (internal bonds).
  • Exopeptidases remove amino acids one at a time from the ends of the chain.
  • Dipeptidases hydrolyse dipeptides into single amino acids.
▶ Why do endopeptidases act first in digestion?

Exopeptidases can only hydrolyse peptide bonds at the very ends of a chain, whereas endopeptidases hydrolyse bonds inside it. Cutting the chain internally first creates far more free "ends" — more surface area — for the exopeptidases to then work on simultaneously. A reference to hydrolysis specifically (not just "breaking down") is needed for full credit here.

Section 3 · spec content point

🧵 The four levels of protein structure

Every protein is folded through the same four stages, and each stage is held together by a specific, named bond or set of bonds. This is the single most word-perfect part of this spec point — get the exact bond at each level right, every time.

📖

What the specification says: "The role of hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds and disulfide bridges in the structure of proteins. Proteins have a variety of functions within all living organisms. The relationship between primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure, and protein function." (This section covers the four levels of structure and their bonds; Section 4 covers the same content point, but for why different proteins end up different shapes and how structure relates to function.)

1
2
3

Picture a long string of beads — that's the amino-acid chain on its own, in whatever order it happens to be in. Twist that string into a coiled phone cable or fold it flat like a fan, and you've got the next level up. Then scrunch the whole coiled string into a tangled ball — that's a much more folded shape. Sometimes two or more of these scrunched-up balls of string stick together to make one bigger clump. Each of those four stages — string, coil, scrunched ball, clump of balls — is a different "level" of protein structure.

The primary structure is simply the order the amino acids are joined in — nothing folded yet. The secondary structure is the chain folding into a coiled shape (an α-helix) or a flat, pleated shape (a β-pleated sheet), held together by hydrogen bonds along the backbone of the chain. The tertiary structure is the whole chain folding further into its final 3-D shape, held together this time by bonds between the R groups — hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds and disulfide bridges — and this final shape is what creates a protein's active site or binding site. The quaternary structure is what you get when two or more separate polypeptide chains associate together, using the same kinds of bonds, now acting between the chains rather than within one.

LevelWhat it isBond(s) involved
PrimaryThe sequence / order of amino acids in the polypeptide.Peptide bonds
SecondaryFolding of the polypeptide chain into an α-helix or a β-pleated sheet.Hydrogen bonds — between the N–H group of one amino acid and the C=O group of another
TertiaryThe 3-D folding of the whole polypeptide; determines the active site or binding site.Hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds and disulfide bridges — between R groups
QuaternaryTwo or more polypeptide chains associated together (may include a prosthetic group).The same interactions, now between the chains
The same amino-acid sequence, folded progressively further at each level — primary never changes again; everything from secondary onward is folding.
📌

The 1-mark quaternary definition: "more than one polypeptide" is enough on its own for a short quaternary-structure question — you don't need to mention a prosthetic group or name the bonds between chains unless the question specifically asks for more detail.

▶ 🚩 The traps that cost the most marks here

For the secondary structure bond, never write "N—C", an ionic bond, a disulfide bridge or a peptide bond — it must be a hydrogen bond, ideally named between the N–H and C=O groups specifically. And never abbreviate tertiary or quaternary structure as "3°" or similar shorthand — the word has to be written out in full at least once, every time. Roughly three-quarters of students can correctly define quaternary structure at A2 — the commonest slip is confusing it with tertiary structure, or assuming quaternary always means exactly four chains (carried over from haemoglobin being the one worked example most students see).

Section 4 · spec content point

🧭 Why proteins differ in shape — and why it matters

This is the single causal chain that answers a huge share of the questions on this spec point — from "why do two proteins have different shapes" all the way to "explain the effect of a gene mutation." Learn it as one unbroken chain, not four separate facts.

📖

What the specification says: "The role of hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds and disulfide bridges in the structure of proteins. Proteins have a variety of functions within all living organisms. The relationship between primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure, and protein function." (Same content point as Section 3 — this half covers why shape differs between proteins, and how structure relates to function.)

1
2
3

A protein's final scrunched-up shape depends completely on the exact order its beads were threaded in — change even one bead, and the string scrunches up into a slightly different ball, because the sticky spots that pull it into shape are now in different places. And shape is everything: a key only opens a lock if it's exactly the right shape, and a protein only does its job if it's exactly the right shape too.

Two proteins end up different shapes for one underlying reason: they have a different sequence of amino acids — a different primary structure. Because the R groups are now in a different order, the hydrogen, ionic and disulfide bonds that pull the chain into shape form in different places, so the chain folds into a different tertiary structure. This same chain can be extended all the way back to DNA: a change in the DNA base sequence causes a different amino acid sequence, which changes where those bonds form, which changes the tertiary structure — and if that structure no longer fits its job, the protein stops working. Shape is also directly linked to function: an enzyme's tertiary structure creates its active site, while a receptor, antibody or carrier protein's structure creates a binding site instead.

The causal chain

  1. A different sequence of amino acids (a different primary structure)…
  2. …means hydrogen, ionic and disulfide bonds form in different places along the chain…
  3. …giving a different tertiary structure.
⭐

Extended all the way back to a mutation, this becomes: "A change in the DNA base sequence causes a different amino acid sequence / primary structure. This means hydrogen, ionic and disulfide bonds form in different places, giving a different tertiary structure, so the protein no longer works." This exact causal chain is reused across enzyme, receptor, antibody and mutation questions right across the specification — not just here.

▶ 🚩 The two traps in this chain

Never write "a different amino acid formed" for the change — the idea required is a change in sequence, not a brand new amino acid appearing. And never refer to an "active site" where the protein in question is a receptor rather than an enzyme — receptors, antibodies and carrier proteins have binding sites, not active sites. If a question assumes two proteins are already identical in structure, the effect of a different pH or temperature is an accepted alternative answer instead.

Relating structure to a named function

ProteinStructural feature → function
EnzymeTertiary structure creates an active site complementary to a specific substrate; globular and soluble.
HaemoglobinQuaternary structure — four polypeptides, each with a haem group, so it carries four oxygen molecules; binding of the first oxygen changes the shape, making further binding easier.
AntibodyVariable region with a specific tertiary structure, complementary to one antigen; quaternary structure of four chains.

📌

The specification explicitly expects you to be able to relate protein structure to properties named throughout the course — so this content is examined inside membrane, immunity, muscle and transport questions just as often as it is on its own.

Section 5 · spec content point

🧪 The biuret test — detecting a protein

A short, precise test — every mark here comes down to getting the exact colour change right and knowing one very specific rejected step.

📖

What the specification says: "The biuret test for proteins."

1
2
3

Imagine a colour-changing spy test: drip a special blue liquid onto a sample. If the sample is a protein, it goes purple — like the liquid is saying "found one!" If there's no protein there, it just stays blue. Unlike some other food tests, you don't need to heat anything up for this one — it works at room temperature.

To test for a protein, add biuret reagent to the sample at room temperature. If a protein is present, the mixture turns purple / violet / mauve / lilac; if there's no protein, it stays blue. What the test is actually detecting is the peptide bond itself, so it gives a positive result for any protein or polypeptide, including dipeptides and larger. To turn the test into a number rather than just a colour, a colorimeter is used to measure how much light the purple colour absorbs, and that reading is compared against a calibration curve built from solutions of known protein concentration.

  1. Add biuret reagent to the sample (sodium or potassium hydroxide plus copper sulfate solution is an accepted alternative description of the reagent).
  2. A protein present gives a purple / violet / mauve / lilac colour; a non-protein sample stays blue.
📌

What the test actually detects: the peptide bond itself — so it gives a positive result for any protein or polypeptide, including dipeptides upwards, not just large intact proteins.

▶ 🚩 The one instruction never to write

Never write that the biuret test is heated. Unlike Benedict's test for reducing sugars, biuret works at room temperature — describing it as heated is a guaranteed lost mark.

▶ Distinguishing an enzyme from its substrate

An enzyme in a mixture with its (non-protein) substrate turns purple, because it is a protein and contains peptide bonds; the substrate — a sugar, for example — has no peptide bonds, so it stays blue.

Making the test quantitative

Biuret can be extended into a quantitative practical technique: add biuret reagent at room temperature as before, then use a colorimeter to measure the absorbance of the purple colour produced, and compare the reading against a calibration curve built from solutions of known protein concentration — a deeper purple colour corresponds to a higher protein concentration.

▶ 🚩 Colorimeter, never "calorimeter"

Write colorimeter, not "calorimeter" — a calorimeter measures energy changes, a completely different instrument, and the wrong word is explicitly not credited here.

▶ A related required practical skill — chromatography of amino acids

This isn't part of the biuret spec wording itself, but the same practical-skills unit that covers quantifying biuret also covers separating a mixture of amino acids by chromatography, so it's worth having alongside this section: spot the amino-acid mixture onto a pencil origin line, run it in a solvent with the solvent level kept below the origin line, then remove the paper before the solvent reaches the top and spray it with ninhydrin to reveal the otherwise colourless amino-acid spots. Calculate Rf = distance moved by the spot ÷ distance moved by the solvent front, and compare the result against known standards run in the same solvent to identify each spot.

Section 6 · exam technique

🎯 Common mistakes & precise wording

This spec point is genuinely one of the harder ones on the whole AS/A2 course — not because the biology is conceptually difficult, but because so many marks ride on exact terminology and precisely which bond gets named where.

Vague / incorrect versionWhat to say instead
"Amino acids joined together" for primary structureThe sequence / order of amino acids in the polypeptide
"3°" or "4°" as shorthandWrite "tertiary" and "quaternary" out in full, at least once
Any bond named for secondary structure other than hydrogenHydrogen bonds only — between the N–H and C=O groups
"A different amino acid formed" (mutation questions)A different amino acid sequence
"Active site" for a receptor, antibody or carrier protein"Binding site" — active sites belong to enzymes only

▶ Where this difficulty actually comes from

This is one of the highest-density topics on the specification for banned abbreviations and precise definitions per mark. At AS level, the primary-structure recall question is regularly answered incompletely because "sequence/order" gets left out — even though the rest of the definition is usually correct. At A2, quaternary structure is frequently muddled with tertiary, and questions about dipeptides are answered more weakly than expected because many students describe how a dipeptide is formed instead of describing its properties — for example wrongly claiming a dipeptide has two amine groups, or that dipeptides possess primary or secondary structure of their own. Watch out too for confusing the sequence/order of amino acids with a "degenerate code," which is a DNA/mRNA idea from a different topic entirely, not a protein-structure one.

Section 7 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

"The importance of proteins in cells and organisms" is a high-likelihood essay title built almost entirely from this spec point — it also opens essays on receptors, and on nitrogen-containing substances more broadly. Use this content as the opening structural-vocabulary paragraph that every later paragraph then "cashes in."

▶ Model opening paragraph

"The 20 amino acids common to all organisms differ only in their R group; a condensation reaction between the amine group of one and the carboxyl group of another forms a peptide bond, releasing water. Primary structure (amino acid sequence) determines secondary structure (α-helix/β-pleated sheet via hydrogen bonding) and tertiary structure (3-D folding via hydrogen, ionic and disulfide bonds between R groups); quaternary structure involves more than one polypeptide chain. The tertiary structure creates the active site in enzymes, or complementary/specific shapes in antibodies, carrier proteins and receptors — so structure directly determines function."

Whenever a later paragraph needs to explain why something is specific to one molecule — an enzyme to its substrate, an antibody to its antigen, a receptor to its ligand — name the exact level of structure and the bond responsible, rather than gesturing vaguely at "shape." And keep "active site" strictly for enzymes: receptors, antibodies and carrier proteins have binding sites instead.

▶ Where this reappears as a supporting paragraph elsewhere

Enzymes and the active site (tertiary structure, induced fit); haemoglobin's quaternary structure and cooperative oxygen binding; antibody variable regions and quaternary structure; receptor binding sites in cell signalling and muscle contraction. Whenever a later paragraph needs a specific shape to explain specificity, name the level of structure and the bond responsible explicitly, rather than repeating a generic "the shape fits" statement.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Amino acids

  • The only monomer proteins are built from.
  • Central carbon + amine (NH₂) + carboxyl (COOH) + H + R group.
  • The 20 amino acids common to all organisms differ only in the R group — everything else is identical.

2 · The peptide bond

  • Condensation reaction: amine group + carboxyl group join, water is lost.
  • Dipeptide = 2 amino acids; polypeptide = many. A functional protein = 1 or more polypeptides.
  • Hydrolysis reverses it (water used). Endopeptidases (internal) act first, creating more ends for exopeptidases (terminal) and dipeptidases.

3 · The four levels of structure

  • Primary = sequence/order (peptide bonds).
  • Secondary = α-helix/β-pleated sheet (hydrogen bonds only).
  • Tertiary = whole 3-D fold, creates active/binding site (hydrogen, ionic, disulfide, between R groups).
  • Quaternary = 2+ polypeptides (same bonds, between chains). Never write "3°".

4 · Why shape determines function

  • Different sequence → bonds form in different places → different tertiary structure.
  • Extend to DNA: base change → amino acid sequence change → bond positions change → tertiary structure changes → protein may stop working.
  • "Active site" = enzymes only; "binding site" = receptors, antibodies, carrier proteins.
  • Know 5 examples: enzyme, haemoglobin, antibody, collagen, channel/carrier.

5 · The biuret test

  • Add biuret reagent (or NaOH/KOH + copper sulfate) at room temperature — never heat.
  • Protein present → purple/violet/mauve/lilac; none → stays blue.
  • Detects the peptide bond itself, so it's positive for dipeptides upwards.
  • Quantitative version: colorimeter (never "calorimeter") + calibration curve — deeper purple = more protein.
🧠

Primary sequence, secondary shape (H bonds only), tertiary the whole thing folds, quaternary more than one chain joins in. Say every name in full — never "3°".

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Amino acids

❓ Draw and label the general structure of an amino acid, and state what makes the 20 amino acids different from one another.
A central carbon atom bonded to four groups: an amine group (NH₂), a carboxyl group (COOH), a hydrogen atom, and an R group. The twenty amino acids common to all organisms differ only in the R group (side chain) — the other three groups are identical in every one.
❓ Name the functional group shown at the labelled end of an amino acid diagram.
The carboxyl group. "Carboxylic acid" is also accepted.

The peptide bond

❓ Describe how a peptide bond is formed between two amino acids.
A condensation reaction occurs, in which a molecule of water is lost; the amine (NH₂) group of one amino acid reacts with the carboxyl (COOH) group of the other, forming a peptide bond and creating a specific sequence of amino acids. Each point is credited if shown clearly in a labelled diagram.
❓ Explain why endopeptidases act before exopeptidases during protein digestion.
Endopeptidases hydrolyse peptide bonds within the polypeptide chain, whereas exopeptidases can only hydrolyse peptide bonds at the ends of a chain; by cutting the chain internally first, endopeptidases produce many more free ends, giving exopeptidases far more surface area to work on.
❓ State the difference between a dipeptide and a polypeptide, and explain how a functional protein relates to a polypeptide.
A dipeptide is formed by the condensation of two amino acids; a polypeptide is formed by the condensation of many amino acids. A functional protein may be made of one polypeptide chain, or of more than one polypeptide chain associated together.

The four levels of structure

❓ Describe the four levels of protein structure, naming the bond(s) involved at each level.
Primary structure is the sequence/order of amino acids, held by peptide bonds. Secondary structure is the folding of the chain into an α-helix or β-pleated sheet, held by hydrogen bonds between the N–H group of one amino acid and the C=O group of another. Tertiary structure is the 3-D folding of the whole polypeptide, held by hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds and disulfide bridges between R groups, and it determines the active site or binding site. Quaternary structure is two or more polypeptide chains associated together, held by the same interactions, now between the chains.
❓ Give a one-mark definition of quaternary structure.
More than one polypeptide (chain). No further detail about prosthetic groups or the bonds involved is required for this mark.
❓ A student writes "hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds hold the secondary structure together." Correct this statement.
Only hydrogen bonds are involved in secondary structure — specifically between the N–H group of one amino acid and the C=O group of another. Ionic bonds, disulfide bridges and peptide bonds are not credited for secondary structure.

Why shape determines function

❓ Explain why two proteins have different shapes.
The two proteins have a different sequence of amino acids (a different primary structure); this means the hydrogen, ionic and disulfide bonds between R groups form in different places; giving a different tertiary structure.
❓ A mutation changes one base in a gene. Explain how this could result in a non-functional protein.
The change in the DNA base sequence causes a different amino acid sequence (a different primary structure); this alters the positions at which hydrogen, ionic and disulfide bonds form; producing a different tertiary structure; if this changes the shape of the active site or binding site sufficiently, the protein no longer functions correctly.
❓ Explain, in terms of structure, why an antibody is specific to one antigen but a carrier protein is specific to one type of molecule.
In both cases, a specific tertiary structure — held together by hydrogen, ionic and/or disulfide bonds between R groups — produces a binding site with a shape complementary to only one target molecule; the term "active site" is not used here, since neither protein is an enzyme.

The biuret test

❓ Describe how you would carry out the biuret test on a sample and state the results for a positive and a negative test.
Add biuret reagent to the sample at room temperature (sodium or potassium hydroxide plus copper sulfate solution is accepted). If protein is present, the sample turns purple/violet/mauve/lilac; if no protein is present, it remains blue. The test should never be heated.
❓ A sample of glucose solution and a sample of dipeptide solution are both tested with biuret reagent. Predict and explain the result for each.
The glucose solution remains blue, as glucose contains no peptide bonds. The dipeptide solution turns purple/violet/mauve/lilac, because the biuret test detects the peptide bond itself, and a dipeptide contains one.
❓ Explain how the biuret test can be adapted to give a quantitative measure of protein concentration.
A colorimeter is used to measure the absorbance of the purple colour produced; this reading is compared against a calibration curve produced using solutions of known protein concentration, where a deeper purple colour (higher absorbance) corresponds to a higher protein concentration. "Colorimeter" must be used, not "calorimeter."
