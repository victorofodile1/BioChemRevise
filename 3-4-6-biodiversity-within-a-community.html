Biodiversity Within a Community

How to measure how varied a community really is — species richness vs the index of diversity, a full worked calculation, valid random sampling technique, and the genuine two-sided balance between farming and conservation.

⚠️

The single habit that protects the most marks on this spec point: species richness and the index of diversity are not the same measure. Richness only counts the number of different species present; the index of diversity is more informative because it also measures the abundance/number of individuals of each species. And inside the formula, capital N is never "number of species" — it is the total number of organisms of all species, while lower-case n is the number of organisms of each individual species. Swap them, and every downstream figure is wrong.

Section 1 · spec content point

🌍 Biodiversity, habitats and communities

Before richness or the index of diversity mean anything, you need four nested terms — and questions are strict about which one they're actually asking for. Getting the scale wrong (writing "population" when the question means "community") loses the mark even when the rest of the answer is right.

📖

What the specification says: "Biodiversity can relate to a range of habitats, from a small local habitat to the Earth."

1
2
3

Think of these four words like nested boxes. The smallest box is a habitat — just the place one animal or plant lives, like under a particular log. Inside that box, all the individuals of one kind of creature living there together (say, every woodlouse under that log) make up a population. Zoom out, and every different kind of creature and plant living and interacting under that log — the woodlice, the beetles, the moss — together make up the community. And biodiversity is simply "how much variety of life is there" — you can ask that question about one log, one garden, or the whole planet.

  • Habitat = the place
  • Population = one species living there
  • Community = every species living there together
  • Biodiversity = how varied life is, at any of these scales

A habitat is the place an organism lives. A population is all the individuals of one species living in that habitat. A community is all the populations of every species living and interacting together in that habitat. Biodiversity is the variety of living organisms, and it can be measured at any scale — a single small pond, a whole ecosystem, or the entire Earth. The reason this matters is that "richness" and "diversity" only make sense when applied to a community (many species) — they can't apply to a population, because a population is only one species by definition.

TermDefinition — learn the level precisely
HabitatThe place an organism lives.
PopulationAll the individuals of one species living in a habitat.
CommunityAll the populations of all the species living and interacting in a habitat.
BiodiversityThe variety of living organisms — measurable at any scale, from one small pond up to the whole Earth.
▶ 🚩 Trap — the scale word that loses the mark

Species richness and biodiversity are always defined "in a community" (or habitat/ecosystem) — never "in a population." A population is only one species, so on its own it cannot have any richness to measure at all: richness is about counting different species, and a population by definition contains only one.

📌

Why this matters before Section 2: this scale vocabulary is rarely examined as a question on its own. It works as a quiet gatekeeping mark buried inside richness and index-of-diversity questions — get the level wrong and the rest of a correct answer still loses that one mark.

Section 2 · spec content point

🔢 Species richness — what it counts, and what it misses

This is the word-perfect definition question of the spec point — and its stated limitation is exactly what makes the index of diversity, in Sections 3–4, worth learning at all.

📖

What the specification says: "Species richness is a measure of the number of different species in a community."

1
2
3

Imagine a jar of sweets. Species richness is just counting how many different colours of sweet are in the jar — 5 colours means a richness of 5. But that count doesn't tell you anything about how many of each colour there are. A jar could have 5 colours but 96 of them are red and only 1 each of the other 4 — richness alone can't tell you that.

  • Species richness = number of different kinds present
  • It says nothing about how many of each kind there are

Species richness is the number of different species present in a community — it's a simple headcount of "how many types," not "how many individuals." Its big weakness is that it completely ignores abundance (how many individuals of each species there are). A sample could be almost entirely one species and still score a high richness, as long as a handful of individuals from other species are also present. That also means two samples can be ranked the wrong way round if you only compare richness — one might have more different species but far fewer total individuals of most of them.

Definition: species richness is the number of (different) species in a community — "in a habitat/ecosystem/area" is also acceptable for the location. Note that "types" on its own isn't precise enough unless it's clearly qualified as "types of species."

The limitation, in full

  • The number of individuals of each species is not known — richness is blind to abundance entirely.
  • A sample can be almost entirely one species and still score a high richness, because nearly all of the sample could be the same species.
  • Two samples can rank the wrong way round if you judge by richness alone: one sample may have a higher richness but far fewer individuals overall, while another has fewer species but many more individuals of each.
💭

The one-line version worth memorising: "species richness measures only the number of different species; it does not measure the number of individuals." That sentence alone answers most short limitation questions.

The converse — why this makes the index of diversity more useful

An index of diversity beats richness precisely because it also measures the abundance — the number/population size — of each species, since a community may contain many individuals of some species and only a few of others. Section 3 unpacks exactly what that means.

▶ 🚩 Trap — the wrong scale word again

Never write "in a population" for species richness — a population is only one species, so it cannot itself have any richness to measure. And don't write "the total number of species" unqualified when the question is really asking about richness of a single sample or site — be precise about what's being counted.

Section 3 · spec content point

📊 What the index of diversity actually measures

Before the formula in Section 4, get the concept solid: the index of diversity adds exactly one thing that richness can never give you — how evenly individuals are spread between species. Nearly every question on this spec point is a variation on "why is the index more useful than richness?", and the answer is always that one word: abundance.

📖

What the specification says: "An index of diversity describes the relationship between the number of species in a community and the number of individuals in each species."

1
2
3

Picture two playgrounds, each with 24 children. Playground A has 4 different games being played, 6 children on each — it feels wonderfully mixed and varied. Playground B also has 24 children, but 20 of them are crowded round just one game and the other 4 are scattered around a second game — it feels far less varied, even though it technically still has "2 games" (2 species). The index of diversity is a number that captures that "feels more mixed" difference — richness on its own cannot.

The index of diversity describes the relationship between two things at once: how many different species are present, and how the individuals are shared out between them — their abundance, or evenness. A community can have a few dominant species and many rare ones; richness alone cannot detect that pattern, because it only counts how many different species exist, not how the individuals are distributed among them. A higher index value always means greater diversity — more species, a more even spread of individuals, or both together.

Species richness counts only how many different species are present. An index of diversity goes further: it also takes account of how many individuals there are of each species — a community can score low on the index even with a reasonable number of species present, if one or two species dominate the numbers and the rest are rare.

🎯

Hardest application skill on this spec point — justify the index, don't just compute it. Whenever a question asks why the index is "better" or "more useful" than richness, always name the extra thing it measures — abundance/evenness — and give the one-line reason: a community could have a few dominant species and many rare ones, which richness alone cannot detect. Naming "diversity" without saying what the index adds over richness scores nothing.

Section 4 turns this relationship into the actual formula — d = N(N−1) ÷ Σn(n−1) — and works through a full calculation, so keep this idea in mind: a higher d always means more species and/or a more even spread of individuals between them, never one or the other in isolation.

Section 4 · spec content point

🧮 Calculating and interpreting the index of diversity

The formula itself is a mechanical, well-taught skill that most students execute correctly. Almost all of the real difficulty is conceptual: reading N and n the right way round, and knowing what to actually do with the number once it's calculated.

📖

What the specification says: "Calculation of an index of diversity (d) from the formula d = N(N−1) ÷ Σn(n−1), where N = total number of organisms of all species and n = total number of organisms of each species."

1
2
3

Imagine tipping a bag of mixed marbles onto a table. N is simply "how many marbles are on the table altogether," counting every colour together. n is "how many marbles of just one colour," counted one colour at a time. The formula takes those two counts and turns them into a single diversity score — the more colours there are, and the more evenly the marbles are split between colours, the higher that score comes out.

To calculate d: first add up every organism of every species to get N, the grand total. Then, for each species in turn, take its own count (n) and work out n × (n − 1). Add all of those species values together to get Σn(n−1). Finally, divide N(N−1) by that sum. A bigger d means greater diversity — either more species, individuals spread more evenly between them, or both. When comparing two values, state the actual ratio between them, not just "bigger" or "more diverse."

The four-step method

  1. Find N — the total number of organisms of all species, added together.
  2. For each species, work out n(n−1) using its own individual count.
  3. Add every species' n(n−1) value together to get Σn(n−1).
  4. Divide N(N−1) by that sum: d = N(N−1) ÷ Σn(n−1).
▶ 🚩 The single most common misreading

N is never "the number of species" — it is the total number of organisms of all species added together. n is the number of organisms of each individual species. Swap the two, and every downstream figure is wrong, even if the rest of the method is otherwise sound.

N(N−1) = 30 × 29 = 870  ·  d = 870 ÷ 184 = 4.73. Every one of the five species must be included in the Σn(n−1) sum — leaving one out is the most common calculation error.

Why the index beats richness — drawn out

The index of diversity is pulled down by two independent things — fewer species (low richness) and an uneven spread of individuals between them (low evenness) — and a community can score low on either, or both, at once. The two communities below have exactly the same total number of organisms (N = 24) but very different index values.

Each dot is one organism, coloured by species. Both communities have N = 24 organisms total — the left one has 4 species spread perfectly evenly (6 each), while the right one has only 2 species, one of which makes up 20 of the 24 individuals. Richness and evenness pull the index down independently, and a community can score low on either, or both, at once.

Interpreting d, and evaluating claims made from data

  • A higher index value always means greater diversity — more species and/or a more even spread of individuals between them.
  • Comparison questions want the ratio stated explicitly, not just "bigger" — for example, "the index in the canopy is 1.13 times bigger than in the understorey."
  • When evaluating a claim against graph data, take each part of the claim in turn and quote the actual figures — the peak value, the value at a stated time, or the point where one line crosses another. A sensible tolerance is usually allowed when reading a value off a graph.
▶ 🚩 Trap — describing a trend with no figures

A bare description of a trend, with no figures quoted, is not credited. Always name a specific value, with its units or axis label, before making a claim about it.

📌

A limitation worth adding wherever it's relevant: if a graph or data set only shows species richness, remember that the index of diversity was not actually measured — the number of individuals of each species isn't known, so only richness can be judged from that data, not true diversity.

Using statistics alongside diversity data

For a species where the calculated P-value is greater than 0.05, the difference in distribution is probably due to chance. Where P is much smaller — say, below 0.001 — the difference is highly unlikely to be due to chance, because that value is far below the standard 5% (0.05) significance threshold.

▶ 🚩 Trap — a generic "the results are significant"

A bare "the results are significant" is rejected across the whole specification. Always name the specific species being discussed and the direction of the difference (e.g. more/fewer individuals than expected), not just the fact that a difference exists.

Common calculation errors

  • Forgetting to include every species in the Σn(n−1) sum.
  • Using n − 1 on its own, instead of the full n(n − 1) for each species.
  • Rounding too early partway through the working, rather than only at the final answer.

A calculated value that rounds to the accepted figure is usually credited (e.g. 3.81–3.84 all round to 3.8), and a correct final calculation carried through from your own — even if earlier wrong — figures is still credited. This is exactly why showing every step of working matters, not just writing down a final number.

Section 5 · spec content point

🚜 How farming reduces biodiversity

Naming a farming practice on its own is never enough — every mark here is paired to the specific mechanism by which that practice removes species, habitats or food sources.

📖

What the specification says: "Farming techniques reduce biodiversity. The balance between conservation and farming."

1
2
3

If a farmer plants only one type of crop, only the animals that depend on exactly that plant can live there — every other creature that needed a different plant loses its food and has to leave. Pulling out hedges and filling in ponds is like knocking down everyone's houses at once. And spraying chemicals to kill pests can also wipe out the food that other animals further up the chain needed to eat. Farmers can undo some of this damage by planting a mix of things, leaving wild patches, and building new ponds and hedges back.

Farming reduces biodiversity mainly by removing things a wide range of species depend on: growing a single crop species (monoculture) removes the variety of plants other organisms need; clearing hedgerows and woodland removes habitats, food sources and shelter all at once; pesticides and herbicides kill target species directly but also strip away food further up the food chain; and draining wetlands or filling in ponds removes entire habitats outright. Conservation techniques reverse these — keeping and planting hedgerows, leaving uncropped field margins, reducing chemical use, rotating crops instead of growing one continuously, and creating new ponds.

Farming practiceEffect on biodiversity — the mechanism that earns the mark
Monoculture (a single crop species)Removes species/fewer species present — only one plant species is grown, so animals dependent on the others are lost.
Removal of hedgerows and woodlandRemoves habitats/fewer habitats or niches; removes food sources, shelter and breeding sites at the same time.
Pesticides and herbicidesKill pest species and weeds directly, and remove a variety of food sources for organisms further up the food chain.
Drainage of wetland; filling ponds/ditchesRemoves entire habitats outright.
Overgrazing; reseeding; early harvest/silagePrevents wildflowers setting seed; destroys nests before young have fledged.
Inorganic fertiliserFavours a few fast-growing species that outcompete the rest; leaching into waterways leads to eutrophication.
▶ 🚩 Trap — naming the practice without its effect

Simply naming "deforestation" or "removal of hedges," with no stated effect, is not credited. Always say what it actually removes — species, habitats, or food sources — to earn the mark.

Conservation techniques that raise biodiversity

  • Maintain existing hedgerows at the most beneficial size/shape, and plant new ones.
  • Plant native trees on land of low farming value, rather than a non-native monoculture.
  • Leave field margins/uncropped strips, and delay hedge cutting until late winter.
  • Reduce pesticide and inorganic fertiliser use — use organic fertiliser or biological control instead.
  • Use crop rotation, including nitrogen-fixing crops, and intercropping rather than monoculture.
  • Create ponds, and undersow crops with grasses.

Section 6 · spec content point

🧪 Practical skills — valid sampling to measure and compare biodiversity

This is the same specification content point as Sections 5 and 7 — sampling and comparison technique is how "farming reduces biodiversity" and "the balance between conservation and farming" actually get tested as a practical, data-handling skill. In practice you'll be handed raw species-count data and expected to run the four-step calculation from Section 4 unprompted, then interpret the result in a farming, succession, or habitat-comparison scenario.

📖

What the specification says: "Farming techniques reduce biodiversity. The balance between conservation and farming." (Same content point as Sections 5 and 7 — this is its practical/data-skills half: the valid sampling technique needed to generate and compare the numbers those sections discuss.)

1
2
3

Imagine picking where to search for minibeasts fairly — like drawing raffle tickets out of a hat instead of just picking your favourite-looking spot. You lay an imaginary numbered grid over the field, use a random number generator to pick a coordinate, and put your square (quadrat) exactly there — every time. That way nobody can accidentally pick the spots that "look" the most interesting. When you compare two different fields, you also have to search the same-sized area, for the same amount of time, so it's a fair contest.

Random sampling has to be genuinely random, not just "randomly placing" a quadrat by eye or throwing it over your shoulder — both are rejected because there's no reproducible method behind them. The valid method is a numbered grid combined with a random number generator, used to fix the exact coordinates for each quadrat or transect start point. Organisms must be identified without harming them — released unchanged afterwards, using a key or photographs to identify them. For a fair comparison between two sites, keep the sampled area size, the net/mesh size, and the sampling time and day the same in both.

Random sampling, done properly

Use a numbered grid together with a random number generator to fix the coordinates at which each quadrat — or each transect start point — is placed.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "randomly placing" and "throwing" quadrats

"Randomly placing quadrats" with no stated method is not credited. "Throwing quadrats" is specifically flagged as an invalid, non-random method — never write it. Both fail for the same reason: there's no reproducible number-based method behind the placement.

Both methods are valid because the position is fixed by a number, not by judgement or by hand. "Randomly placing" or "throwing" a quadrat is rejected because there's no stated, reproducible method behind it.

Identifying organisms without harming them

  • The method must not harm the organisms, and must allow them to be released unchanged afterwards.
  • It must also allow close examination, or the use of an identification key — using photographs or reference specimens to identify species is acceptable.
▶ A boundary worth knowing

DNA sequencing to identify organisms belongs to a different part of the specification (investigating genetic and species diversity), not to this fieldwork context — don't reach for it here.

Designing and improving a fair sampling comparison

  • For a fair comparison between two sites: keep the same size of area sampled, the same net/mesh size, and the same sampling time, day and time of day.
  • To improve a study: collect at more times of year, giving more points for a better line of best fit; count the number of individuals of each species so an index of diversity can actually be calculated, not just richness; collect from more sites or more years to increase the accuracy of the mean data.
📌

Marking habit worth building: both a suggestion and its explanation are needed for the mark — "sample more sites" alone isn't enough; you must also say why (e.g. to increase the accuracy of the mean data).

Explaining differences in organism numbers between two sites

These questions are marked as paired cause-and-consequence statements — stating a cause with no consequence scores nothing. Learn the pairing pattern, then apply it (or its converse) to whichever site has the higher count:

Cause at the lower-count siteConsequence — must be stated to score
Less food/preySo more organisms starve
Less oxygenSo less respiration/ATP/energy available
More predatorsSo more organisms are killed/eaten/removed
More competitionSo more organisms starve

The converse pairing is accepted for the site with the higher count (e.g. "more food at the second site → so fewer organisms starve").

Section 7 · spec content point

⚖️ Evaluating the farming–conservation balance

Same specification content point as Sections 5–6 again — this is its evaluative half. It's frequently answered as a one-sided description; a genuine evaluation weighs both sides and reaches a justified conclusion.

📖

What the specification says: "Farming techniques reduce biodiversity. The balance between conservation and farming." (Same content point as Sections 5 and 6 — this is the "discuss/evaluate the balance" half.)

1
2
3

Think of it like a seesaw. On one side, farmers need to grow as much food as possible for a growing number of people, and that food needs to be affordable — which pushes them towards growing just one crop and spraying away anything that competes with it. On the other side, nature needs variety to stay healthy and resilient. Push too hard on the farming side, and you risk losing the very insects that pollinate crops, and making crops easier for disease to wipe out. So governments often pay farmers extra money to keep some balance on both sides of the seesaw.

Farming aims to maximise yield from a given area, which favours monoculture, pesticides and removing competing species — all of which reduce biodiversity. Conservation aims to maintain that diversity, which usually means accepting a lower yield or taking land out of production altogether. The tension is real on both sides: a growing population needs affordable food, but reduced biodiversity makes crops more vulnerable to disease and pests, removes pollinators, and loses potentially useful alleles and species for the future. Because conservation measures cost farmers money, subsidies or other financial incentives are often needed to make them viable alongside farming.

Farming aims to maximise yield from a given area, which favours monoculture, pesticides and the removal of competing species — all of which reduce biodiversity. Conservation aims to maintain that diversity, which usually means accepting a lower yield or taking land out of production.

Points for a genuinely balanced answer

  • A growing population needs food, and that food needs to be affordable — which pushes towards higher-yield, lower-diversity farming methods.
  • But reduced biodiversity makes crops more vulnerable to disease and pests, removes pollinators, and loses potentially useful alleles and species that might be needed later.
  • Conservation measures cost farmers money, so subsidies/financial incentives are often needed to make them viable alongside continued farming.
▶ 🚩 Trap — a one-sided "evaluate" answer

Evaluate questions on this trade-off are frequently answered as one-sided descriptions of only farming's impact, or only conservation's benefits. A genuine evaluation weighs evidence for and against, then reaches a justified conclusion — a one-sided answer is capped however much correct biology it contains.

🎯

Second-hardest application skill on this spec point. Treat "evaluate the balance" as an essay-style mini-answer: one clear point for farming's side, one clear point for conservation's side, then a justified conclusion — for example, "…so subsidies are needed to make conservation financially viable alongside farming." A list of correct facts with no conclusion is capped, even if every fact in it is accurate.

The formula itself is a well-taught, mechanical skill most students execute correctly — the real difficulty is almost entirely conceptual and evaluative. Section 9 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

This spec point rarely appears as a single flagship long-answer question on its own — it usually earns marks as a supporting paragraph across several essay titles about biodiversity, habitat loss, or human impact.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around "how do we measure this?"

A community is all the populations of different species interacting within a habitat; species richness and the index of diversity quantify the extent and evenness of these interactions. Species richness alone only counts how many different species are present, but an index of diversity is more informative because it also measures the abundance of each species — a community with a few dominant species and many rare ones can score low on the index even with reasonable richness.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around human/farming impact

Farming practices such as monoculture, hedgerow removal and pesticide use reduce the number of interacting species present in a community by removing habitats and food sources, so human activity of this kind directly reduces measurable biodiversity. Because reduced biodiversity in turn makes crops more vulnerable to disease and pests and removes pollinators, conservation measures — such as maintaining hedgerows, reducing chemical use and creating new habitats — are needed to offset this loss, even though they usually come at some cost to yield.

Use this spec point as the "how do we measure this, and what's damaging it?" paragraph whenever an essay title mentions biodiversity, habitat loss, or human/farming impact: define richness and the index precisely, state that the index is more informative because it captures abundance as well as species count, then link straight into the named farming mechanisms as the concrete cause of whatever loss the title is asking about.

Essay titles this spec point supports

  • "The importance of biodiversity and its conservation"
  • "The causes of the loss of biodiversity and its biological importance"
  • "Human activity has reduced the biodiversity of many habitats. Describe and explain the effects of this and how the loss can be reduced."

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Biodiversity, habitats & communities

  • Habitat = place; population = one species there; community = all species interacting there; biodiversity = variety of life, measurable at any scale.
  • Richness/biodiversity always defined "in a community" — never "in a population" (one species can't have richness).

2 · Species richness

  • Number of different species in a community. Accept habitat/ecosystem/area; reject population.
  • Limitation: says nothing about the number of individuals of each species — a sample could be almost all one species and still score high richness.
  • Index of diversity beats it because it also measures abundance of each species.

3 · What the index of diversity measures

  • Describes the relationship between number of species AND number of individuals of each species — abundance/evenness, not just a headcount.
  • Higher d = more species and/or a more even spread of individuals between them.

4 · Calculating & interpreting d

  • d = N(N−1) ÷ Σn(n−1). N = total organisms, ALL species. n = organisms of EACH species. Never swap them.
  • Method: find N → n(n−1) per species → sum them (Σn(n−1)) → divide N(N−1) by that sum.
  • Comparisons want the ratio stated explicitly ("1.13 times bigger"), and graph claims need a quoted figure, not just a described trend.
  • P > 0.05 = probably chance; P well below 0.05 (e.g. 0.001) = highly unlikely due to chance — always name the species and direction.

5 · How farming reduces biodiversity

  • Monoculture → removes species. Hedgerow/woodland removal → removes habitats/food/shelter. Pesticides/herbicides → kill species + remove food sources up the chain. Draining/filling ponds → removes habitats. Fertiliser → favours fast growers + eutrophication.
  • Always pair the named practice with its mechanism — naming alone scores nothing.
  • Conservation reverses it: hedgerows, native trees, field margins, less pesticide/fertiliser, crop rotation, new ponds.

6 · Practical skills — valid sampling

  • Numbered grid + random number generator fixes quadrat/transect coordinates. "Throwing"/unqualified "randomly placing" is invalid.
  • Identify organisms without harm; release unchanged; use a key/photos (not DNA sequencing here).
  • Fair comparison = same area, mesh size, sampling time/day. Improve a study = more times/sites/years + count individuals per species, each with its reason.
  • Site differences marked as cause→consequence pairs (e.g. less food → more organisms starve) — a cause alone scores nothing.

7 · Farming vs conservation — the balance

  • Farming maximises yield (favours monoculture/pesticides); conservation maintains diversity (usually lowers yield).
  • Balanced answer: growing population needs affordable food, but lost biodiversity risks disease/pest vulnerability, lost pollinators, lost alleles/species.
  • Subsidies often needed to make conservation viable. One-sided "evaluate" answers are capped — always reach a justified conclusion.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise, dense wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Biodiversity, habitats & communities

❓ Distinguish between a population and a community, and explain why species richness cannot be measured for a population.
A population is all the individuals of one species living in a habitat; a community is all the populations of all the species living and interacting in a habitat. Species richness measures the number of different species present, so it requires more than one species to be meaningful — a population, by definition, contains only one species, so it cannot itself have any richness to measure.
❓ Explain what is meant by "biodiversity can relate to a range of habitats, from a small local habitat to the Earth."
Biodiversity is the variety of living organisms, and it can be measured and compared at any scale — from a single small habitat such as a pond or hedgerow, up through a whole ecosystem, to the entire planet.

Species richness

❓ Define species richness.
The number of (different) species in a community. Accept "in a habitat/ecosystem/area" for the location. Reject "in a population."
❓ Explain one limitation of using species richness alone to compare the diversity of two communities.
The number of individuals of each species is not known — a sample could be almost entirely one species and still score a high richness, so two samples can be ranked the wrong way round if compared on richness alone.
❓ A student claims that species richness and the index of diversity always give the same result when comparing two communities. Evaluate this claim.
This is not correct: species richness only counts the number of different species present, while the index of diversity also takes account of the number of individuals of each species (abundance/evenness); two communities can have identical richness but very different index values if individuals are distributed unevenly between species in one of them.

What the index of diversity measures

❓ Explain why an index of diversity is considered more useful than species richness.
An index of diversity also measures the abundance/number of individuals of each species, since a community may have many individuals of some species and few of others; species richness alone cannot detect this pattern, because it only counts how many different species are present.
❓ Two communities have the same species richness. Explain how their index-of-diversity values could still differ.
The index of diversity depends on both the number of species and how evenly individuals are distributed between them; a community where individuals are spread evenly across species will have a higher index than one with the same richness but dominated by one or two species.

Calculating & interpreting d

❓ State the formula for the index of diversity, defining each term.
d = N(N−1) ÷ Σn(n−1), where N is the total number of organisms of all species, and n is the number of organisms of each individual species.
❓ A sample contains three species with counts of 6, 4 and 2 individuals. Calculate the index of diversity, showing your working.
N = 6 + 4 + 2 = 12. n(n−1): 6×5 = 30, 4×3 = 12, 2×1 = 2; Σn(n−1) = 30 + 12 + 2 = 44. N(N−1) = 12 × 11 = 132. d = 132 ÷ 44 = 3.0.
❓ A student states "the woodland has a higher diversity than the field because the graph shows a bigger number." Improve this answer.
Quote the actual index values from the graph/axis for both sites and state the ratio explicitly (e.g. the woodland's index is a stated number of times bigger than the field's), rather than a bare description of "bigger," since an unsupported description with no figures is not credited.

How farming reduces biodiversity

❓ Explain how converting a mixed hedgerow-bordered field to a single-crop monoculture reduces biodiversity.
Growing a single crop species removes the range of plant species previously present, so animals dependent on those other plant species for food or shelter are lost; removing the hedgerow also removes habitats, food sources and breeding/shelter sites for the organisms that depended on it.
❓ Explain how the use of inorganic fertiliser can reduce biodiversity.
Inorganic fertiliser favours a few fast-growing species, which outcompete slower-growing species for light and space; leaching of excess fertiliser into waterways can also cause eutrophication, reducing biodiversity in aquatic habitats.
❓ Suggest two conservation techniques a farmer could use to increase biodiversity without stopping crop production entirely.
Leave uncropped field margins/strips around fields, which provide habitat and food sources without removing land from the main crop; use crop rotation including nitrogen-fixing crops rather than continuous monoculture, which maintains a greater variety of plant species over time.

Practical skills — valid sampling

❓ Describe a valid method for randomly positioning quadrats to sample a field.
Lay a numbered grid over the field, then use a random number generator to obtain a pair of coordinates; place the quadrat at the coordinate generated, and repeat for the required number of samples. This is valid because the position is determined by a reproducible, unbiased method rather than by judgement.
❓ Suggest one way to improve a biodiversity survey that only counted species richness, and explain why it would help.
Count the number of individuals of each species present, not just the number of different species; this would allow an index of diversity to be calculated, giving a more informative measure of diversity than richness alone.
❓ Site 1 has fewer individuals of a predatory beetle species than Site 2. Suggest and explain one possible reason.
There may be less food/prey available at Site 1, so more of the beetles starve; alternatively, there may be more predators of the beetle at Site 1, so more individuals are killed/eaten/removed.

Farming vs conservation — the balance

❓ Evaluate the statement: "Farmers should always prioritise conservation over yield."
Prioritising conservation would raise biodiversity, reducing the risk of disease/pest damage and preserving pollinators and potentially useful alleles/species; however, it would likely lower yield, and a growing population needs affordable food, so farmers may be unable to afford this without financial support. A balanced conclusion should note that subsidies/incentives are usually needed to make conservation financially viable alongside continued farming, rather than treating one side as always correct.
❓ Explain why reduced biodiversity on farmland can ultimately harm farming itself.
Reduced biodiversity removes pollinating insects needed for some crops, makes the remaining crop species more vulnerable to disease and pest outbreaks because there is less genetic and species variety to buffer against them, and loses potentially useful alleles or species that might otherwise be a future resource.
