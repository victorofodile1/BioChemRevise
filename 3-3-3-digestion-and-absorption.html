Digestion and Absorption

How mammals hydrolyse starch, lipid and protein into absorbable monomers, and exactly how the ileum epithelium gets those monomers into the blood — including the single most heavily-tested distinction on this page: which step of absorption is genuinely active transport, and which steps only look like it.

⚠️

The one habit that protects the most marks on this topic: every digestion step needs its correct verb and correct bond named — "breaks down starch" scores nothing where hydrolyses glycosidic bonds is required, and bile salts must never be described as digesting or hydrolysing anything, because bile salts are not enzymes. In absorption, only one step is genuine active transport — the basal Na⁺/K⁺ pump. Sodium re-entering through the co-transporter, and the glucose or amino acid riding in with it, are both driven by facilitated diffusion down a gradient, never by active transport at that step.

Section 1 · spec content point

🧩 Digestion: the big idea

Before any of the three named pathways, one single idea underpins the whole topic: molecules only cross a membrane if they're small and soluble enough, so anything too big has to be taken apart first. Everything else in this spec point is really just the details of how.

📖

What the specification says: "During digestion, large biological molecules are hydrolysed to smaller molecules that can be absorbed across cell membranes."

1
2
3

Cell membranes only let small things through the front door. Starch, fat and protein are all huge — far too big to fit — so before your gut can absorb any of them, it has to chop them up into much smaller pieces, using water to help snap them apart. Once the pieces are small enough, they can finally get through.

  • Hydrolysis = "water-splitting" — using a water molecule to break a bond apart
  • Polymer = a big molecule made of lots of repeating small units joined together
  • Monomer = one single small unit on its own
  • Soluble = able to dissolve, so it can travel in watery fluid like blood

Starch, triglycerides and proteins are all large, insoluble polymers — none of them can cross a cell-surface membrane as they are. Digestion is the process of hydrolysing (breaking apart using water) each polymer into small, soluble monomers or near-monomers: glucose from starch, fatty acids and monoglycerides from lipid, and amino acids from protein. Only once a molecule is small and soluble enough can it actually be absorbed into the blood.

MacromoleculeFinal absorbable product
Carbohydrate (starch)Glucose
Lipid (triglyceride)Fatty acids + monoglycerides
ProteinAmino acids

All three digestion pathways look different on the surface, but they run on the same underlying logic. Large biological molecules — starch, triglycerides, polypeptides — are polymers or large molecules that are both too big and, in most cases, insoluble, so they cannot cross the phospholipid bilayer of an epithelial cell, whether by diffusion or via a carrier/channel protein. Hydrolysis reactions, each catalysed by a specific enzyme, break the relevant bond (glycosidic, ester or peptide) using a water molecule, producing smaller, soluble molecules that either diffuse directly across the membrane or are transported in by a specific protein.

MacromoleculeEnzyme(s) & broad siteBond hydrolysedFinal absorbable product
CarbohydrateAmylase (salivary glands, pancreas); membrane-bound maltase on the ileum epitheliumGlycosidic bondsGlucose (a monosaccharide)
LipidBile salts (liver/gallbladder — emulsify, not an enzyme); lipase (pancreas)Ester bondsFatty acids + monoglycerides, delivered in micelles
ProteinEndopeptidases (e.g. pepsin, trypsin); exopeptidases; membrane-bound dipeptidases on the ileum epitheliumPeptide bondsAmino acids

Notice the shared design: in every pathway, the very last hydrolysis step happens either at a membrane-bound enzyme sitting directly on the ileum epithelium (maltase, dipeptidases), or — for lipid — inside a micelle delivered right up to that same membrane. That's not a coincidence: it keeps the local concentration of the finished monomer high at exactly the point where it needs to be absorbed, which matters for diffusion and even more for the co-transport mechanism covered in Section 5.

Every pathway ends the same way: a membrane-bound enzyme (green boxes) or a membrane-delivered structure (the micelle) hands the final monomer straight to the ileum epithelium.
▶ 🚩 Trap — "breaks down" is not "hydrolyses"

Across every pathway on this spec point, "breaks down starch/fat/protein" is treated as a vague, low-credit phrase. The words that actually score are hydrolysis (or "hydrolyses") plus the specific bond being broken — glycosidic, ester or peptide. Write both, every time, even when it feels repetitive.

Section 2 · spec content point

🌾 Carbohydrate digestion — starch to glucose

Two hydrolysis steps, two enzymes, one bond type throughout — but the location of the second step is the part most students under-explain.

📖

What the specification says: "Digestion in mammals of carbohydrates by amylases and membrane-bound disaccharidases."

1
2
3

Starch is like a long chain of paper clips all linked together. First, a pair of scissors called amylase cuts the chain into short two-clip pieces (maltose). Then a second, tinier pair of scissors — fixed right at the gut wall itself — snips each pair in half into single clips (glucose), right at the doorway where they're about to be absorbed.

  • Amylase = the first pair of scissors, made in your mouth and pancreas
  • Maltase = the second pair of scissors, fixed to the gut wall itself
  • Glycosidic bond = the "link" joining sugar units together

Starch digestion happens in two stages. First, amylase — made by the salivary glands and the pancreas — hydrolyses the glycosidic bonds holding starch together, producing the shorter sugar maltose. Second, maltase finishes the job: it hydrolyses the glycosidic bond in maltose to give glucose. The key detail is where maltase is — it's fixed onto the cell membrane of the cells lining the ileum, not floating free in the gut fluid, so glucose appears right where it's about to be absorbed.

  1. Amylase, produced by the salivary glands and the pancreas, hydrolyses the glycosidic bonds in starch, producing maltose.
  2. Membrane-bound maltase, fixed in the cell-surface membrane of the ileum epithelium, hydrolyses the glycosidic bond in maltose, producing glucose — which is then absorbed.

Maltase isn't the only membrane-bound disaccharidase on the ileum epithelium — sucrase and lactase sit alongside it, each hydrolysing a different disaccharide:

EnzymeSubstrateProducts
MaltaseMaltoseGlucose + glucose
SucraseSucroseGlucose + fructose
LactaseLactoseGlucose + galactose

Why starch has to be digested before it can be absorbed

Starch is a large, insoluble polymer, so it cannot cross the epithelial cell membrane — it's simply too big, and it isn't soluble enough to be transported in the blood either. Its hydrolysis products, maltose and then glucose, are small and soluble, so they can be absorbed — glucose, ultimately, via the co-transport mechanism covered in Section 5.

Why maltase is membrane-bound rather than free in solution

Because maltase sits fixed in the ileum epithelium's membrane, glucose is produced right at the point of absorption, keeping the local glucose concentration high exactly where the co-transporter needs a steep gradient to exploit. Digestion and absorption are physically coupled at this final step, rather than happening in two separate places.

🩺

This is exactly the mechanism that fails in lactose intolerance: without functional lactase, lactose is never hydrolysed, so it stays in the gut lumen instead of being absorbed — see Section 7 for the full reasoning chain this leads to.

▶ 🚩 Trap — naming the wrong bond, or no bond at all

"Amylase breaks down starch" on its own scores nothing at full A-level standard — "hydrolysis" and "glycosidic bonds" are both independently creditable, not optional extras. And in a carbohydrate-digestion answer, never write "peptide bond" — that belongs to protein digestion only (Section 4).

Section 3 · spec content point

💧 Lipid digestion — bile salts, lipase and micelles

Three distinct jobs happen here, done by two completely different things — never let them blur into one process.

📖

What the specification says: "Digestion in mammals of lipids by lipase, including the action of bile salts."

1
2
3

Fat in your gut starts as one big greasy blob. Bile salts act like washing-up liquid — they don't chemically change the fat at all, they just break the big blob into lots of tiny droplets, so there's much more surface for the next step to work on. Then an enzyme called lipase — a real pair of chemical scissors — actually cuts the fat molecules apart. The pieces then hitch a ride inside tiny transport bubbles called micelles, which ferry them over to the gut wall to be absorbed.

  • Emulsify = break into tiny droplets, without any chemical change (that's bile salts' job)
  • Hydrolyse = actually cut a bond apart (that's lipase's job)
  • Micelle = a tiny ferry that carries fat pieces across the watery gut fluid

Lipid digestion is three separate steps, and it's important not to mix them up. First, bile salts — made in the liver, stored in the gallbladder — emulsify lipid into small droplets; this is purely physical, increasing surface area, and bile salts are not enzymes and never hydrolyse anything. Second, lipase, from the pancreas, hydrolyses the ester bonds in triglycerides, producing fatty acids and monoglycerides. Third, and only after that hydrolysis has happened, those fatty acids and monoglycerides combine with bile salts to form micelles, which transport them to the ileum epithelium.

  1. Bile salts emulsify lipid into small droplets, increasing the surface area of lipid exposed to lipase.
  2. Lipase (produced by the pancreas) hydrolyses the ester bonds in triglycerides, producing fatty acids and monoglycerides.
  3. Fatty acids and monoglycerides, together with bile salts, form micelles, which transport them to the ileum epithelium for absorption (see Section 6).

Why lipid digestion products need no carrier protein to enter the epithelial cell

Fatty acids and monoglycerides are non-polar and lipid-soluble, so they can diffuse directly through the phospholipid bilayer of the epithelial cell membrane — no carrier or channel protein is required, unlike glucose or amino acids.

What happens after lipid products are absorbed

Inside the epithelial cell, fatty acids and monoglycerides are recombined into triglycerides in the smooth endoplasmic reticulum, then packaged into chylomicrons by the Golgi apparatus, and leave the cell by exocytosis into the lacteals (part of the lymphatic system) — not directly into the blood capillaries, unlike glucose and amino acids.

▶ 🚩 The single costliest mix-up on this content point

Confusing emulsification (bile salts, producing droplets, before lipase acts) with micelle formation (happening afterwards, from the digestion products, to enable absorption) is treated as the single most-penalised error here. Bile salts emulsify — they never digest, break down, or hydrolyse lipid, because they are not enzymes. And micelles do not increase surface area for lipase; that's emulsification's job — micelles exist purely to transport the finished products to the membrane.

Section 4 · spec content point

🧬 Protein digestion — three proteases, three jobs

Three enzyme classes, distinguished by exactly where along the chain they cut — not by whether they work inside or outside a cell.

📖

What the specification says: "Digestion in mammals of proteins by endopeptidases, exopeptidases and membrane-bound dipeptidases."

1
2
3

A protein is like a long necklace of beads. Endopeptidases cut the necklace somewhere in the middle, making lots of shorter necklaces. Exopeptidases then nibble beads off the very ends of those shorter necklaces, two at a time. Finally, dipeptidases — fixed right at the gut wall — snip that very last pair of beads apart into single beads, right where they're about to be absorbed.

  • Endo- = "inside" — cuts within the chain
  • Exo- = "outside/end" — cuts at the ends of the chain
  • Peptide bond = the link joining amino acids together

All three enzyme types hydrolyse peptide bonds — the difference is purely about position along the chain, not location relative to a cell. Endopeptidases cut peptide bonds in the middle of a polypeptide, creating lots of new chain ends. Exopeptidases then remove amino acids one at a time from those ends, producing dipeptides. Dipeptidases, fixed to the ileum epithelium's membrane, finish the job by hydrolysing the last peptide bond in each dipeptide, releasing single amino acids exactly where they're absorbed.

Enzyme classWhere it actsEffect
EndopeptidasesHydrolyse peptide bonds within the polypeptide chain, at internal, non-terminal positionsCreates many new chain ends, giving exopeptidases far more sites to work on
ExopeptidasesHydrolyse peptide bonds at the terminal (end) residues of a peptide chainRemoves amino acids one at a time from the ends, producing dipeptides
DipeptidasesHydrolyse the final peptide bond in a dipeptide; membrane-bound on the ileum epitheliumProduces single amino acids at the point of absorption

Why endopeptidases act before exopeptidases

Endopeptidases hydrolyse bonds within the chain, which increases the number of chain ends available. Because exopeptidases can only act at ends, this gives them many more sites to work on simultaneously, increasing the overall rate of protein hydrolysis compared with exopeptidases acting alone on an intact polypeptide.

The named enzymes, their sites, and why pepsin stops working in the small intestine

  • Pepsin is an endopeptidase secreted in the stomach, active at the stomach's low pH.
  • Trypsin and chymotrypsin are endopeptidases secreted by the pancreas, active at the neutral/slightly alkaline pH of the small intestine.
  • Exopeptidases and dipeptidases act at the ileum epithelium.

When food leaves the acidic stomach and enters the small intestine, the higher pH denatures pepsin — it disrupts the bonds maintaining pepsin's tertiary structure, changing its active site shape so its substrate no longer binds.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "endo- outside cells, exo- inside cells"

A well-documented misconception is believing that endopeptidases work outside cells and exopeptidases inside cells. That's not the distinction at all — endo-/exo- describes position along the polypeptide chain (internal versus terminal), nothing to do with location relative to a cell.

Section 5 · spec content point

⚡ Co-transport mechanisms for amino acid and monosaccharide absorption

The most heavily-tested mechanism on this whole spec point. The hardest skill isn't describing the sequence — it's correctly labelling each individual step as active or passive.

📖

What the specification says: "Mechanisms for the absorption of the products of digestion by cells lining the ileum of mammals, to include: co-transport mechanisms for the absorption of amino acids and of monosaccharides."

1
2
3

Imagine a bucket-and-slope system. A pump at the bottom (needing energy) constantly empties a bucket of sodium out of the cell — that's the only bit that costs energy. That creates a slope: sodium now really "wants" to flow back in. As it flows back downhill through a special doorway, it drags a passenger — glucose or an amino acid — in with it, like a rope tow pulling a skier uphill using someone else running downhill. Finally, the passenger walks out the other side of the cell into the blood through its own separate doorway, again just flowing downhill.

  • The pump = the only part that spends energy (ATP) directly
  • Everything else = just flowing downhill (diffusion), even though the passenger is being dragged "uphill" against its own gradient

A sodium-potassium pump on one side of the epithelial cell constantly pumps sodium out using ATP, which keeps the sodium concentration inside the cell low. Sodium then flows back in down that gradient through a co-transporter protein — and this same protein drags glucose or an amino acid in with it, even though the glucose or amino acid is moving against its own concentration gradient. Only the pump itself directly spends ATP; sodium re-entering and the passenger molecule being dragged in are both driven by the sodium gradient, not by ATP at that specific step. Glucose then leaves the cell into the blood through a separate carrier protein, moving down its own gradient.

Click a numbered dot, or use Next / Back above, to step through the co-transport mechanism.
▶ 🚩 The most heavily-policed distinction on this spec point

Only one step directly uses ATP: the basal Na⁺/K⁺ pump. Everything else in the chain is passive, moving down a concentration gradient — including sodium re-entering the cell, and even the glucose or amino acid being co-transported with it. That solute moves against its own gradient only because it's dragged in by the sodium gradient, which makes this indirect (secondary) active transport of the solute — powered entirely by the ATP spent earlier at the pump, not by ATP at the co-transporter itself. Never write "glucose is actively transported" for this step, and never write "sodium is actively transported into the cell from the lumen" — the pump only ever moves sodium out of the cell.

The four-step recall sequence

  1. The sodium–potassium pump, in the basal membrane, actively transports Na⁺ out of the epithelial cell into the blood, using ATP.
  2. This maintains a low Na⁺ concentration inside the cell relative to the gut lumen — a steep concentration gradient for Na⁺, directed into the cell.
  3. Na⁺ diffuses back into the cell down this gradient, through a co-transporter protein in the apical (microvillus) membrane; this protein simultaneously carries glucose or an amino acid into the cell with it, against that solute's own concentration gradient.
  4. Glucose (or the amino acid) then passes out of the cell into the blood across the basal membrane by facilitated diffusion through a separate carrier protein (GLUT), down its own concentration gradient.
🧭

How to tackle the "active or passive" labelling task: before writing prose, label each of the three proteins (pump, co-transporter, GLUT carrier) with "active" or "passive" and name its membrane (basal vs apical). Never write "actively transported" for glucose or an amino acid in this context — write "co-transported" or "carried in by facilitated diffusion coupled to the Na⁺ gradient."

Section 6 · spec content point

🫧 The role of micelles in lipid absorption

Fatty acids and monoglycerides are not soluble on their own — micelles are the ferry that gets them across the watery gut contents to the membrane, where they finally can cross without any transport protein at all.

📖

What the specification says: "Mechanisms for the absorption of the products of digestion by cells lining the ileum of mammals, to include: … the role of micelles in the absorption of lipids."

1
2
3

Fatty acids don't dissolve in watery gut fluid any better than oil dissolves in water — so on their own they'd just float uselessly. A micelle is like a tiny lifeboat: it wraps the oily bits safely inside, with a "waterproof" outer layer facing outward, so the whole boat can float and travel through the watery gut fluid all the way to the gut wall. Once it arrives, the boat "unloads its passengers" and they slip straight through the gut wall on their own.

Fatty acids and monoglycerides are non-polar, so they don't dissolve well in the watery fluid of the gut lumen. Micelles solve this: they're small, soluble structures with the fatty acids and monoglycerides tucked inside (non-polar core) and bile salts/phospholipids facing outward (polar surface), which lets the whole structure dissolve and move through the gut contents. Micelles continually break down and re-form as they travel, delivering fatty acids and monoglycerides to the epithelial cell surface at a high local concentration, where the lipid products then diffuse straight through the membrane on their own.

Micelles are small, soluble structures with fatty acids and monoglycerides oriented tails-inward (forming a non-polar core) and bile-salt/phospholipid heads outward (a polar surface), which lets them dissolve and move through the watery gut contents — something the lipid-digestion products alone cannot do.

Micelles transport fatty acids and monoglycerides to the epithelial cell surface, continually breaking down and re-forming as they travel, so the lipid products are delivered to the membrane at a high effective local concentration. Once there, the fatty acids and monoglycerides diffuse directly through the phospholipid bilayer — no carrier protein is needed, because they are non-polar and lipid-soluble.

📌

Micelles are a transport structure, not a digestion step and not a surface-area step. They form only after lipase has already produced fatty acids and monoglycerides (Section 3) — never describe bile salts as forming micelles directly from fat, and never credit micelles with increasing surface area for lipase.

▶ Why this matters for the co-transport comparison in Section 5

Lipid absorption needs no membrane protein at all — a genuinely different route from glucose and amino acids, which absolutely require a specific carrier or co-transporter protein. If a question asks you to compare how the three types of digestion product are absorbed, this contrast (simple diffusion through the bilayer vs co-transport via a named protein) is exactly the kind of synoptic point that's rewarded.

Section 7 · applying the mechanism

🏗️ The ileum built for absorption — structure, adaptations & disorders

The co-transport mechanism in Section 5 doesn't work in isolation — it depends on a whole set of structural features of the ileum wall. This section pulls those together, then shows how the same reasoning chain explains what goes wrong in a named disorder.

Structural adaptations of the ileum for absorption

  • Villi and microvilli — hugely increase surface area (villi at tissue level, microvilli at cell level).
  • Single-cell-thick epithelium — minimises diffusion distance.
  • Rich blood supply — maintains a steep concentration gradient by constantly removing absorbed products.
  • Many mitochondria in epithelial cells — supply the ATP for active transport.
  • Muscle in the gut wall — mixes food and moves it along, maintaining contact with the epithelium.
  • Lacteals in each villus — absorb the products of lipid digestion into the lymphatic system.
Villi increase surface area at tissue level; microvilli increase it further at cell level. The capillary network carries away absorbed glucose/amino acids; the lacteal, not the capillary, takes up the products of lipid digestion (as chylomicrons).

Applying the absorption chain to a disorder — e.g. lactose intolerance

This is a reasoning chain worth being able to apply to any unfamiliar disorder, not just memorise for one:

  • If a disaccharidase or a co-transporter is missing or non-functional (e.g. lactase deficiency), the substrate is not hydrolysed / not absorbed, and remains in the gut lumen.
  • This lowers the water potential of the lumen contents (more solute is present), so water is not absorbed from the gut — or moves into the lumen by osmosis, down its own water potential gradient — causing diarrhoea.

The same reasoning chain — substrate not hydrolysed/transported → product remains in lumen → water potential falls → water retained or enters the lumen → diarrhoea — applies to cholera, where a toxin instead disrupts ion transport across the epithelium, again lowering the water potential of the lumen and drawing water in by osmosis.

🧪

Practical-skills link: amylase, lipase and protease investigations are a classic vehicle for designing a controlled enzyme-rate experiment (e.g. pH or temperature against amylase activity using the iodine test for starch disappearance, or lipase activity tracked via the pH fall as fatty acids are released). Separately, Visking (dialysis) tubing models a partially permeable membrane: a mixture such as starch + glucose solution is sealed inside tubing suspended in water, and the surrounding water is tested over time to show that small, soluble molecules (glucose) diffuse out while large molecules (starch) cannot — the same size/solubility logic that runs through every section above, and exactly why digestion must precede absorption.

🎯 Common mistakes & precise wording

This spec point isn't conceptually the hardest in the course, but it's dense with easily-confused terminology and one very heavily-policed mechanistic distinction. Three parallel digestion pathways with distinct enzymes, sites and named bonds have to be kept separate under pressure, and the co-transport mechanism has to be re-applied correctly with the right membrane and the right protein named at each step.

▶ Where students genuinely lose marks

Protein digestion tends to be weaker than carbohydrate or lipid digestion — many answers describe digestion "in general terms" and mistakenly bring in lipase or a generic carbohydrase when the question specifically asks about protein-digesting enzymes. Co-transport of amino acids and monosaccharides is well known at a surface level, but many answers default to describing simple active transport or plain facilitated diffusion instead of explicitly explaining the role of the sodium-ion gradient. Micelle formation is a nationally weak topic — answers often confuse it with emulsification, and few correctly describe micelles as transporting fatty acids and monoglycerides to the ileum epithelium rather than helping lipase act.

Section 9 · exam technique

✍️ Using this content in longer written answers

This spec point works well as a supporting paragraph inside a longer essay-style answer on diffusion or transport — the trick, as always, is closing with a sentence that ties the mechanism back to whatever the title is actually about.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around diffusion

Lipid-soluble products of lipid digestion — fatty acids and monoglycerides — are absorbed into epithelial cells by simple diffusion through the phospholipid bilayer; they need no carrier protein. Glucose, by contrast, moves into the blood from the epithelial cell by facilitated diffusion through a carrier protein, after first being co-transported into the cell against its own gradient. Even where active transport (the basal Na⁺/K⁺ pump) sets up the gradient that drives the whole system, the final movement of every absorbed nutrient into the blood is, in the end, diffusion or facilitated diffusion — a strong synoptic point when the essay title is about the importance of diffusion in organisms.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around surface area and enzyme location

Digestion and absorption are physically coupled at the ileum epithelium: membrane-bound enzymes such as maltase and the dipeptidases produce their final monomer directly at the cell surface, keeping the local concentration of that monomer high exactly where transport proteins need a steep gradient to exploit. This illustrates a more general biological theme — that the site of an enzyme-controlled reaction is often optimised to sit as close as possible to where its product is next needed, minimising any diffusion distance between one process and the next.

Where 3.3.3 reappears in the rest of the specification

  • Transport across cell membranes — the co-transport mechanism is first introduced there in general terms; the ileum epithelial cell is the worked example reused directly here.
  • Inorganic ions — sodium-ion context feeds directly into the sodium-gradient mechanism examined here.
  • Mass transport in animals — the glucose, amino acids and lipid-derived products absorbed here are subsequently transported around the body by the blood, e.g. as chylomicrons and dissolved glucose/amino acids.

Rule of thumb: whenever a later question anywhere in the course needs a nutrient to get from the gut into the blood, it is, underneath, still testing the digestion pathways and the co-transport/diffusion logic named on this page.

Section 10

🧷 Memory hook & final checklist

🧠

Memory hook — "one pump, one job." Across the whole absorption mechanism, there's exactly one active-transport step: the basal Na⁺/K⁺ pump, spending ATP to push Na⁺ out. Everything else — Na⁺ back in, glucose or an amino acid riding with it, glucose out into the blood — is diffusion or facilitated diffusion, riding on the gradient that one pump created. And for digestion: bile salts emulsify, lipase hydrolyses, micelles transport — three different jobs, three different structures, never the same word for two of them.

Self-check before moving on

  • I can describe all three digestion pathways (carbohydrate, lipid, protein), naming the correct enzyme(s), site, bond hydrolysed and final product for each.
  • I can distinguish endopeptidases, exopeptidases and membrane-bound dipeptidases by where each acts in the peptide chain, not by "inside/outside the cell."
  • I can distinguish emulsification (bile salts, produces droplets) from micelle formation (happens after lipase, transports fatty acids/monoglycerides to the epithelium) without merging the two.
  • I can narrate the sodium-gradient co-transport mechanism in the correct order, naming each protein and membrane, and state that only the basal Na⁺/K⁺ pump is active transport.
  • I would never write "glucose is actively transported" or "sodium is actively transported into the cell from the lumen" in a co-transport answer.
  • I can apply the digestion/absorption chain to explain a named disorder (e.g. lactose intolerance, cholera) via the water-potential/diarrhoea reasoning chain.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Digestion: the big idea

  • Large biological molecules are hydrolysed into smaller, soluble molecules that can cross a cell membrane.
  • Always name the specific bond: glycosidic (carbs), ester (lipids), peptide (proteins) — "breaks down" alone scores nothing.
  • The final hydrolysis step in every pathway happens right at the ileum epithelium (membrane-bound enzyme, or micelle delivery), keeping the product concentration high at the point of absorption.

2 · Carbohydrate digestion

  • Amylase (salivary glands + pancreas) hydrolyses glycosidic bonds in starch → maltose.
  • Membrane-bound maltase on the ileum epithelium hydrolyses maltose → glucose, right at the point of absorption.
  • Sucrase and lactase are the other membrane-bound disaccharidases — sucrose → glucose + fructose; lactose → glucose + galactose.
  • Starch is too large and insoluble to be absorbed directly — that's why it must be digested first.

3 · Lipid digestion

  • Bile salts emulsify lipid into droplets (↑ surface area) — not enzymes, never hydrolyse anything.
  • Lipase (pancreas) hydrolyses ester bonds in triglycerides → fatty acids + monoglycerides.
  • These, with bile salts, form micelles — formed after lipase acts, never confuse with emulsification.
  • Fatty acids/monoglycerides need no carrier protein — non-polar, diffuse straight through the bilayer.

4 · Protein digestion

  • Endopeptidases (e.g. pepsin — stomach; trypsin/chymotrypsin — pancreas) cut peptide bonds within the chain.
  • Exopeptidases remove amino acids from the chain ends → dipeptides.
  • Membrane-bound dipeptidases on the ileum epithelium hydrolyse the final peptide bond → amino acids.
  • Endo-/exo- = position in the chain, NOT inside/outside a cell.

5 · Co-transport absorption

  • Basal Na⁺/K⁺ pump actively pumps Na⁺ out using ATP — the ONLY active-transport step.
  • Low internal Na⁺ → Na⁺ diffuses back in via the apical co-transporter, dragging glucose/amino acid in too (indirect active transport of the solute).
  • Glucose/amino acid then leaves into the blood via GLUT carrier, by facilitated diffusion.
  • Never say "glucose is actively transported" or "sodium is actively transported into the cell."

6 · Micelles & lipid absorption

  • Micelles = soluble structures, non-polar tails inward, polar bile-salt/phospholipid heads outward.
  • Transport fatty acids/monoglycerides through watery gut fluid to the epithelium, at high local concentration.
  • At the membrane, lipid products diffuse straight through — no transport protein needed.
🧠

One pump, one job. Only the basal Na⁺/K⁺ pump is active transport. Bile salts emulsify, lipase hydrolyses, micelles transport — three different jobs, never the same word twice.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Digestion: the big idea

❓ Explain why a large biological molecule such as starch cannot be absorbed directly by cells lining the ileum.
Starch is a large, insoluble polymer, so it cannot cross the phospholipid bilayer of the epithelial cell membrane, either by diffusion or via a carrier/channel protein. It must first be hydrolysed into smaller, soluble molecules before it can be absorbed.
❓ A student writes "digestion breaks down food so it can be used by the body." Rewrite this using precise biological wording.
During digestion, large biological molecules are hydrolysed into smaller molecules that are soluble enough to be absorbed across cell membranes lining the ileum.
❓ Suggest why the final hydrolysis step of each digestion pathway typically occurs at, or very close to, the ileum epithelium.
Producing the final monomer at the epithelial cell membrane keeps its local concentration high exactly where it needs to be absorbed, maximising the concentration gradient available for diffusion or co-transport.

Carbohydrate digestion

❓ Describe the digestion of starch to glucose.
Amylase, produced by the salivary glands and the pancreas, hydrolyses the glycosidic bonds in starch, producing maltose. Membrane-bound maltase, fixed in the cell-surface membrane of the ileum epithelium, then hydrolyses the glycosidic bond in maltose, producing glucose, which is absorbed.
❓ Explain why maltase is described as membrane-bound rather than free in the gut lumen.
Because maltase is fixed in the ileum epithelium's cell-surface membrane, glucose is produced directly at the point of absorption, keeping the local glucose concentration high exactly where a steep gradient is needed for uptake.
❓ Name the three membrane-bound disaccharidases of the ileum epithelium and state the products of each.
Maltase hydrolyses maltose to glucose + glucose; sucrase hydrolyses sucrose to glucose + fructose; lactase hydrolyses lactose to glucose + galactose.

Lipid digestion

❓ Describe the digestion and emulsification of lipids.
Bile salts emulsify lipid into small droplets, increasing the surface area available for lipase to act on. Lipase hydrolyses the ester bonds in triglycerides, producing fatty acids and monoglycerides. These, together with bile salts, then form micelles, which transport them to the ileum epithelium for absorption.
❓ A student states that "bile salts hydrolyse fat into micelles." Identify and correct the error.
Bile salts are not enzymes and do not hydrolyse anything — they emulsify lipid into droplets, which is a purely physical process that increases surface area. Micelles are formed later, after lipase has hydrolysed the ester bonds, from the resulting fatty acids and monoglycerides.
❓ Explain why fatty acids and monoglycerides do not require a carrier protein to enter an epithelial cell.
Fatty acids and monoglycerides are non-polar and lipid-soluble, so they can diffuse directly through the phospholipid bilayer of the epithelial cell membrane.

Protein digestion

❓ Distinguish between endopeptidases, exopeptidases and dipeptidases.
Endopeptidases hydrolyse peptide bonds within the polypeptide chain, at internal, non-terminal positions, producing more chain ends. Exopeptidases hydrolyse peptide bonds at the terminal residues, removing amino acids one at a time to produce dipeptides. Dipeptidases hydrolyse the final peptide bond in a dipeptide to give single amino acids, and are membrane-bound on the ileum epithelium.
❓ Explain why endopeptidases act on a polypeptide before exopeptidases do.
Endopeptidases hydrolyse bonds within the chain, creating many new chain ends; because exopeptidases can only act at ends, this gives them far more sites to act on simultaneously, increasing the overall rate of protein hydrolysis.
❓ Explain why pepsin, which is active in the stomach, stops working once food enters the small intestine.
The small intestine has a higher, neutral/slightly alkaline pH than the stomach; this disrupts the bonds maintaining pepsin's tertiary structure, changing the shape of its active site so its substrate can no longer bind — pepsin is denatured.

Co-transport absorption

❓ Describe the co-transport of glucose into an ileum epithelial cell.
The sodium–potassium pump, in the basal membrane, actively transports Na⁺ out of the epithelial cell into the blood, using ATP. This maintains a low internal Na⁺ concentration relative to the gut lumen. Na⁺ then diffuses back into the cell down this gradient, through a co-transporter protein in the apical membrane, which simultaneously carries glucose into the cell against its own concentration gradient. Glucose then passes into the blood across the basal membrane by facilitated diffusion through a separate carrier protein.
❓ A student writes: "sodium is actively transported into the epithelial cell, dragging glucose in with it." Identify and correct the error.
Sodium is not actively transported into the cell — the Na⁺/K⁺ pump actively transports sodium OUT of the cell into the blood, using ATP. Sodium then re-enters the cell down its concentration gradient by facilitated diffusion through the co-transporter, dragging glucose in with it; this is indirect (secondary) active transport of glucose, not direct active transport of sodium at that step.
❓ Explain why the process by which glucose enters an epithelial cell via the co-transporter is not classed as simple active transport of glucose.
No ATP is used directly at the co-transporter; glucose moves against its own concentration gradient only because it is carried in alongside sodium ions moving down the sodium gradient that the ATP-powered pump created — the energy is spent at the pump, not at the co-transporter itself.

Micelles & lipid absorption

❓ Explain the role of micelles in the absorption of the products of lipid digestion.
Micelles are small, soluble structures with fatty acids and monoglycerides oriented tails-inward (non-polar core) and bile-salt/phospholipid heads outward (polar surface), allowing them to dissolve and move through the watery gut contents. They continually break down and re-form, delivering fatty acids and monoglycerides to the epithelial cell surface at a high local concentration, where these products then diffuse directly through the phospholipid bilayer.
❓ Compare how glucose and the products of lipid digestion are absorbed into an ileum epithelial cell.
Glucose requires a specific transport protein — it is co-transported into the cell alongside sodium ions via a co-transporter, then leaves into the blood via a separate GLUT carrier by facilitated diffusion. Fatty acids and monoglycerides, delivered to the membrane by micelles, need no transport protein at all — being non-polar and lipid-soluble, they diffuse directly through the phospholipid bilayer.
