Mass Transport in Plants

Two mechanisms either side of one hard vocabulary boundary: the passive, tension-driven cohesion-tension theory pulls water up the xylem, while the active, ATP-dependent mass flow hypothesis pushes sucrose through the phloem from source to sink. Mixing the two tissues' vocabulary is the single most common error nationally on this spec point.

⚠️

The single rule that costs the most marks on this spec point: never borrow xylem vocabulary for phloem, or vice versa. Xylem moves water and mineral ions upwards only, in dead vessels, by a passive pull (cohesion-tension). Phloem moves organic substances up or down, in living sieve tubes, by a mechanism requiring ATP/active transport (mass flow). And name the phloem molecule precisely — "sugars" is accepted for sucrose, but any other named sugar such as glucose is not.

Section 1 · spec content point

💧 Xylem & the cohesion-tension theory

Xylem carries water and mineral ions upwards only, through dead, lignified vessels — driven by a passive, tension-based pull. This is one of the weakest-known topics nationally, mostly because the causal chain gets stated out of order, or osmosis gets blamed for a step it doesn't explain.

📖

What the specification says: "Xylem as the tissue that transports water in the stem and leaves of plants. The cohesion-tension theory of water transport in the xylem."

1
2
3

Picture a drinking straw full of water, where every water molecule is holding hands with the ones next to it. If you sip from the top, the whole chain of holding-hands molecules gets dragged up together, right down to the bottom of the straw — not one molecule at a time. That's roughly how a tree gets water to its leaves: water escapes as vapour out of tiny holes in the leaf, and because all the water molecules below are holding hands (sticking together), the whole column gets pulled up the plant's drinking-straw tubes (the xylem) to replace what was lost.

  • Losing water vapour from the leaf = transpiration
  • Water molecules holding hands with each other = cohesion
  • Water molecules sticking to the tube walls = adhesion
  • The pulling force in the tube = tension
  • The drinking-straw tubes themselves = xylem — dead, hollow, and stacked end to end

Water constantly evaporates out of the leaf's mesophyll cells and diffuses out through the stomata — this loss is transpiration. Losing that water lowers the water potential of the mesophyll cells, and because water always moves from a higher to a lower water potential, more water gets pulled out of the xylem to replace it. That pulling creates tension — a negative pressure — running all the way down the xylem tube. Water molecules can be pulled as one continuous column, rather than snapping apart, because they cohere (stick together) via hydrogen bonds; they also adhere to the vessel walls, which helps but isn't strictly required in an answer. Right down at the root, water gets into the xylem in the first place mostly by osmosis — but that's the only step in this whole process where osmosis is actually the right word. Once water is in the dead xylem vessel itself, it is tension and cohesion doing the work, never osmosis.

Tissue stateDead — lignified, hollow vessels, no cytoplasmLiving — sieve tube elements are enucleate but alive; companion cells alongside are fully living
DirectionUpwards only, roots → leavesUp or down, source → sink

▶ 🚩 The costliest sequencing error

Cohesion does not "create" tension, and evaporation does not "create" cohesion — the order is: evaporation lowers the mesophyll's water potential, which is what pulls water up the xylem, creating tension; cohesion (hydrogen bonding between water molecules) is what lets that pull act on an unbroken, continuous column rather than snapping it apart. Also never write that water moves up the dead xylem vessel "by osmosis" — osmosis only explains water entering living cells, at the root and again at the mesophyll; the bulk movement up the vessel itself is driven purely by tension.

▶ Evidence for the cohesion-tension theory

Three observations are worth quoting directly rather than re-explaining the theory: water moves up a trunk at a fast, measurable rate, consistent with a pulled column rather than diffusion; the trunk's diameter is smallest at midday, when transpiration — and so tension — is greatest, because the tension pulls the vessel walls slightly inward; and if a xylem vessel is broken or a stem is cut, air is drawn in rather than water leaking out, which shows the column was under tension (negative pressure), not positive pressure.

How water gets from the soil into the xylem in the first place

In the root, the Casparian strip blocks the apoplast pathway (the route through cell walls), forcing water and dissolved ions through the symplast pathway instead. The endodermis then actively transports ions into the xylem, producing a lower water potential inside the xylem — so water follows by osmosis, down a water potential gradient. This root-uptake step is genuinely the one place in the whole cohesion-tension story where osmosis is the correct mechanism; everything from that point up to the leaf is driven by tension and cohesion instead.

📌

Full "explain water uptake from soil to leaf" answers need both halves — the root-uptake steps (Casparian strip, active transport, osmosis) and the leaf-end steps (evaporation, tension, cohesion, adhesion). A "transpiration pull" answer alone only covers the leaf end.

Section 2 · spec content point

🧪 Transpiration rate & the potometer

Every factor that changes transpiration rate works through the same route: it changes the water potential gradient between the leaf's air spaces and the outside air, which changes how fast water vapour diffuses out through the stomata. Practical questions on the potometer are examined in precise, procedural detail.

📖

What the specification says: "Xylem as the tissue that transports water in the stem and leaves of plants. The cohesion-tension theory of water transport in the xylem." (Same specification content point as Section 1 — this half applies the theory to the exam factors affecting transpiration rate, and to the potometer as the practical technique for measuring it.)

1
2
3

Water vapour leaves a leaf faster whenever the air outside is drier, windier, hotter, or whenever the leaf's tiny doors (stomata) are open wider. And to measure how fast a cut shoot drinks water, scientists use a gadget called a potometer — basically a tube with a little air bubble in it that gets pushed along as the plant sucks water up.

  • Tiny doors on a leaf = stomata
  • Gadget that measures water uptake = potometer
  • The moving marker inside it = the air bubble

Four things speed up or slow down transpiration, and each one works by changing the water potential gradient between the leaf and the air, or by changing how open the stomata are: more air movement blows saturated air away from the leaf, steepening the gradient; higher humidity does the opposite, since water vapour builds up around the leaf and the gradient shrinks; higher temperature gives water molecules more kinetic energy so they diffuse and evaporate faster; and brighter light makes the stomata open (for photosynthesis), which simply gives water vapour more space to escape through. A potometer measures how fast a cut shoot takes up water, tracked by an air bubble moving along a scale — but it isn't a perfect stand-in for transpiration, since some of that water is used for support, some for photosynthesis, and some is even added by respiration.

FactorEffect, reasoned through the water potential gradient
Air movementRemoves water vapour / saturated air from around the leaf, steepening the water potential gradient, so more diffusion and evaporation occurs
HumidityWater vapour accumulates around the leaf as humidity rises, so the gradient is reduced and transpiration falls
TemperatureIncreases the kinetic energy of water molecules, so they move faster, increasing diffusion and evaporation
Light intensityBrighter light causes the stomata to open (for photosynthesis); transpiration increases, and more water is pulled up by cohesion-tension as a result
▶ 🚩 The stomata mark must be explicit

"Loss through stomata" alone is not sufficient for the light-intensity factor — you must state that the stomata open (or close). A correctly described guard-cell response — guard cells becoming turgid or flaccid — is accepted as equivalent to stomata opening or closing, but a vague reference to "stomata" with no change of state is not.

The bubble potometer

Not a single formally numbered required practical, but the potometer method is examined in precise procedural detail — and there are two distinct designs that must never be blended together.

A bubble potometer — the air bubble moves along the capillary scale as the shoot takes up water, and is reset by opening the tap to let water in from the reservoir.
  • Setting up (precautions): seal all joints so the apparatus is airtight and watertight; cut the shoot underwater; cut it at a slant; dry off the leaves; insert the shoot into the apparatus underwater; make sure no air bubbles are already present; shut the tap; note where the bubble is at the start.
  • Resetting the bubble: open the tap, or add water from the reservoir.

Why a potometer measures water uptake, not transpiration directly

  • Some water taken up is used for support / turgidity, not lost as vapour.
  • Some water is used in photosynthesis.
  • Water is also produced in respiration — adding to the plant's water content without counting as "uptake".
  • The apparatus may not be perfectly sealed and may leak (water used in hydrolysis or condensation reactions is also accepted here).

Effect of removing leaves on water uptake

Fewer leaves means less surface area for evaporation and fewer stomata through which water vapour can diffuse out — so less evaporation/transpiration occurs, which in turn generates less cohesion-tension pulling force to draw water up.

⚖️

A weight/mass potometer is a completely different design — it measures water uptake by recording the change in mass of the whole apparatus (shoot plus reservoir) over time, with no bubble or capillary scale involved at all.

▶ 🚩 Trap — blending the two potometer designs

Describing a bubble potometer's method when a weight/mass potometer has been asked about — or the reverse — is a very commonly seen error. Keep the two designs completely separate in any answer, and always frame the result as "water uptake", never "transpiration rate" directly, since the two are not identical.

Section 3 · spec content point

🍬 Phloem & the mass flow hypothesis

Phloem carries organic substances — chiefly sucrose and amino acids — up or down the plant, from source to sink, through living sieve tubes, moved by an active, ATP-dependent process. This is one of the weakest-known topics nationally, because so many answers default to cohesion-tension theory instead.

📖

What the specification says: "Phloem as the tissue that transports organic substances in plants. The mass flow hypothesis for the mechanism of translocation in plants."

1
2
3

Imagine a leaf pumping sugar into a pipe, using energy to do it — like squeezing extra toothpaste into an already-full tube. Water then rushes in after the sugar, because wherever there's lots of dissolved sugar, water wants to follow. With the pipe now fuller and under more pressure at the leaf end, the whole mixture gets squeezed along the pipe toward wherever sugar is needed — like roots or growing fruit — where it gets pumped back out and used up or stored.

  • Where sugar is pumped in = the source (usually a leaf making sugar by photosynthesis)
  • Where sugar is pumped out and used = the sink (roots, fruit, buds, growing parts)
  • The sugar being moved = sucrose — never call it "sugar" or "glucose" generically
  • The pipe = phloem, specifically the sieve tubes

At the source — usually a photosynthesising leaf — companion cells actively load sucrose into the phloem, using ATP, alongside hydrogen ions. That extra dissolved sucrose lowers the water potential inside the phloem, so water follows it in by osmosis from the neighbouring xylem. With more water now inside, a high hydrostatic pressure builds up at the source end. That pressure difference between source and sink is what actually pushes the sugary solution — as a bulk flow, not by diffusion — all the way along the phloem to wherever it's needed. At the sink, sucrose is actively removed from the phloem and either respired for energy or stored, often as starch.

At the source — a photosynthesising leaf, or any part of the plant releasing stored sugar — sucrose is actively transported into the phloem sieve elements, co-transported with H⁺ ions, by the companion cells alongside them. This lowers the water potential inside the phloem, so water enters (from the adjacent xylem) by osmosis. The extra water produces a high hydrostatic pressure — or a hydrostatic pressure gradient — at the source. That pressure gradient drives mass flow towards the sink: roots, storage tissue, respiring cells, buds, young leaves, fruits, seeds, or meristems, whichever is currently using or storing sugar. At the sink, sucrose is removed / unloaded by active transport, and used in respiration or stored as starch.

Term used in the answerWhat's accepted
The transported molecule"Sugars" is accepted for sucrose — but any other named sugar, such as glucose, is not credited where sucrose specifically is meant
Phloem cellSieve element / sieve tube is accepted for phloem cell

Mass flow: sucrose loaded at the source raises hydrostatic pressure there, pushing the phloem solution — by bulk flow, not diffusion — to wherever it's unloaded at a sink.
▶ 🚩 Trap — naming the molecule and the mechanism precisely

"Glucose" or an unqualified "sugar" alone is not credited wherever the demand specifically asks for the substance loaded into the phloem — it is sucrose. Mass flow is also bulk flow of solution driven by a pressure gradient, not diffusion — describing it as sugar molecules "diffusing" along the phloem is incorrect.

Interpreting translocation-rate data

  • Linking rate to photosynthesis: the rate of photosynthesis is related to the rate of sucrose production, so the rate of translocation is higher when the sucrose concentration is higher.
  • Where translocation continues after a treatment: the rate does not fall to zero — translocation still occurs, but sucrose can no longer enter the cytoplasm of the phloem cells as effectively as before.

Section 4 · spec content point

🔬 Tracers, ringing experiments & evaluating mass flow

The named skill on this spec point: interpreting evidence from tracer and ringing experiments, and evaluating the evidence for and against the mass flow hypothesis in a genuinely balanced way.

📖

What the specification says: "The use of tracers and ringing experiments to investigate transport in plants." Students should be able to recognise correlations and causal relationships, interpret evidence from tracer and ringing experiments, and evaluate the evidence for and against the mass flow hypothesis.

1
2
3

To prove sugar really moves through the outer "pipe" layer of a tree trunk, scientists strip a ring of bark off and watch what happens: a bulge of trapped sugar builds up just above the missing ring, and the part below starts to wither, because sugar can no longer get past. In another experiment, they feed a plant a "glowing" (radioactive) version of carbon dioxide, let it turn that into sugar by photosynthesis, and then use special film to see exactly where that glowing sugar ends up in the stem.

A ringing experiment strips a ring of bark and phloem from a woody stem, leaving the xylem untouched; a bulge of sugar-rich fluid builds up above the ring while the tissue below withers, showing organic substances travel in the phloem, downward from the leaves in this case. A tracer experiment feeds a plant radioactively labelled carbon dioxide, which gets built into sugar during photosynthesis; thin sections of the stem placed on X-ray film then reveal exactly where that labelled sugar ends up — in the phloem. Evidence for mass flow includes things like sap being released under pressure when phloem is punctured; evidence against includes the fact that sieve plates should, in theory, slow flow down more than is actually observed. A good evaluation always uses both sides, not just one.

Ringing experiments

A ring of bark and phloem is removed from a woody stem, leaving the xylem intact. After a period, a bulge (swelling) appears above the ring — the tissue above accumulates sugars, and the fluid there has a high sugar concentration. Tissue below the ring withers. This shows that organic substances are transported in the phloem, and — in this particular set-up — that transport here is downwards, from the leaves.

Tracer experiments

A plant is supplied with radioactively labelled carbon dioxide. The labelled carbon is converted into sugar / organic substances during photosynthesis. Thin sections of the stem are then placed on X-ray film — autoradiography shows the labelled sugar is present in the phloem.

▶ The heat-treatment version — two separate routes, don't mix them

Route A (untreated / control plant): the labelled carbon is converted into sugar during photosynthesis, and mass flow / translocation in the phloem occurs throughout the plant, in the untreated plant only.

Route B (heat-treated plant): movement in the phloem requires living cells, respiration, active transport and ATP; heat treatment damages living cells (or stops respiration / ATP production), so transport in the phloem occurs only in the untreated plant, not the heat-treated one. A named organic substance — glucose, sucrose or an amino acid — is accepted specifically in this tracer context, relaxing the usual "sucrose-only" naming rule.

🧮

Calculating a rate from a tracer: take the time between the peak concentration of labelled carbon reaching the top of the trunk and reaching the bottom; take the distance as the length of trunk between the two measuring points; then rate = distance ÷ time.

Evaluating the evidence for and against mass flow

Evidence in supportEvidence against
Ringing shows phloem is involved in transporting organic substancesNo bulge appears above the ring in some ringing experiments
Cooling or metabolic inhibitors reduce or stop flow, showing respiration / active transport is involvedNo role for osmosis, hydrostatic pressure or water movement has been directly demonstrated
Sucrose concentration is higher at the source than at the sinkMovement could simply be due to gravity

▶ 🚩 Balance is required

Evaluation mark schemes cap one-sided answers — typically setting a maximum mark available on each side. Giving six "for" points and no "against" points does not score full marks; a genuinely balanced answer, drawing on both columns above, is what's needed.

Using a control properly

A control is used to compare the effect of other treatments, or as a baseline — it shows or measures the effect of the substance being tested, or accounts for the effect of substances produced naturally by the plant itself. Specifically, it lets you say that it was the named treatment — and not, for example, the agar block, the water, or some other part of the set-up — that caused the observed difference. An unqualified reference to "compare results" on its own is not accepted; the answer needs to say what the comparison actually isolates.

▶ Where this point sits in difficulty terms

Two full mechanisms — cohesion-tension and mass flow — each a multi-step chain of cause and effect, sit either side of a strict vocabulary boundary that must never be crossed. The cohesion-tension sequence is easy to state out of order, and it's common to see water described as moving up the xylem "by osmosis", which it doesn't. The mass flow hypothesis is one of the weakest-known topics nationally — many answers default to cohesion-tension theory instead, mix up "source and sink" language incorrectly, or confuse translocation with transpiration entirely; students frequently don't know that sucrose specifically, rather than a vague "sugar" or "glucose", is the molecule loaded by co-transport with H⁺ ions.

🧭

A quick self-check before writing any answer on this spec point: silently sort every noun you're about to use into one of two lists — xylem/cohesion-tension words (evaporation, water potential, tension, cohesion, adhesion, continuous column) on one side, phloem/mass flow words (source, sink, sucrose, companion cell, co-transport, hydrostatic pressure, active transport, unloading) on the other. If a word could plausibly belong to either list, it's being used wrong in that particular answer.

Section 6 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

This content works well as a supporting paragraph inside a longer essay-style answer on more than one theme — the trick is closing each paragraph with a sentence that ties the mechanism explicitly back to whatever the title is actually about.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around diffusion

Water vapour diffuses out through open stomata during transpiration, lowering the water potential of the mesophyll cells and pulling water up the xylem under tension, as described by the cohesion-tension theory. Carbon dioxide separately diffuses into the leaf through the stomata and through the air spaces of the mesophyll, down its own diffusion gradient, to reach photosynthesising cells. Linking this back to a title on diffusion: the diffusion of water vapour out of the leaf is the driving force behind the entire mass transport system in the xylem, while diffusion of carbon dioxide supplies the substrate needed for the sugar that phloem later transports — diffusion acting both at the start of the water transport system, and in feeding the process that the phloem's mass flow depends on.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around pressure and bulk flow

At the source, sucrose is actively loaded into the phloem by companion cells, lowering the water potential there so that water enters by osmosis from the adjacent xylem and raises the hydrostatic pressure. This pressure gradient then drives mass flow — bulk movement of the whole solution, not diffusion of individual molecules — all the way to the sink, where sucrose is unloaded and either respired or stored. This is a useful example of a pressure-driven bulk transport system, distinct from the purely diffusion-based or tension-based systems seen elsewhere in exchange and transport.

Whichever essay theme this content supports, treat it as the "mechanism" paragraph: state the process precisely — naming water potential, tension and cohesion for xylem, or hydrostatic pressure and mass flow for phloem, depending on what the title is asking about — then add one sentence linking it explicitly back to that theme.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Xylem & the cohesion-tension theory

  • Chain, in order: evaporation from mesophyll + diffusion out through stomata → lowers water potential → tension pulls water up → cohesion (hydrogen bonds) keeps the column continuous → adhesion to vessel walls is accepted but not required.
  • Osmosis only applies at the root, entering the xylem — never for bulk movement up the vessel itself.
  • Root uptake: Casparian strip blocks the apoplast route → active transport of ions into the xylem by the endodermis → lower water potential → water enters by osmosis.
  • Evidence: fast measurable uptake rate; trunk diameter smallest at midday; air (not water) drawn in if a vessel breaks.

2 · Transpiration rate & the potometer

  • Air movement ↑ and temperature ↑ → transpiration ↑ (steeper gradient / faster diffusion); humidity ↑ → transpiration ↓; light intensity ↑ → stomata open → transpiration ↑.
  • Stomata mark must be explicit — "open" or "close", not just "loss through stomata".
  • Bubble potometer measures water uptake, not transpiration: some water is used for support, some for photosynthesis, some is produced by respiration, and the apparatus may leak.
  • Weight/mass potometer is a totally different design (mass change) — never blend the two.

3 · Phloem & the mass flow hypothesis

  • Chain: sucrose actively loaded at the source (co-transport with H⁺, via companion cells) → lowers water potential in phloem → water enters by osmosis from the xylem → high hydrostatic pressure → mass flow (bulk flow) to the sink → sucrose unloaded by active transport → respired or stored as starch.
  • Never "sugar"/"glucose" unqualified — the molecule is sucrose. "Source" and "sink" belong only here, never to xylem.
  • Translocation rate tracks sucrose concentration / photosynthesis rate; it never falls to zero after a treatment, only slows.

4 · Tracers, ringing & evaluating mass flow

  • Ringing: remove a ring of bark + phloem (xylem intact) → bulge of high-sugar fluid above, withering below → shows organic transport in phloem.
  • Tracer: ¹⁴CO₂ fed to plant → built into sugar by photosynthesis → autoradiography of stem sections shows it in the phloem. Heat-treated plants lose transport (needs living cells/ATP) — don't mix the untreated and heat-treated routes.
  • Rate from a tracer = distance between measuring points ÷ time between the ¹⁴C peaks at each point.
  • Evaluation must be balanced — for: pressure gradient, sap released on puncture, mitochondria-rich companion cells; against: sieve plates should impede flow more than observed, not all solutes move at the same rate.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Xylem & the cohesion-tension theory

❓ Explain how water is pulled from the roots to the leaves of a tall tree, using the cohesion-tension theory.
Water evaporates from the mesophyll cells and diffuses out through the stomata (transpiration), lowering the water potential of the mesophyll cells; this pulls water up the xylem, creating tension; water molecules cohere by hydrogen bonding, forming a continuous, unbroken water column from root to leaf; adhesion of water molecules to the xylem vessel walls also contributes.
❓ A student claims water moves up the xylem "by osmosis, down a water potential gradient." Assess this statement.
This is only correct for water entering the xylem at the root, and for water re-entering the mesophyll cells at the leaf — both are living-cell steps driven by osmosis. Bulk movement of water up the dead xylem vessel itself is driven by tension created by transpiration pull, not by osmosis.
❓ Give two pieces of evidence that support the cohesion-tension theory.
A tree trunk's diameter is measurably smallest at midday, when transpiration — and so tension in the xylem — is greatest; and if a xylem vessel is broken or a stem is cut, air is drawn in rather than water leaking out, showing the water column was under tension (negative pressure) rather than positive pressure.

Transpiration rate & the potometer

❓ Explain why increasing air movement around a leafy shoot increases its rate of transpiration.
Increased air movement removes water vapour / saturated air from around the leaf; this increases the water potential (diffusion) gradient between the leaf's air spaces and the surrounding air, so more water vapour diffuses out and evaporation increases.
❓ Describe the precautions that must be taken when setting up a bubble potometer.
Seal all joints so the apparatus is airtight and watertight; cut the shoot underwater and at a slant; dry off the leaves; insert the shoot into the apparatus underwater, ensuring no air bubbles are present; shut the tap; note the bubble's starting position on the scale before timing begins.
❓ Explain why a potometer measures water uptake rather than transpiration rate directly.
Some water taken up is used for support / turgidity rather than lost as vapour; some is used in photosynthesis; water is also produced by respiration, adding to the plant's water content without being "uptake"; and the apparatus may not be perfectly sealed and may leak.

Phloem & the mass flow hypothesis

❓ Explain the mass flow hypothesis for the movement of sucrose from a leaf to a growing root.
At the source, sucrose is actively transported into the phloem sieve elements, co-transported with H⁺ ions, by companion cells; this lowers the water potential in the phloem, so water enters from the adjacent xylem by osmosis; this produces a high hydrostatic pressure gradient; the pressure gradient drives mass flow of the solution towards the sink, where sucrose is unloaded by active transport and used in respiration or stored as starch.
❓ A student writes "glucose is pumped into the phloem at the leaf." Correct this statement with precise wording.
The molecule actively loaded into the phloem is sucrose, not glucose — "sugars" is accepted as an alternative term for sucrose in this context, but no other individually named sugar is credited.
❓ Data show translocation rate is higher on a sunny day than a cloudy one. Interpret this finding.
The rate of photosynthesis is related to the rate of sucrose production, so on a sunnier day sucrose concentration at the source is higher, and the rate of translocation is correspondingly higher.

Tracers, ringing & evaluating mass flow

❓ Describe a ringing experiment and explain what its results show about phloem transport.
A ring of bark and phloem is removed from a woody stem, leaving the xylem intact; after a period, a bulge appears above the ring where sugars accumulate, and the tissue below the ring withers; this shows organic substances are transported in the phloem, moving downwards from the leaves in this set-up.
❓ Explain how a ¹⁴CO₂ tracer experiment provides evidence that sugar is transported in the phloem.
A plant is supplied with radioactively labelled carbon dioxide; the labelled carbon is converted into sugar during photosynthesis; thin sections of the stem are placed on X-ray film, and autoradiography shows the labelled sugar is present specifically in the phloem tissue.
❓ Evaluate the evidence for and against the mass flow hypothesis.
For: a hydrostatic pressure gradient exists, shown by sap being released when the phloem is punctured; sucrose concentration is higher at the source than the sink; companion cells contain many mitochondria, consistent with active loading. Against: sieve plates would be expected to impede mass flow more than is actually observed; not all solutes move at the same speed, which simple mass flow does not fully explain; no bulge appears above the ring in some ringing experiments.
