Most of a Cell's DNA is Not Translated

Every cell in the body is built from an identical genome, yet a neurone, a heart-muscle cell and a skin cell end up looking and behaving completely differently — this is the genetic basis of specialisation, and the reason totipotent, pluripotent, multipotent and unipotent stem cells all behave so differently from one another. This page also covers how induced pluripotent stem cells are made in the lab, and how to evaluate stem-cell treatments.

⚠️

The single rule that costs the most marks on this spec point: never write that specialised cells "use their genes differently" — the credited mechanism is that cells translate only part of their DNA. The second recurring trap is reversing totipotent (whole organism + extra-embryonic tissue, brief window) and pluripotent (any body cell, but never a whole organism) — keep the two strictly apart.

Section 1 · spec content point

🧬 Same DNA, different cells

Why can a neurone, a heart-muscle cell and a skin cell look and behave so differently, when every one of them was built from an identical genome? This is the idea underneath almost every other spec point in Topic 3.8 — get the mechanism exact here and everything else builds on it cleanly.

📖

What the specification says: "Totipotent cells can divide and produce any type of body cell. During development, totipotent cells translate only part of their DNA, resulting in cell specialisation."

1
2
3

Imagine every cell in your body is handed the exact same enormous instruction manual the moment it's made — because every cell comes from copying the very first cell, over and over. But no cell ever reads the whole manual. Each cell only ever opens a few particular pages and actually follows those instructions — the rest of the book stays shut, page for page, forever. Which pages get read is what turns one plain starting cell into a nerve cell, a heart cell or a skin cell, even though they were all handed the identical book.

  • Genome = the complete instruction manual (all the DNA) inside a cell
  • Gene = one instruction/recipe inside that manual, for building one protein
  • Transcribe = copy a recipe out of the manual so it can be used
  • Translate = actually follow that copied recipe and build the protein
  • Specialised cell = a cell built for one particular job, because of which recipes it followed

Every cell in an organism is produced by mitosis, ultimately from a single fertilised egg — so every cell carries an identical set of genes. What makes cells different from one another isn't which genes are present, since that never changes. It's which of those genes actually get transcribed and translated in that particular cell type — the rest stay permanently switched off. Because different genes are translated, different cells make different proteins, and different proteins give a cell a different structure suited to a different job. That whole process is specialisation.

  1. All cells contain the same genes (identical DNA), inherited by mitosis from the same starting cell.
  2. In any one cell, only some of those genes are transcribed and translated — the rest are switched off.
  3. Different genes translated → different proteins made → different structure and function → the cell is specialised.

Every cell in an organism — a neurone, a cardiomyocyte, a skin cell — arises by mitosis from a single zygote, so every cell carries an identical genome (this links directly back to 3.2.2 All cells arise from other cells). What makes them different is not their DNA content but which part of it is actually used: each cell type translates only part of that DNA, and it's that selection — not any difference in the genes present — that produces cell specialisation.

The full exam-answer chain

A question such as "explain why cells with identical DNA become structurally different" is built from three separate marking points, and each one needs to be stated explicitly rather than implied:

  1. All the cells of an organism contain the same genes (identical DNA), because they arise by mitosis from a single zygote.
  2. In any one cell, only some of those genes are transcribed and translated — the rest remain permanently switched off.
  3. This selective translation produces different proteins in different cells, giving each a different structure suited to a specific function.
🔑

This is selective gene expression, never selective gene content — no genes are lost or gained as a cell specialises. Which genes get switched on is controlled by transcription factors, epigenetics and RNA interference — the molecular machinery for this is covered in full at 3.8.2.2, so it doesn't need to be named here, only implied by "translated."

▶ 🚩 The single costliest wording slip on this point

Never write that cells "use their genes differently" as a stand-alone answer — this vaguer phrasing on its own is not the credited mechanism. State explicitly that cells translate only part of their DNA, and never imply that specialised cells lose genes as they develop — every cell keeps the full genome, whether or not it's being used.

All three cells carry the same six genes — specialisation comes from which genes are transcribed and translated, never from a different set of genes being present.

Section 2 · spec content point

🪜 Totipotency and the stem-cell hierarchy

Four categories, one ordered ladder — potential narrows as development proceeds. The examiners' favourite trap is blurring totipotent and pluripotent into a single "can become anything" idea; the two discriminators below fix that.

📖

What the specification says: "Totipotent cells occur only for a limited time in early mammalian embryos. Pluripotent cells are found in embryos; multipotent and unipotent cells are found in mature mammals and can divide to form a limited number of different cell types. Pluripotent stem cells can divide in unlimited numbers and can be used in treating human disorders. Unipotent cells, exemplified by the formation of cardiomyocytes."

1
2
3

Think of four different master keys. A totipotent key opens absolutely every door in a huge building — including a special store room that can build a brand-new copy of the whole building. It only exists for a tiny window right at the start, before the building is even finished. A pluripotent key opens every ordinary room too, but not that special store room — it can't build a whole new building. A multipotent key only opens a small cluster of related rooms. A unipotent key opens just one single door.

  • Totipotent = can become any body cell, plus the "extra" tissue that builds a whole new organism
  • Pluripotent = can become any body cell, but never a whole new organism
  • Multipotent = can only become a small related group of cell types
  • Unipotent = can only become one single type of cell
  • Stem cell = a cell that can keep dividing AND can turn into other cell types

A stem cell is defined by two separate abilities: it can carry on dividing indefinitely, and it can differentiate into other cell types — both parts matter. Beyond that, there's a hierarchy of four categories, and potential narrows as you go down it. Totipotent cells can become literally any cell in the body, plus the placenta and other tissue outside the embryo itself — but this only lasts for the first few divisions of a fertilised egg. Pluripotent cells, found in embryos, can still become any type of body cell and divide without limit, but they've lost the ability to build a whole new organism. Multipotent cells, found in adults, can only become a limited range of related cell types. Unipotent cells, also found in adults, can only become one single cell type — heart-muscle cells (cardiomyocytes) are the named example.

The full definition of a "stem cell"

A stem cell is a cell that can both divide / replicate indefinitely and differentiate into other cell types — both halves are separately credited, so a full answer states both explicitly, even if it feels repetitive.

▶ 🚩 "Differentiate" ≠ "divide/replicate"

These are marked as two separate points — an answer giving only one half of the stem-cell definition scores only half the marks available.

Learn it as one ordered ladder, not four isolated facts: totipotent → any body cell + extra-embryonic tissue, embryo only, brief window; pluripotent → any body cell, not a whole organism, found in embryos, unlimited division; multipotent → a limited range of related cell types, mature mammals; unipotent → one cell type only (cardiomyocytes), mature mammals.

▶ 🚩 The single most-tested boundary

Totipotent vs pluripotent is the distinction reversed most often. Totipotent cells can form a whole organism, because they can additionally produce extra-embryonic tissue; pluripotent cells can form any body cell type but never a whole organism. Remembering that only totipotent cells can form extra-embryonic tissue — and that this is also what limits totipotency to a brief early window — resolves most of the confusion.

Potential narrows as development proceeds: totipotent → pluripotent → multipotent / unipotent. A stem cell is defined by two separate abilities, not one.

Section 3 · spec content point

🧫 Induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cells

The one artificial way to climb back up the potency ladder: take an already-specialised adult cell, and reprogram it back to pluripotent using transcription factors.

📖

What the specification says: "Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPS cells) can be produced from adult somatic cells using appropriate protein transcription factors."

1
2
3

Imagine an already-specialised cell, like a skin cell, is like a video that's been fast-forwarded a long way through — it's already playing one particular scene and can't go back on its own. Scientists can press a special "rewind" button (a set of proteins called transcription factors) that sends the cell all the way back to the very start of the video, where it can become basically anything again. From there, it can be fast-forwarded to a completely different scene — turned into whatever specialised cell is actually needed.

  • Adult somatic cell = an ordinary, already-specialised body cell (e.g. a skin cell)
  • Transcription factor = a protein that switches particular genes on
  • iPS cell = induced pluripotent stem cell — a "rewound," lab-made pluripotent cell

Scientists can take an ordinary adult body cell — one that's already specialised — and treat it with specific proteins called transcription factors. These switch on the genes needed for pluripotency, so the cell effectively "rewinds" and becomes pluripotent: it can now divide without limit and be turned into whatever specialised cell type is actually needed. Because these cells are made from the patient's own adult cells rather than taken from an embryo, they come with a capped list of advantages over other treatments: they give a lasting effect rather than needing repeated doses, they need no donor, no embryo is destroyed, and there's a lower chance of the patient's immune system rejecting them.

How iPS cells are made and what they can do

  1. Adult somatic cells are treated with specific protein transcription factors.
  2. These switch on the genes associated with pluripotency, so the cell becomes pluripotent.
  3. The resulting iPS cell can then differentiate into the required specialised cell type (e.g. green-sensitive cone cells, in a colour-blindness treatment context).
🔗

How the transcription factor itself works — moving from cytoplasm to nucleus, binding the promoter region, stimulating RNA polymerase — is examined as a direct extension of this idea, and is covered in full at 3.8.2.2. It doesn't need to be described here.

Advantages of iPS cells (capped at 3 marks)

Even though more valid points exist, questions here are capped — select the strongest, rather than listing everything you know:

  • Permanent effect — avoids the need for repeated treatment (e.g. repeated drug doses)
  • No donor needed — the patient's own cells are reprogrammed
  • No embryo destroyed — sidesteps the embryonic stem cell ethical debate entirely
  • Reduced immune rejection — the cells are genetically the patient's own
▶ 🚩 State the ethical contrast explicitly

iPS cells are repeatedly used as the "ethically cleaner" alternative to embryonic stem cells, precisely because no embryo is destroyed and no donor is required — say this explicitly in an "advantages" answer, don't just imply it.

iPS cells are made by treating an adult somatic cell with transcription factors that switch on pluripotency genes — the same route can also lead back to unipotent cells such as cardiomyocytes.

Section 4 · exam skill

⚖️ Evaluating stem-cell treatments

This is the AO3 skill this spec point is built to test — and it's marked strictly to a template, not to general knowledge. "Produces healthy cells" alone is an incomplete answer, and evidence-evaluation questions specifically reward species, duration, rejection risk and completeness over a generic "small sample size."

Explain how stem cells could treat a named disorder

Written as separate marking points, never as one merged sentence:

  • The stem cells → produce healthy / normal cells
  • Separately → no faulty / defective cells are produced
  • The stem cells → divide (repeatedly replenishing the tissue)
  • From a sibling donor specifically → lower rejection risk
▶ 🚩 "Produce only healthy cells" is not two marks

"Produce only healthy cells" on its own scores only one of the two "healthy cells" ideas — the absence of faulty/defective cells has to be stated as well, as a distinct, separate point.

Evaluate animal-trial evidence for a stem-cell treatment

Focus the evaluation on what the data actually shows:

  • Species — the trial used animals, not humans, so direct extrapolation is limited
  • Duration of the study / follow-up
  • Rejection risk observed
  • Completeness of the data set (e.g. missing a healthy control, or results not given for every animal)
▶ 🚩 Sample size and statistical tests are not credited here

Never rely on sample size or statistical significance as an evaluative point for this specific demand — the mark scheme wants biological, not statistical, critique: species, duration, rejection risk and completeness of the data.

Discuss the ethics of embryonic stem cell use

AgainstFor
The embryo has the potential to become a person, and using it removes that potential without consent.Surplus IVF embryos would otherwise be destroyed anyway, and the treatment relieves suffering in an existing patient.

iPS cells sidestep this debate entirely, since no embryo is used at all — worth stating explicitly if a question invites a comparison.

📌

A "discuss" or "evaluate" answer that names both sides of an argument, and links back to the evidence given, always scores better than one that argues in a single direction — these command words are asking for balance, not a verdict.

Section 5 · exam technique

🎯 Common mistakes & precise wording

This spec point is not conceptually hard — the risk is entirely in precise wording and boundary confusion. A short list of sharply defined terms causes most of the marks lost here.

Vague / incomplete versionWhat to say instead
"Cells use their genes differently"Cells translate only part of their DNA — name the mechanism, not a vague paraphrase
"Totipotent and pluripotent are basically the same"Only totipotent cells can form extra-embryonic tissue and a whole organism — pluripotent cells cannot
"Produces healthy cells" as a full answerSplit into two separate sentences: produces healthy cells, AND no faulty/defective cells are produced
"Differentiate" used as if it means the same as "divide/replicate"Keep them as two separately credited ideas in any stem-cell definition
"The sample size was too small" as an evaluation pointUse species, duration, rejection risk and completeness of data instead — sample size isn't credited here
"Specialised cells lose genes as they develop"No genes are lost or gained — every cell keeps the full genome, whether it's translated or not
Naming the stem-cell category as if that were the answerThe marks are for what the cells actually do (divide, differentiate, produce healthy cells) — not for naming totipotent/pluripotent/etc.
▶ Where this point sits in difficulty terms

Memorisation load is medium, application depth is medium–high, and misconception frequency is high, specifically around totipotent/pluripotent. Every part of this spec point sits at A2 only — it isn't revisited from the AS course beyond the general idea that mitosis produces genetically identical daughter cells (3.2.2). The iPS-cell production chain used in a named-disease context is also heavily synoptic, since it can draw on inheritance genetics as well as the transcription-factor mechanism from 3.8.2.2 — a full answer has to connect all three without losing track of which spec point each part belongs to.

Section 6 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

This content works as a recurring paragraph across more than one essay title — the trick is using 3.8.2.1 to supply the genetic explanation for differentiation, then handing off directly to 3.8.2.2 for the molecular machinery that actually carries out the switching.

Titles this paragraph recurs in: "The importance of specialisation and differentiation of cells in organisms," "Only a small proportion of a cell's DNA codes for polypeptides," "The importance of the control of gene expression in cells and organisms."

▶ Example paragraph — angled around specialisation itself

All the cells of an organism contain the same genes, but only some are transcribed and translated in each cell — the rest are permanently switched off — so different proteins are produced in different cells, giving each a different structure and function. Totipotent cells, found only briefly in the early embryo, can divide and produce any type of body cell, including extra-embryonic tissue; pluripotent cells can become any body cell but not a whole organism; multipotent cells produce a limited range within one lineage; unipotent cells, e.g. cardiomyocytes, produce only one cell type. A stem cell is defined by two separate abilities: it can divide/replicate indefinitely, and it can differentiate into other cell types.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around "a small proportion of DNA"

This spec point states the title directly: the small coding proportion of a cell's DNA is used selectively, and that selective use is the entire basis of cell specialisation. Totipotent cells translate only part of their DNA, resulting in cell specialisation; induced pluripotent stem cells are made from adult somatic cells using transcription factors, demonstrating that this "switching" can even be reversed artificially, back to an earlier, less specialised state.

Essay technique: treat 3.8.2.1 as the paragraph that supplies the genetic explanation for differentiation itself, then hand off directly to 3.8.2.2 for the molecular machinery — transcription factors, epigenetics, RNA interference — that actually carries out the "switching on and off" this paragraph describes. The two spec points are almost always paired in essay plans.

Section 7

🧠 Memory hook, the plant exception & final checklist

Where 3.8.2.1 reappears across the rest of the specification

  • 3.2.2 All cells arise from other cells — the mitosis that produces the genetically identical daughter cells this spec point's cells subsequently diverge from.
  • 3.4.1 DNA, genes and chromosomes — the non-coding DNA (introns, multiple repeats, regulatory sequences) that sits alongside "not translated" in the wider sense of the phrase.
  • 3.8.2.2 Regulation of transcription and translation — the transcription-factor, epigenetic and RNA-interference machinery that actually carries out the gene-switching described here.
  • 3.8.2.3 Gene expression and cancer — what happens when this controlled specialisation process fails.
  • 3.8.3 Using genome projects — non-coding DNA and regulatory genes as the reason a genome sequence cannot easily be translated into a full proteome in complex organisms.
🌱

Plants are the exception. Unlike almost all mature animal cells, many mature plant cells keep the ability to develop into a whole new plant — they stay totipotent long after the embryonic stage. The specification's named practical link is producing tissue cultures of explants of cauliflower (Brassica oleracea): a small piece of tissue, grown under sterile (aseptic) conditions on a nutrient/hormone medium, divides by mitosis into an undifferentiated mass (a callus) that can then differentiate into a complete, genetically identical clone plant. Sterile technique matters here for the same reason it matters in any aseptic-technique practical — it prevents contamination by unwanted microorganisms, which would otherwise out-compete the explant tissue or introduce disease.

🧠

Memory hook — one genome, many jobs. Every cell in the body carries the same DNA, copied by mitosis from a single zygote — specialisation happens because each cell type translates a different part of it. Potential then narrows on a one-way ladder: totipotent (whole organism + extra-embryonic tissue, briefly) → pluripotent (any body cell, unlimited division) → multipotent / unipotent (a limited range, down to just one cell type — cardiomyocytes). iPS cells are the one artificial way to climb back up that ladder: adult cell + transcription factors → pluripotent again.

Self-check before moving on

  • I can explain why genetically identical cells specialise: same genes present, only some transcribed and translated, different proteins, different structure/function.
  • I can define totipotent, pluripotent, multipotent and unipotent as an ordered hierarchy, and I never confuse totipotent (whole organism + extra-embryonic tissue, brief window) with pluripotent (any body cell, never a whole organism).
  • I can give the full two-part definition of a stem cell: divides/replicates indefinitely, AND differentiates.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Same DNA, different cells

  • All cells contain identical DNA (mitosis from one zygote) — specialisation is never from a different set of genes being present.
  • Each cell type translates only part of its DNA — this exact phrase is what's credited, not "uses genes differently."
  • Chain: same genes present → only some transcribed/translated → different proteins → different structure/function.
  • No genes are ever lost as a cell specialises — this is selective gene expression, not selective gene content.

2 · The stem-cell hierarchy

  • Stem cell = two halves: divides/replicates indefinitely AND differentiates — both separately credited.
  • Totipotent: any body cell + extra-embryonic tissue, early embryo only, brief window.
  • Pluripotent: any body cell, NOT a whole organism, embryos, unlimited division, treats disorders.
  • Multipotent: limited related range, mature mammals. Unipotent: one cell type only (cardiomyocytes), mature mammals.
  • Most-reversed boundary: only totipotent forms extra-embryonic tissue / a whole organism.

3 · Induced pluripotent (iPS) cells

  • Adult somatic cell + protein transcription factors → pluripotency genes switched on → cell becomes pluripotent → differentiates into required cell type.
  • Advantages (capped at 3): permanent effect, no donor needed, no embryo destroyed, reduced immune rejection.
  • State the ethical contrast explicitly: no embryo used, no donor needed.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Same DNA, different cells

❓ A student is shown a liver cell and a muscle cell from the same organism, and told both contain identical DNA. Explain why the two cells have different structures.
Both cells contain the same genes, because they arose by mitosis from the same original cell. In each cell type, only some of those genes are transcribed and translated — the rest remain switched off. This produces different proteins in the liver cell and the muscle cell, giving each a different structure suited to its own function.
❓ A student writes: "the two cells are different because they use their genes differently." Rewrite this using the wording that earns full credit.
The two cells translate only part of their DNA, and it is a different part in each cell type — this selective translation, not a difference in which genes are present, is what causes cell specialisation.
❓ Explain why it would be incorrect to say that a skin cell has "lost" the genes needed to become a neurone.
Every cell in the organism retains the complete genome, because DNA content does not change as a cell specialises. The skin cell still contains the genes associated with neurones — they are simply not transcribed and translated in a skin cell, not physically absent.

The stem-cell hierarchy

❓ Give the full definition of a stem cell.
A stem cell is a cell that can divide / replicate indefinitely, and can differentiate into other, specialised cell types — both parts of this definition are needed for full credit.
❓ A cell can develop into any type of body cell, but cannot form a whole new organism. Name this category of cell, and explain how you know it is not totipotent.
This is a pluripotent cell. It is not totipotent because totipotent cells can additionally form extra-embryonic tissue (such as the placenta) and so can produce a whole organism, whereas pluripotent cells are restricted to body cells only and cannot do this.
❓ Compare multipotent and unipotent stem cells, giving the named example the specification uses for one of them.
Multipotent cells, found in mature mammals, can divide to form a limited number of different, related cell types. Unipotent cells, also found in mature mammals, can produce only one cell type — the named example is cardiomyocytes (heart muscle cells).

Induced pluripotent (iPS) cells

❓ Describe how an induced pluripotent stem cell is produced from an adult somatic cell.
The adult somatic cell is treated with specific protein transcription factors; these switch on the genes associated with pluripotency, so the cell becomes pluripotent and can then differentiate into the required specialised cell type.
❓ Give three advantages of using iPS cells rather than embryonic stem cells to treat a genetic disorder.
Any three of: a permanent effect, avoiding the need for repeated treatment; no donor is required, since the patient's own cells are reprogrammed; no embryo is destroyed, avoiding the main ethical objection to embryonic stem cells; reduced chance of immune rejection, because the cells are genetically the patient's own.
❓ Explain why iPS cells are described as avoiding the ethical debate that surrounds embryonic stem cell research.
iPS cells are produced from the patient's own adult somatic cells using transcription factors, so no embryo is used or destroyed at any stage — this sidesteps the central objection to embryonic stem cell use, that an embryo's potential to become a person is removed without consent.
