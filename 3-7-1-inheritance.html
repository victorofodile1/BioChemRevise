⚠️

The single habit that protects the most marks on this spec point: whenever a cross doesn't come out as the standard 9:3:3:1 (dihybrid) or 3:1 (monohybrid) ratio, work out and name which mechanism explains the numbers you were actually given — linkage, sex linkage, epistasis or a lethal allele — rather than forcing the familiar ratio onto data that clearly doesn't fit it. This spec point is examined at A-level only (not the AS paper), and it is consistently one of the most content-dense single points in the whole course, so a little organisation here goes a long way.

Section 1 · spec content point

🧬 Genotype — an organism's genetic constitution

Every genetics question on this spec point ultimately rests on getting this definition, and the next one, exactly right. They're worth learning as a precise pair.

📖

What the specification says: "The genotype is the genetic constitution of an organism."

1
2
3

Think of every living thing as carrying a little instruction card inside each of its cells. That card lists which specific versions of each instruction it has — even before you can see what those instructions actually build. That list of versions is the genotype. Two pea plants could carry different cards (different genotypes) even if, from the outside, they end up looking the same.

  • The instruction card = the genotype
  • Each line on the card = a gene
  • The specific version written on that line = an allele

Genotype means the specific combination of alleles an organism carries — not its whole set of DNA (that's the genome), and not what it looks like (that's the phenotype, next section). A genotype is usually written as a short pair of letters for whichever gene a question is focused on, for example Tt for a pea plant carrying one tall allele and one dwarf allele. The genotype exists whether or not you can see its effect — a heterozygote carrying a hidden recessive allele still has that allele written into its genotype.

TermWhat it actually refers to
GenomeAll of the DNA / all of the genes in an organism
GenotypeThe specific alleles present, usually for one gene (or a small number) relevant to the question
PhenotypeWhat is actually expressed/observed (Section 2)

The genotype is the genetic constitution of an organism — in practice, the specific alleles it carries at the locus (or loci) a question is asking about. It is written as a pair of allele symbols, one from each parent, following whatever symbol convention the question defines — for example Tt for a pea plant heterozygous for stem height. Genotype is distinct from genome (an organism's entire complement of DNA/genes) and from phenotype (what is actually expressed — Section 2).

A genotype can be homozygous (two identical alleles) or heterozygous (two different alleles — see Section 5), and this is exactly what determines, together with the dominance relationship between the two alleles (Section 4), what phenotype results.

📌

Notation discipline: once a question defines its symbols (e.g. "T = tall, t = dwarf"), keep using exactly those letters, in exactly that case, throughout your diagram and your written answer. Switching to your own letters, or swapping upper/lower case partway through, is a precision error worth avoiding entirely — pick your habit now and stick to it under exam pressure.

▶ 🚩 Trap — genotype is not the same as "genes" or "genome"

A genotype answer that just says "the genes an organism has" is imprecise — a gene is the general instruction (e.g. the gene for stem height); the genotype is the specific alleles present at that gene's locus (e.g. Tt). Likewise, don't confuse genotype with genome: the genome is the organism's entire set of DNA, while a genotype conversation is almost always scoped to one gene, or a handful of genes, relevant to the cross or scenario in the question.

Section 2 · spec content point

👁️ Phenotype — genotype expressed, environment included

This definition has two halves, and both are separately worth marks — dropping the second half is one of the most common ways this spec point loses easy credit.

📖

What the specification says: "The phenotype is the expression of this genetic constitution and its interaction with the environment."

1
2
3

Imagine baking a cake from a recipe card (that's the genotype). The finished cake you actually get — its taste, its height, whether it's a bit burnt — depends on the recipe and on things like your oven, your ingredients, and how long you left it in. The actual cake that comes out is the phenotype: it's the recipe, played out under real conditions.

Phenotype is what you can actually observe or measure about an organism — its physical characteristics, or biochemical/behavioural traits. It comes from two things combined: the genotype (which sets out what's possible) and the environment the organism experiences (which affects how that potential is actually expressed). Two pea plants with identical genotypes for height can still end up different heights if one gets much less light or fewer nutrients than the other — so phenotype is never genotype alone.

Phenotype is the expression of the genotype and its interaction with the environment — both halves of this definition are independently credited, so an answer that stops at "the expression of the genotype" is incomplete. Simply describing "what an organism looks like" is not accepted as a definition of phenotype on its own, because it leaves out the causal explanation entirely.

The environmental half matters biologically, not just for the mark: features like plant height, leaf size, and many continuously-varying (polygenic) traits are visibly shaped by light availability, nutrient supply, temperature and other conditions, on top of whatever the genotype allows. This is also why identical genotypes (e.g. clones, or identical twins) can still show different phenotypes.

ComponentContributes…
GenotypeThe range of what's genetically possible for that characteristic
EnvironmentHow much of that genetic potential is actually expressed in this particular organism, in these particular conditions
PhenotypeThe observed result of both acting together
▶ 🚩 Trap — dropping the environment clause

The phenotype mark is regularly lost by candidates who write only "the characteristics an organism shows" or "what an organism looks like" — this misses the causal explanation the definition actually requires. Always include both halves: expression of the genotype, and its interaction with the environment. Treat the phrase "interaction with the environment" as a fixed, separately-credited part of the definition, not an optional extra.

Section 3 · spec content point

🩸 Multiple alleles — one gene, many versions across a population

A gene can have far more than two versions once you look across an entire population — the trap is forgetting that any single individual still only ever carries two.

📖

What the specification says: "There may be many alleles of a single gene."

1
2
3

Picture one style of shoe that comes in lots of colours — red, blue, green, and more. The whole shop (the whole population) stocks every colour. But you, personally, only own two shoes at once (one gene, two alleles per person) — you might own a red one and a blue one, but you can't be wearing three colours on two feet.

Most genes only have two alleles floating around a population (like tall/dwarf), but some genes have three or more alleles across the whole population — this is called multiple alleles. The classic example is human ABO blood group: there are three alleles for this gene across the population, usually written IA, IB and IO. Even though there are three alleles available at the population level, any one person still only carries two of them (one from each parent).

Multiple alleles means a single gene has more than two allele versions present across a population, even though any one diploid individual still only ever carries two of them (one per homologous chromosome). The standard worked example is the ABO blood-group gene: it has three alleles, IA and IB (which are codominant to each other — see Section 4), and IO (recessive to both).

GenotypePhenotype (blood group)
IAIA or IAIOA
IBIB or IBIOB
IAIBAB — both alleles expressed (codominance)
IOIOO
▶ 🚩 Trap — writing three alleles into one genotype

The ABO example is specifically testing whether you remember that any one individual still only has two alleles, even though three exist in the population — never write all three alleles (IA, IB, IO) into a single person's genotype. Keep multiple alleles (a population-level idea) conceptually separate from codominance (a heterozygote expressing both of its own two alleles — Section 4).

Section 4 · spec content point

⚖️ Dominant, recessive and codominant alleles

These three relationships between a pair of alleles decide exactly what a heterozygote's phenotype will look like — and codominance in particular is very easy to describe imprecisely.

📖

What the specification says: "Alleles may be dominant, recessive or codominant."

1
2
3

Imagine two people in a room, one with a loud voice and one with a quiet voice. If the loud one (dominant) is there, you only ever hear them — the quiet one might as well not be talking. The quiet one (recessive) only gets heard if the loud one isn't in the room at all. But sometimes both voices are equally loud (codominant) — you hear both of them clearly, at the same time, not blended into some new in-between voice.

A dominant allele shows up in the phenotype whenever it's present — even with just one copy (heterozygous). A recessive allele only shows up when there's no dominant allele around to mask it, i.e. when there are two copies of it (homozygous). A codominant allele doesn't get masked by the other allele at all — instead, a heterozygote shows both versions of the characteristic at once, fully, side by side. This is different from "blending," where you'd expect some halfway, in-between characteristic instead.

Classic example: roan cattle. Red coat colour (CR) and white coat colour (CW) are codominant — the heterozygote CRCW is roan, meaning it has patches of red hair and patches of white hair, not a pink/blended colour.

RelationshipWhat happens in a heterozygoteWorked example
DominantIts characteristic appears in the phenotype even when only one copy is presentTall (T) in pea plants — Tt is tall
RecessiveIts characteristic only appears in the phenotype when homozygous (no dominant allele present to mask it)Dwarf (t) in pea plants — only tt is dwarf
CodominantBoth alleles are expressed in the phenotype — neither masks the otherRoan cattle, CRCW (red and white hairs both present); AB blood group

By convention, dominant alleles are usually given an upper-case letter and recessive alleles the corresponding lower-case letter (e.g. T/t), while codominant alleles are usually written with a shared base letter and a superscript to distinguish them (e.g. CR/CW, IA/IB) — but always follow whatever notation a specific question actually gives you, rather than defaulting to your own habit.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "stronger allele," and codominance vs blending

Describing a dominant allele as "the allele that is stronger" or "overrides" the other is imprecise — the correct explanation is about which characteristic is expressed in the phenotype, not about one allele being biologically more powerful. And codominance is not the same as blending: in codominance, both phenotypes are fully, separately visible in the heterozygote (e.g. distinct red and white hairs); blending would produce a single intermediate phenotype (e.g. uniformly pink), which is not what codominance describes.

Section 5 · spec content point

🧩 Homozygous and heterozygous — one locus, two alleles

A short definition, but one that underpins how every genetic diagram from Section 6 onward gets set up.

📖

What the specification says: "In a diploid organism, the alleles at a specific locus may be either homozygous or heterozygous."

1
2
3

Think about a pair of socks. If both socks in the pair are identical, that's like being homozygous — two matching alleles. If the two socks don't match — one stripy, one plain — that's like being heterozygous — two different alleles for the same "sock slot".

A diploid organism (like a human or a pea plant) has two copies of every chromosome, so it has two alleles at every gene's locus — its fixed position on the chromosome. If those two alleles are identical, the organism is homozygous at that locus (e.g. TT or tt). If the two alleles are different, it's heterozygous (e.g. Tt). What the organism actually looks like at that locus (its phenotype) depends on the dominance relationship between whichever two alleles it happens to be heterozygous or homozygous for (Section 4).

TermDefinition
LocusThe fixed position of a gene on a chromosome
HomozygousCarrying two identical alleles of a gene at its locus (e.g. TT, tt, IOIO)
HeterozygousCarrying two different alleles of a gene at its locus (e.g. Tt, CRCW, IAIB)

A heterozygote's phenotype is entirely determined by the dominance relationship between its two alleles: if one allele is dominant and the other recessive, only the dominant characteristic shows (Section 4); if the two alleles are codominant, both characteristics show together. A homozygote's phenotype is unambiguous either way, since both alleles carry the same instruction.

▶ 🚩 Trap — locus vs gene vs allele

Keep these three terms distinct in an answer: the locus is a position on a chromosome; the gene is the general instruction found at that position; an allele is one specific version of that gene. "Homozygous" and "heterozygous" describe the relationship between the two alleles present at one locus in one individual — they are not properties of a population (that's what "multiple alleles," Section 3, describes instead).

Section 6 · spec content point

🧮 Monohybrid and dihybrid crosses — building the diagram

Genetic diagrams are marked stage by stage, so a diagram that's unfinished but correctly staged still scores partial credit — the method below is worth over-learning until it's automatic.

📖

What the specification says: "The use of fully labelled genetic diagrams to interpret, or predict, the results of: monohybrid and dihybrid crosses involving dominant, recessive and codominant alleles." (This is the first half of one longer specification bullet — the second half, covering sex-linkage, autosomal linkage, multiple alleles and epistasis, is the same content point continued in Section 7.)

1
2
3

A genetic diagram is just a recipe for working out every possible combination of two parents' "instruction cards" for their children. First you say what each letter means, then you write down each parent's cards, then you list every card each parent could hand down (a gamete), then you match them up in every possible pairing, and finally you count up how many children would look which way.

Building a genetic diagram always follows the same five moves: say what each letter stands for; write the parents' genotypes; work out the gametes each parent can make; combine those gametes in every possible pairing (usually in a grid — a Punnett square); then read off the ratio of resulting phenotypes. A dihybrid cross is exactly the same idea, just tracking two genes — and two unlinked genes independently assorting, both parents heterozygous at both, always gives the same 9:3:3:1 pattern.

The five-stage method

  1. Define the symbols for each allele — state clearly which letter stands for which phenotype.
  2. State the parental genotypes.
  3. Show the gametes each parent can produce (conventionally circled, every type shown — four types for a dihybrid cross).
  4. Combine the gametes in a Punnett square (or equivalent grid) to get the offspring genotypes.
  5. Read off the resulting phenotypic ratio.
Click a numbered box, or use Next / Back above, to step through the five stages — the caption stays in sync with the highlighted box.

A codominant monohybrid cross

Cattle coat colour: CR (red) and CW (white) are codominant; the heterozygote CRCW is roan (both colours of hair present, not blended pink). Crossing two roan cattle, CRCW × CRCW, gives offspring genotypes 1 CRCR : 2 CRCW : 1 CWCW — phenotypes 1 red : 2 roan : 1 white, a 1:2:1 ratio, distinct from the 3:1 of a simple dominant/recessive cross, because the heterozygote now has its own third phenotype.

A dihybrid cross — RrYy × RrYy

A dihybrid cross tracks two genes at once. Provided the two genes assort independently (are unlinked — Section 7 covers what happens when they aren't), a cross between two double heterozygotes always gives the same underlying pattern. Pea seed shape (R = round, dominant; r = wrinkled, recessive) and seed colour (Y = yellow, dominant; y = green, recessive) are unlinked; each RrYy parent produces four gamete types — RY, Ry, rY, ry — and combining them in a 4×4 Punnett square gives 9 round yellow : 3 round green : 3 wrinkled yellow : 1 wrinkled green out of 16 boxes — the classic 9:3:3:1 dihybrid ratio.

Recognise the standard ratios on sight

RatioWhat produces it
3:1Monohybrid cross, two heterozygotes (Aa × Aa), full dominance
1:2:1Monohybrid cross, two heterozygotes, codominance (heterozygote has its own phenotype)
1:1Test cross (Aa × aa) — used to determine an unknown dominant genotype
9:3:3:1Dihybrid cross, two unlinked genes, both parents heterozygous at both loci
1:1:1:1Dihybrid test cross, unlinked genes (AaBb × aabb)

The epistasis ratios (9:3:4, 9:7, 12:3:1) and the lethal-allele ratio (2:1) belong to Section 7, since they only appear once one of those extra mechanisms is involved.

▶ 🚩 The zero-mark trap — wrong parental genotypes

If the very first genotypes you write down (stage 2) are wrong, everything staged afterwards from them scores nothing, however well the rest of the diagram is drawn — incorrect parental genotypes can void the whole question. Always double-check the parental genotypes before building the gametes, the grid, or the ratio, and never skip straight to writing the final ratio without showing the staged working — each stage is credited separately.

Section 7 · spec content point

🔗 Sex linkage, autosomal linkage and epistasis in genetic diagrams

These three mechanisms are the reasons a cross can quietly stop giving 9:3:3:1 or 3:1 — and being handed a set of numbers and working out which of the three explains them, without being told, is the single hardest recurring skill on this spec point.

📖

What the specification says: "The use of fully labelled genetic diagrams to interpret, or predict, the results of: crosses involving sex-linkage, autosomal linkage, multiple alleles and epistasis." (Same specification content point as Section 6 — this half covers the four named complications, plus applying multiple alleles, Section 3, inside a full diagram.)

1
2
3

Sex linkage: some instruction cards live specifically on the "girl/boy" chromosome (X), so whether you get one copy or two of that card depends on whether you're male or female — this is why some conditions affect far more boys than girls. Autosomal linkage: two cards that sit right next to each other on the same chromosome usually get handed down together as a pair, like they're stapled — only occasionally do they come unstapled. Epistasis: sometimes one instruction card completely overrides whether a second card's instruction can even be seen at all — like a "power switch" card that has to be turned on before a second card's colour choice matters.

A sex-linked gene sits on the X chromosome (the Y carries very few genes), so males — who only have one X — need just one copy of a recessive allele to show the condition, while females need two. Autosomal linkage happens when two genes sit close together on the same non-sex chromosome, so they're usually inherited as a pair rather than independently — a cross gives mostly the two original ("parental") combinations, with only a small number of new ("recombinant") combinations produced by crossing over. Epistasis is when one gene's alleles decide whether a second gene's alleles get expressed at all, usually because the first gene acts earlier in a biochemical pathway.

Sex linkage — the X/Y notation this spec point wants

A sex-linked gene sits on the X chromosome, because the Y chromosome carries very few genes of its own. Genotypes must show this explicitly — for a condition controlled by allele pair H/h: XHXH and XHXh females are unaffected (the second is a carrier); XhXh females are affected; XHY males are unaffected; XhY males are affected.

A recessive sex-linked condition is far more common in males, because males are hemizygous for the X chromosome — they carry only one allele of the gene (the Y has no matching allele there), so a single recessive allele is always expressed. A female needs two copies (homozygous recessive) to be affected.

Genotype ratio 1 XHXH : 1 XHXh : 1 XHY : 1 XhY. No daughters are affected here, but half of all sons are — the classic sex-linked signature: a condition that skips generations in females but appears whenever a carrier mother has sons.
▶ 🚩 Trap — "one gene" instead of "hemizygous," and missing the X

Saying "males only have one gene" is rejected — the correct term is hemizygous: males have only one allele of the gene, because they only have one X chromosome. Genotype notation for sex-linked characteristics must show the X explicitly with a superscript allele (XHXh, XhY) — writing "Hh" as if the gene were autosomal is marked down even when the underlying logic is otherwise correct.

Autosomal linkage — why linked genes break the 9:3:3:1 ratio

A dihybrid cross between two genes that are autosomally linked (on the same non-sex chromosome, close enough together to usually travel together) does not give 9:3:3:1. Instead, the offspring data shows two large classes (the parental-type phenotypes) and two small classes (the recombinant-type phenotypes) — because the genes are usually inherited together, and only crossing over during meiosis I occasionally separates them.

Because A/a and B/b are linked, most gametes carry the original ("parental") combinations AB and ab. Crossing over between the two loci only occasionally falls between them, producing a small minority of recombinant gametes, Ab and aB — the closer together two genes sit, the rarer this crossover, and the smaller the recombinant minority.

▶ 🚩 The single most cited error on this sub-point

When data doesn't fit 9:3:3:1, forcing a standard dihybrid ratio onto data that actually shows evidence of gene linkage is the single most cited error here — always look for the tell-tale two-large/two-small pattern and the small recombinant classes instead of defaulting to the familiar ratio. Also note: "the genes are on the sex chromosomes" is not the same claim as autosomal linkage — autosomal specifically means not a sex chromosome.

Multiple alleles inside a full genetic diagram

The ABO blood-group example from Section 3 works exactly like any other monohybrid cross once you're inside a diagram — you're just choosing two of the three population-level alleles for each parent. For example, a cross between a group-A parent (IAIO) and a group-B parent (IBIO) gives gametes IA/IO and IB/IO, and offspring genotypes IAIB (group AB), IAIO (group A), IBIO (group B) and IOIO (group O) in a 1:1:1:1 ratio — the same five-stage method as any monohybrid cross, just with three possible allele symbols to choose from instead of two.

Epistasis — one gene masking another

Epistasis is the interaction of two genes at different loci, where the expression of one gene is masked or suppressed by the alleles of the other — this is different from ordinary (non-epistatic) dihybrid inheritance, where the two genes are simply independent of each other. It's usually explained through a biochemical pathway: one gene's product acts earlier in the pathway than the other's, so if that earlier step fails (e.g. a non-functional enzyme means no pigment is made at all), the later gene's alleles can never be expressed, whatever they are.

TypeMechanismWorked example
Recessive epistasis (9:3:4)Being homozygous recessive for the epistatic gene masks the second gene's phenotype, replacing it with one extra phenotypeCoat colour: E/e controls whether pigment is deposited at all; ee is epistatic to B/b and gives yellow regardless of the B/b genotype
Dominant epistasis (12:3:1)One dominant allele of the epistatic gene masks the second gene's phenotype entirelySummer squash colour: a dominant allele at one gene gives white regardless of the second gene; only the double recessive at that gene lets colour (yellow/green) show
Duplicate recessive (complementary) epistasis (9:7)A dominant allele is needed at both genes to produce the phenotype; recessive at either gene alone gives the same alternative phenotypeFlower colour in sweet peas: pigment needs a dominant allele at both of two genes in the same pathway; homozygous recessive at either gene gives white
Gene B/b decides which pigment enzyme is made — but gene E/e decides whether any pigment is deposited at all. If an individual is ee, the pathway is blocked before pigment is ever made, so the coat is yellow whatever the B/b genotype is: this is why ee is described as epistatic to B/b.
▶ 🚩 Trap — epistasis mistaken for ordinary dihybrid inheritance or codominance

Epistasis is often mislabelled as "dihybrid inheritance" or "codominance" when a gene-interaction question actually describes one gene's expression masking another's. Even when every genotype and phenotype in a completed diagram is correct, it's still common to give the wrong final ratio by defaulting to a memorised 1:1:1:1 or 9:3:3:1 — always derive the ratio from your own completed diagram, never recall it from memory alone.

Section 8 · spec content point

📐 The chi-squared (χ²) test

Chi-squared answers the question every departure-from-ratio question is secretly asking: is this gap between what I expected and what I actually got just chance, or is something real going on?

📖

What the specification says: "Use of the chi-squared (χ²) test to compare the goodness of fit of observed phenotypic ratios with expected ratios."

1
2
3

Imagine you predicted 9 red sweets out of every 16, but your actual bag has only 7. Is that just bad luck from picking a small bag, or is something genuinely different going on? Chi-squared is a way of turning "how far off was I" into one single number, then comparing that number against a table to decide: was this gap probably just luck, or is it big enough that something real explains it?

Chi-squared compares the numbers you actually counted (observed) with the numbers you predicted from a ratio (expected). You work out how far off each group was, square it so negative and positive differences don't cancel out, divide by what was expected, and add all of those up into one number. Then you compare that number with a "critical value" from a table — if your number is bigger than the critical value, the difference is too big to be chance, so you reject your original ratio; if it's smaller, the difference is small enough to just be chance, and your ratio still stands.

The formula and the method

🧮
χ² = Σ [ (O − E)² ÷ E ]
O = observed count · E = expected count (from the hypothesised ratio) · Σ = sum across every phenotype class
  1. Calculate the expected numbers for each class from the hypothesised ratio and the total sample size.
  2. Work out (O − E)² ÷ E for each class, and sum them to get χ².
  3. State the degrees of freedom = number of phenotype classes − 1.
  4. Compare the calculated χ² against the critical value at p = 0.05, for the correct degrees of freedom.
  5. State the conclusion, in the correct direction (see below).

Worked example — testing a suspected linked-gene cross

A dihybrid cross predicted to follow 9:3:3:1 (H₀: the two genes assort independently) produced 200 offspring. If the genes were actually linked, the two parental classes should come out higher than expected and the two recombinant classes lower.

Conclusion: χ² calculated (14.76) is greater than χ² critical (7.82) at p = 0.05 with 3 degrees of freedom, so the difference is significant — reject H₀. The probability that a difference this big is due to chance alone is less than 0.05 (5%). The observed ratio does not fit the hypothesised 9:3:3:1 ratio — something besides chance is acting on these results, consistent with the two genes being autosomally linked rather than assorting independently.

▶ 🚩 The comparison direction — reversed under pressure more than almost anything else in the course

Get the direction exactly right: calculated χ² greater than the critical value means the difference is significant (reject H₀ — the data does not fit the hypothesised ratio); calculated χ² less than the critical value means the difference is not significant (accept H₀ — chance alone explains it, and the data does fit). Reversing this is one of the most commonly made errors in the whole course. Precise wording matters too — the conclusion should state "the probability that the difference is due to chance is less than 0.05 / 5%," not just "it is significant." The degrees-of-freedom step is often credited independently of the final numerical answer, so always write it out explicitly even under time pressure.

Section 10 · exam technique

🕵️ Diagnosing an unusual ratio, probability & pedigree analysis

Three closely related application skills that come up constantly on this spec point but aren't a single named spec bullet on their own — they're the practical toolkit you apply once Sections 1–8 are secure.

Diagnosing which mechanism explains a set of offspring numbers

The hardest version of this spec point's questions hands you a set of offspring counts that don't fit 9:3:3:1 (or 3:1) and asks you to work out why, without naming the mechanism in the question. Work out the ratio the numbers actually show, compare it against the reference list below, and only then explain the biology behind whichever one matches.

What the data actually showsLikely mechanism
Two large classes, two small classes, instead of four roughly equal onesAutosomal linkage
A far higher proportion of one sex affected than the otherSex linkage
Four phenotype classes collapsing into three or fewer, unequal-sized groups (9:3:4, 9:7, 12:3:1)Epistasis
One expected class is simply missing, survivors in a 2:1 ratioA lethal allele — the homozygous class dies before it can be counted
A small, roughly proportional deviation, close to but not exactly the expected ratioChance / small sample size — use chi-squared (Section 8) to check

Probability rules for genetic crosses

  • Express an answer exactly as the question asks — a fraction, decimal, percentage or ratio.
  • For two independent events (e.g. the sex of a baby and whether it inherits a particular allele), use the multiplication rule: P(both) = P(event 1) × P(event 2).
  • Each fertilisation is independent of every other one — a family having already had three girls does not change the probability that the next child is a girl.
▶ 🚩 The conditional-probability trap

Where a question gives you a phenotype rather than a genotype, the denominator is restricted to the surviving/relevant class only. Worked mini-example, from a 3:1 ratio (1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa): "given that an offspring shows the dominant phenotype, what is the probability it is heterozygous?" — restrict to the 3 dominant-phenotype individuals only (1 AA : 2 Aa within that group), so the answer is 2/3, not 2/4.

Reading a pedigree (family tree)

Reading a family tree to establish the mode of inheritance follows a small set of rules: an affected child with two unaffected parents is strong evidence the allele is recessive (both parents must be carriers); a condition seen in every generation is more consistent with dominant inheritance; a condition affecting far more males than females, and never passed from an affected father to an affected son, points to X-linked recessive inheritance.

Pattern in the pedigreePoints towards
Two unaffected parents, an affected childRecessive (both parents are carriers)
Every affected individual has an affected parentDominant
Mostly males affected; never father-to-son transmission; an affected daughter needs an affected fatherX-linked recessive
Roughly equal in both sexes; father-to-son transmission occursAutosomal
▶ 🚩 Trap — describing evidence without naming alleles

Pedigree questions are often poorly answered because candidates pick the wrong individuals as their supporting evidence, or describe the evidence purely in terms of individuals/phenotypes without ever explicitly referring to alleles. Always justify an answer by citing specific individuals on the pedigree (by their given number/letter) and naming the allele: "individual 4 must be heterozygous, XHXh, because…" — never just "the trait" or "the gene."

Section 11 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

3.7.1 is almost always the paragraph that turns a molecular cause, or a source of new allele combinations, into something observably inherited. State explicitly which named mechanism is doing the work, rather than leaving "inheritance" as an unexplained black box.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around variation and diversity

Genotype — the alleles an organism carries — is expressed as phenotype, the result of the genotype interacting with the environment; alleles may be dominant, recessive or codominant, and a gene may have multiple alleles across a population, such as the ABO blood group. Genetic diagrams — monohybrid and dihybrid crosses — show how combinations of alleles at one or more loci produce characteristic phenotypic ratios among offspring, and departures from those ratios, via linkage, sex linkage or epistasis, reveal how genes interact and are physically arranged on chromosomes. This shows how the genotypic variation generated by mutation and meiosis is translated into observable phenotypic variation, and reliably passed on to offspring.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around inherited disease

Whether a mutated allele causes heritable disease depends on the dominant/recessive/codominant relationship at that locus — for example, a recessive condition requires two copies to be expressed. Genetic diagrams predict the probability of offspring inheriting a disease-causing allele, and pedigree analysis can establish the mode of inheritance (dominant, recessive, or sex-linked). Because males are hemizygous for the X chromosome, a recessive sex-linked condition is expressed far more often in males — showing how the pattern of inheritance of a mutated allele determines whether, and how, a genetic disease is passed on and expressed within a family.

Whichever essay theme this content supports, treat it as the "mechanism" paragraph: state the process precisely — naming the dominance relationship, the linkage, or the epistasis involved, depending on what the title is asking about — then add one sentence linking it explicitly back to that theme.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Genotype

  • Genotype = the genetic constitution of an organism — the alleles it carries.
  • Not the same as genome (all DNA) or phenotype (what's expressed).
  • Keep whatever symbol convention the question gives you throughout.

2 · Phenotype

  • Phenotype = expression of the genotype and its interaction with the environment — both halves needed for the mark.
  • "What it looks like" alone is never accepted.

3 · Multiple alleles

  • A gene can have 3+ alleles across a population; any one individual still only carries two.
  • ABO: IA and IB codominant to each other, IO recessive to both.

4 · Dominant, recessive, codominant

  • Dominant: shown even when heterozygous. Recessive: shown only when homozygous.
  • Codominant: both alleles fully expressed together (e.g. roan cattle) — never "blending."
  • Never call dominant "the stronger allele."

5 · Homozygous / heterozygous

  • Homozygous = two identical alleles at a locus; heterozygous = two different alleles.
  • A heterozygote's phenotype depends entirely on the dominance relationship between its two alleles.

6 · Monohybrid & dihybrid crosses

  • Five stages every time: symbols → parental genotypes → gametes → Punnett square → ratio.
  • Wrong parental genotypes can void the whole question — check them first.
  • Know: 3:1, 1:2:1, 1:1, 9:3:3:1, 1:1:1:1 on sight.

7 · Sex linkage, autosomal linkage & epistasis

  • Sex linkage: males hemizygous (one allele only) — always write the X, e.g. XHY. Never "one gene."
  • Autosomal linkage: two large (parental) + two small (recombinant) classes, not 9:3:3:1. Crossing over makes the recombinants.
  • Epistasis ratios: 9:3:4 (recessive), 12:3:1 (dominant), 9:7 (duplicate recessive) — one gene masks another via a pathway.
  • Always derive the ratio from your own diagram, never from memory.

8 · The chi-squared test

  • χ² = Σ (O − E)² ÷ E. df = classes − 1. Compare against the critical value at p = 0.05.
  • Calculated > critical → significant → reject H₀. Calculated < critical → not significant → accept H₀.
  • Say "probability the difference is due to chance is less than 5%," not just "significant."
🧠

If it's not 9:3:3:1 or 3:1, name what's stopping it. Two large + two small = linkage. One sex more affected = sex linkage. Fewer than four classes = epistasis. Missing class, 2:1 = lethal allele. χ²: calculated > critical → reject H₀.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Genotype

❓ Define the term genotype.
Genotype is the genetic constitution of an organism — the alleles it carries at the locus, or loci, in question.
❓ A student writes "the genotype is the genes an organism has." Explain why this loses the mark, and give a full-credit version.
"Genes" describes the general instruction present (e.g. the gene for seed colour), not the specific alleles carried; genotype refers to the specific combination of alleles present at that gene's locus, e.g. Yy.

Phenotype

❓ Define the term phenotype.
Phenotype is the expression of the genotype and its interaction with the environment.
❓ Two genetically identical plants grown in different light conditions reach different heights. Explain this in terms of genotype and phenotype.
Both plants share the same genotype for height, but phenotype is the expression of the genotype and its interaction with the environment; the difference in light availability is an environmental factor that alters how much of the genetic potential for height is expressed, producing different phenotypes from identical genotypes.

Multiple alleles

❓ Explain what is meant by "multiple alleles," using the ABO blood group gene as an example.
Multiple alleles means a gene has more than two allele forms present across a population; the ABO gene has three alleles, IA and IB, which are codominant to each other, and IO, which is recessive to both — although any single individual still only carries two of these three alleles.
❓ Give the possible genotypes for a person with blood group A.
IAIA (homozygous) or IAIO (heterozygous).

Dominant, recessive, codominant

❓ Explain the difference between codominance and incomplete blending, using roan cattle coat colour as an example.
In codominance, the heterozygote shows both phenotypes fully and separately — a roan cow has patches of both red and white hair, because both alleles code for a different functional product and both are expressed; this is different from blending, which would produce a single intermediate phenotype such as a uniform pink coat.
❓ A student describes a dominant allele as "the stronger allele." Rewrite this using precise, full-credit wording.
A dominant allele's characteristic is expressed in the phenotype even when only one copy is present, i.e. in a heterozygote; this is not a description of relative strength between the alleles.

Homozygous & heterozygous

❓ Distinguish between a homozygous and a heterozygous genotype.
A homozygous genotype has two identical alleles of a gene at its locus (e.g. TT or tt); a heterozygous genotype has two different alleles of a gene at its locus (e.g. Tt).
❓ Explain why a heterozygote's phenotype cannot be predicted from its genotype alone, without knowing the dominance relationship between its alleles.
A heterozygote's phenotype depends on the relationship between its two alleles: if one is dominant and one recessive, only the dominant characteristic is expressed; if the two alleles are codominant, both characteristics are expressed together — so the genotype alone is not sufficient without also knowing this relationship.

Monohybrid & dihybrid crosses

❓ Construct a fully labelled genetic diagram for a cross between two pea plants heterozygous for stem height (T = tall, dominant; t = dwarf, recessive).
Symbols: T = tall, t = dwarf. Parental genotypes: Tt × Tt. Gametes: each parent produces T and t gametes. Punnett square gives offspring genotypes 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt. Offspring phenotypes: 3 tall : 1 dwarf.
❓ A dihybrid cross between two plants heterozygous at two unlinked loci (RrYy × RrYy) is set up. State the expected phenotypic ratio and explain why it occurs.
9 : 3 : 3 : 1 (round yellow : round green : wrinkled yellow : wrinkled green), because the two genes assort independently at meiosis, so each of the four gamete types (RY, Ry, rY, ry) from one parent combines with each of the four gamete types from the other with equal probability.
❓ A cross between two roan cattle (CRCW × CRCW) is carried out. Predict the phenotypic ratio of the offspring and explain why it differs from a simple dominant/recessive monohybrid cross.
1 red : 2 roan : 1 white — a 1:2:1 ratio, because CR and CW are codominant, so the heterozygote (roan) has its own distinct phenotype rather than being masked by a dominant allele as in a 3:1 cross.

Sex linkage, autosomal linkage & epistasis

❓ Explain why a recessive sex-linked condition occurs far more frequently in males than in females.
The gene is carried on the X chromosome, and males are hemizygous for the X — they have only one allele of the gene, because the Y chromosome carries no matching allele; a single recessive allele is therefore always expressed in males, whereas a female needs two copies (homozygous recessive) to be affected.
❓ A dihybrid cross between two double heterozygotes gives offspring in a ratio of approximately 9 : 1 : 1 : 9 rather than 9:3:3:1. Explain what this data suggests, and why.
This pattern — two large classes and two small classes — suggests the two genes are autosomally linked rather than assorting independently; because the genes are on the same chromosome, they are usually inherited together, so the parental combinations occur more often than expected and the recombinant combinations, produced only by crossing over between the loci during meiosis I, occur less often than expected.
❓ In a cross between two mice heterozygous at two coat-colour loci, the offspring show a 9:3:4 ratio instead of 9:3:3:1. Explain this result in terms of gene interaction.
This is recessive epistasis: being homozygous recessive at one locus (ee) masks the expression of the second gene (B/b) entirely, producing one additional phenotype (yellow) in place of two of the expected classes, rather than the two genes acting independently as in ordinary dihybrid inheritance.

The chi-squared test

❓ A dihybrid cross expected to give a 9:3:3:1 ratio produces a calculated χ² value of 14.76 with 3 degrees of freedom. The critical value at p = 0.05 is 7.82. State and explain the conclusion.
Since the calculated χ² value (14.76) is greater than the critical value (7.82), the difference between observed and expected results is significant; the null hypothesis is rejected. The probability that a difference this large is due to chance alone is less than 0.05 (5%), so the data does not fit the hypothesised 9:3:3:1 ratio.
❓ Explain how the number of degrees of freedom is calculated for a chi-squared test on a dihybrid cross with four phenotype classes.
Degrees of freedom = number of phenotype classes − 1; with four phenotype classes, degrees of freedom = 4 − 1 = 3.
❓ A monohybrid cross produces a calculated χ² value smaller than the critical value at p = 0.05. State the conclusion.
The difference between observed and expected results is not significant; the null hypothesis is accepted, and the data is considered to fit the expected ratio, with any difference explained by chance alone.
