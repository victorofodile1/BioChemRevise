Water

One cause — water's polarity, and the hydrogen bonds it forms between molecules — produces five separately examined biological properties, plus the water-potential vocabulary that carries straight into osmosis, transport and homeostasis later in the course.

⚠️

The one habit that protects the most marks on this spec point: every property is marked as a paired statement — name the property, with its qualifier, then say what it lets the organism do. "Water has a heat capacity" scores nothing on its own; "water has a high specific heat capacity, so it buffers changes in temperature" scores. Never let a property stand alone without its consequence, and never let "high" or "large" quietly drop off the front of it.

Why water behaves this way — the shared cause behind every property below

A water molecule is polar: it's a bent shape, with a bond angle of about 104.5°, and because the bonding electrons in each O–H covalent bond are shared unevenly, the oxygen carries a slight negative charge (δ⁻) while each hydrogen carries a slight positive charge (δ⁺). The δ⁺ region of one molecule is then attracted to the δ⁻ region of a neighbouring molecule, and this attraction is called a hydrogen bond. Any one hydrogen bond is weak and short-lived on its own — but water forms them in enormous numbers, and it's that huge number of weak bonds, not any single strong one, that gives water every property covered in Sections 1–5.

Left: one water molecule, bent at ≈104.5°, oxygen δ⁻ and each hydrogen δ⁺. Right: the δ⁺ of one molecule is attracted to the δ⁻ of its neighbour, forming a hydrogen bond — every property in Sections 1–5 traces back to this, repeated across huge numbers of molecules.
🔗

Whenever an answer needs to explain why water has a property, rather than just name it, this is the reasoning to reach for: energy is needed to break the very large number of hydrogen bonds between molecules — that single idea explains the high heat capacity, the large latent heat of vaporisation, and cohesion (and the surface tension it produces), all at once.

▶ A confusion worth avoiding: where exactly is the hydrogen bond?

A hydrogen bond never forms within a single water molecule, between its own oxygen and hydrogen — that bond is the covalent O–H bond, and it's a completely different, much stronger thing. A hydrogen bond only ever forms between the δ⁺ of one molecule's hydrogen and the δ⁻ of a neighbouring molecule's oxygen. Keeping "within one molecule = covalent" and "between neighbouring molecules = hydrogen bond" clearly separate avoids one of the most common slips on this topic.

Section 1 · spec content point

⚗️ Water as a metabolite

The smallest of the five properties to write about, and the easiest to lose all the marks on — because naming "metabolite" alone, with no reaction named alongside it, earns nothing.

📖

What the specification says: "Water is a major component of cells. It has several properties that are important in biology. In particular, water: is a metabolite in many metabolic reactions, including condensation and hydrolysis reactions…"

1
2
3

Cells build big molecules by joining smaller ones together, like clicking Lego bricks together — and every time two bricks click together, a tiny drop of water pops out. That's called condensation. When a cell wants to take a big model apart again, it uses a drop of water like a tool to split the bricks back apart — that's hydrolysis. So water is being made and used up all the time inside cells, not just sitting around as a liquid.

  • Metabolite = any molecule made or used up in a cell's chemical reactions
  • Condensation = joining two smaller molecules together and releasing water
  • Hydrolysis = using water to split one bigger molecule into two smaller ones

Water is directly involved as a reactant or product in some of the most common reactions in the cell. Every time a polymer is built up from monomers — a polysaccharide from monosaccharides, a protein from amino acids, a triglyceride from glycerol and fatty acids — a molecule of water is released; this is condensation. Every time that polymer is digested back into its monomers, a molecule of water is used up to break the bond; this is hydrolysis. Water is also produced in respiration, and split apart during the light-dependent reaction of photosynthesis (photolysis), supplying protons, electrons and oxygen.

ProcessWhat water does there
Condensation (building polymers)Released as a product
Hydrolysis (digesting polymers)Used up as a reactant
RespirationProduced, when oxygen accepts electrons and protons at the end of the electron transport chain
Photosynthesis (photolysis)Split apart by light energy, releasing protons, electrons and oxygen

Water sits inside the biochemistry of the cell as an active participant, not a passive medium — it is used in hydrolysis and released in condensation reactions right across metabolism.

Reaction / processRole of waterExample
CondensationA molecule of water is a product, formed as a bond joins two smaller moleculesGlycosidic, peptide and ester bond formation when polymers are built
HydrolysisA molecule of water is a reactant, used to break a bond apartDigestion of every polymer (polysaccharides, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids)
RespirationWater is produced, when oxygen finally accepts electrons and protons at the end of the electron transport chainOxidative phosphorylation
PhotosynthesisWater is split (photolysis) by light energy, releasing protons, electrons and oxygenLight-dependent reaction
▶ 🚩 Trap — "water is a metabolite" on its own

A bare statement that water "is a metabolite" scores nothing by itself. The credited version always names which reaction it's a metabolite in — condensation, hydrolysis, photolysis, or respiration — so the property and its example are always written as one paired statement, never split apart.

Section 2 · spec content point

🧫 Water as a solvent

This property is what makes the rest of metabolism physically possible — reactions need dissolved substances that can actually meet and move.

📖

What the specification says: "…water: is an important solvent in which metabolic reactions occur…"

1
2
3

Water molecules are a bit like tiny magnets, with one slightly positive end and one slightly negative end. That lets them grab onto anything else that's charged and pull it apart into the water, a bit like a stream carrying leaves away — anything charged gets carried off, dissolved. That's why so many of a cell's ingredients can float around and bump into each other inside a watery cytoplasm, and why blood can carry dissolved substances all round the body.

  • Solvent = a liquid that other substances dissolve in
  • Solute = the substance being dissolved
  • Polar = having a slightly positive end and a slightly negative end

Because water is polar, its slightly negative oxygen and slightly positive hydrogens can surround a charged or polar solute from every side, pulling it away from other solute particles and keeping it in solution. This matters in two connected ways: metabolic reactions can only happen once their reactants are dissolved and free to collide with each other, and dissolved substances can then be carried around the body or the plant in a fluid — blood plasma, tissue fluid, xylem sap, phloem sap.

Dissolved substanceWhere it's transported dissolved in water
Glucose, amino acids, ionsBlood plasma, tissue fluid
Mineral ions, e.g. nitrateXylem sap
Sucrose, amino acidsPhloem sap

Because water is polar, it can surround and separate charged and polar solute particles, holding them apart in solution rather than letting them re-form solid crystals or clumps. That single ability is credited two separate ways, and either is enough to earn the mark alongside the property name.

Consequence (either is credited)What it means
Metabolic reactions can occurEnzymes and substrates must be in solution, in the cytoplasm, to collide and react
Substances can be transported dissolved in itIons, glucose and amino acids move dissolved in blood and tissue fluid; solutes move dissolved in xylem and phloem sap
▶ 🚩 Trap — "solvent" with no stated consequence

Naming "solvent" alone is not enough — the pairing needs either "so reactions can occur" or "so substances can be transported", written explicitly. As with every property on this page, the qualifier-free, consequence-free version scores nothing.

Section 3 · spec content point

🌡️ High specific heat capacity

The property most often stated without its qualifier — "heat capacity" alone is a different, uncredited idea from "high specific heat capacity."

📖

What the specification says: "…water: has a relatively high heat capacity, buffering changes in temperature…"

1
2
3

Imagine water molecules holding hands with hydrogen bonds. Before a pool of water can actually get any hotter, a huge number of those hands have to let go first — and that takes a lot of energy and a lot of time. That's why a pond warms up (and cools down) much more slowly than the air above it, which keeps the animals living in it at a much steadier temperature.

  • Specific heat capacity = how much energy it takes to heat something up by a set amount
  • Buffer = to soften or resist a change
  • Denaturation = an enzyme losing its shape (often from too much heat) and stopping working

Water heats up and cools down slowly because a lot of the energy that's added or removed goes into breaking or reforming hydrogen bonds between molecules, rather than straight into speeding up or slowing down the molecules themselves — and it's the speed of the molecules that temperature actually measures. Because there are enormous numbers of hydrogen bonds to break before any real temperature change shows up, water resists sudden temperature swings. That keeps organisms — and the enzymes inside them — inside a stable, workable temperature range.

Much energy must be absorbed to break the very large number of hydrogen bonds between water molecules before the average kinetic energy of the molecules — and so the temperature — can actually rise; the same applies in reverse on cooling. As a result, water's temperature changes slowly relative to the energy gained or lost.

PropertyConsequence (credited pairing)
High (specific) heat capacityBuffers changes in temperature — protects enzymes from denaturation, and provides a thermally stable habitat/internal environment

"Resist" is an accepted alternative wording for "buffer" in this pairing.

▶ 🚩 Trap — confusing this with latent heat of vaporisation

Specific heat capacity and latent heat of vaporisation are two separate, separately named and separately credited properties — one is about raising temperature, the other about changing state. Writing them as if they're the same idea, or naming one while meaning the other, loses the mark even if the underlying explanation (breaking hydrogen bonds) sounds right. Always attach the correct qualifier — "high" for heat capacity, "large" for latent heat of vaporisation — to the correct name.

Section 4 · spec content point

💨 Large latent heat of vaporisation

The single most common wording slip on this whole spec point lives here — the credited term is "vaporisation," never "evaporation."

📖

What the specification says: "…water: has a relatively large latent heat of vaporisation, providing a cooling effect with little loss of water through evaporation…"

1
2
3

When water evaporates off a surface — like sweat off your skin — it carries heat away with it, a bit like tiny hot-air balloons lifting warmth up and off into the air. Because it takes so much energy to break all those hydrogen bonds and turn liquid water into vapour, only a small amount of sweat needs to evaporate to carry away a big amount of heat. That's why sweating cools you down so effectively without you losing much actual water.

Turning liquid water into water vapour takes a large amount of energy, separate from the energy needed just to heat it up, because every hydrogen bond holding a molecule to its neighbours has to be broken completely before that molecule can escape as a gas. That energy is drawn from the surrounding surface as heat, so when water evaporates from skin, a leaf, or a tongue, it removes a disproportionately large amount of heat energy for a relatively small loss of water — an efficient way to cool down.

Much energy is needed to break hydrogen bonds and evaporate water molecules from a surface. Because that energy is taken from the surrounding surface as heat, evaporating even a small mass of water removes a large quantity of heat — a cooling effect achieved with little overall loss of water.

PropertyConsequence (credited pairing)
Large latent heat of vaporisationProvides a cooling effect (through evaporation) — sweating and panting in animals, transpiration in plants
▶ 🚩 Trap — "latent heat of evaporation"

The credited term is latent heat of vaporisation. "Latent heat of evaporation" is not accepted, even though the underlying process by which water actually leaves a surface is evaporation — the name of the property itself is "vaporisation," and mixing the two up is the single most frequent wording error on this spec point.

🎯

The application mark, not just the recall mark: stating "large latent heat of vaporisation" only secures the recall mark. The application mark needs the link made explicit — that evaporating a relatively small mass of water removes a disproportionately large quantity of heat, which is exactly why sweating and panting are effective cooling mechanisms.

Section 5 · spec content point

🔗 Cohesion & surface tension

One mechanism, two separately credited consequences — supporting a column of water inside a plant, and supporting small organisms on top of a puddle.

📖

What the specification says: "…water: has strong cohesion between water molecules; this supports columns of water in the tube-like transport cells of plants and produces surface tension where water meets air."

1
2
3

Water molecules holding hands with each other means they don't want to let go. So if you pull one water molecule up a very thin straw, it drags its neighbours up behind it too, like a conga line — that's how water gets pulled all the way up a tall tree through very thin tubes. At the very top surface of a puddle, all those linked hands make a kind of stretchy "skin," strong enough for a light insect like a pond skater to stand on without falling through.

  • Cohesion = water molecules sticking to each other
  • Adhesion = water sticking to something else, like the wall of a tube
  • Surface tension = the "skin" effect where water meets air
  • Xylem = the plant's tube-like water-transport tissue

Hydrogen bonds don't just link a molecule briefly to its neighbours — they create a real "stickiness" between water molecules called cohesion. This stickiness lets a continuous, unbroken thread of water be pulled all the way up the narrow tubes of a plant's xylem without the column snapping apart, even against gravity. At the surface where water meets air, that same stickiness pulls surface molecules inward and makes the surface behave a bit like a stretched, elastic skin — surface tension — strong enough to support a few small organisms.

Hydrogen bonds hold water molecules to each other — cohesion. Two separate consequences are credited from this one cause:

ConsequenceMechanism
Supports columns of water in the tube-like transport cells of plantsCohesion keeps a continuous water column intact under tension as it is pulled up the xylem — accept "transpiration stream" or "cohesion-tension theory" for this consequence
Produces surface tension where water meets airCohesion pulls surface molecules inward at the air–water interface, creating a resistant surface that can support small organisms

"Cohesion" itself is also accepted as an alternative way of naming the underlying mechanism when a question is really asking about hydrogen bonding in this context.

Two related, creditable extras beyond the named consequences

PropertyCauseConsequence
Ice is less dense than liquid waterHydrogen bonds hold molecules in a fixed, open lattice as ice formsIce floats and insulates the water below, maintaining a stable aquatic habitat beneath it
Water is transparentWater does not absorb visible light stronglyAllows light penetration through water, supporting photosynthesis by aquatic plants and algae

These two are not named in the specification bullet itself, but a valid explanation given alongside either one is creditable as an additional water property. Adhesion — water molecules sticking to the walls of the xylem — works the same way: it isn't a specification requirement in its own right, but it's expected within transport answers, and "cohesion" is also accepted there.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "cohesion" with no consequence, or "transpiration" standing in for it

Naming "cohesion" with nothing else scores nothing — the consequence (supporting the water column, or producing surface tension) always has to be stated too. The bare word "transpiration" is not accepted as a substitute for stating cohesion either, although describing the mechanism of the transpiration stream is accepted. Any reference to pH in this context isn't part of the credited answer and should be left out.

Section 6 · extends Section 2

⚖️ Water potential & osmosis

This isn't its own line in the 3.1.7 spec bullet — it's where the solvent property gets extended into the vocabulary you'll actually be tested on across 3.2, 3.3 and 3.6. It's worth learning here, once, precisely.

Water potential (Ψ, pronounced "psi") is the pressure created by water molecules, measured in kPa. Pure water has a water potential of zero — the maximum possible value. Adding a solute lowers the water potential, so every solution has a negative water potential, and the more concentrated the solution, the more negative it becomes.

Water potential only ever runs from higher (less negative, closer to zero) to lower (more negative) — never the reverse.

Water moves by osmosis from a region of higher (less negative) water potential to a region of lower (more negative) water potential, through a partially permeable membrane. This is a direct extension of the solvent property from Section 2 — because water is the solvent that dissolves a cell's solutes, adding more solute to one side of a membrane is what creates the water potential gradient osmosis then runs down.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "water concentration"

Never write "water concentration" when describing water movement — mark schemes across the whole specification refuse to credit it, in any topic, at any point. Always use "water potential" instead. This is the single most consistently penalised substitution in the entire course, not just on this spec point.

▶ A second common slip — direction confusion

It's easy to treat "high water potential" and "low water potential" as interchangeable labels rather than a genuine gradient, or to describe water moving the wrong way along it. Always state the direction explicitly, in terms of the gradient itself: "water moves from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential" — not a vague "water moves in" or "water moves out."

🔎

This page is the theory that later required practicals depend on, rather than a required practical in its own right. RP3 (water potential of plant tissue) only makes sense once you can state that pure water has Ψ = 0 and that water moves down a water potential gradient — both facts belong here. The transpiration/potometer practicals rest on cohesion and adhesion (Section 5) moving a continuous water column under tension, and on the large latent heat of vaporisation (Section 4) making evaporative water loss from a leaf a cooling process at all.

Section 7 · exam technique

This spec point looks like pure recall, so it's easy to assume it's low-risk — but the qualifiers attached to each property are separately credited, so recall that looks complete on paper often isn't.

▶ Where the difficulty on this topic actually comes from

3.1.7 is a single, short spec point built from five sub-properties — genuinely low in memorisation load — but the qualifiers attached to each property are treated as separately credited, so answers that look complete are often missing the one word that earns the mark. At the deeper level, water-potential questions are a recurring weak spot: students often reach for GCSE-level "wet to dry" language instead of stating a proper water-potential gradient, and treat high and low water potential as if they mean the same thing when explaining osmosis. Neither issue is really about the biology being hard — both come down to precision.

Section 8 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

Water's five properties make a natural opening paragraph for any essay asking about the importance of water to living organisms — every later paragraph then "cashes in" against a named property from this page.

▶ Example opening paragraph

Water is a major component of cells and has several properties that make it essential to life. It acts as a metabolite, being released in condensation reactions and used in hydrolysis reactions; it is an important solvent, allowing metabolic reactions to occur in solution and substances to be transported around the organism; it has a relatively high specific heat capacity, buffering the cell against sudden changes in temperature; and it has a large latent heat of vaporisation, allowing effective cooling through evaporation of only a small volume of water. All of these properties trace back to a single cause — water's polarity, which allows extensive hydrogen bonding between molecules.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around cohesion and transport

Because hydrogen bonds hold water molecules to one another, water shows strong cohesion; this allows a continuous, unbroken column of water to be pulled up the narrow xylem vessels of a plant under tension, without the column breaking, and produces the surface tension seen where water meets air. This illustrates how a single molecular-level property — cohesion between individual molecules — can scale up to explain a whole-organism process, such as long-distance water transport against gravity.

The technique: use 3.1.7 as the essay's opening paragraph, then whenever a later paragraph needs water to move, cool, dissolve or react, name the specific property explicitly — solvent, cohesion, high specific heat capacity, large latent heat of vaporisation — rather than leaving it implicit.

Section 9

🧷 Memory hook & final checklist

🧠

One cause, five hats. Polarity → hydrogen bonding is water's single biological engine — metabolite, solvent, high specific heat capacity, large latent heat of vaporisation, and cohesion are just that one cause wearing five different hats. Name the hat and the head underneath it, every time: never a property without its qualifier, and never a qualifier without its stated consequence.

Self-check before moving on

  • I can explain all five properties of water as paired statements (property + qualifier + consequence), never as bare names.
  • I can explain why water is polar and how that polarity causes hydrogen bonding between neighbouring molecules — and I know a hydrogen bond never forms within a single molecule.
  • I can state water potential's definition precisely (Ψ, kPa, pure water = 0, solute lowers it) without ever writing "water concentration."
  • I can keep "latent heat of vaporisation" and "high specific heat capacity" as two separate, correctly named properties, and never write "latent heat of evaporation."

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Water as a metabolite

  • Released in condensation, used in hydrolysis — every polymer's digestion needs it.
  • Also produced in respiration, and split by photolysis in photosynthesis.
  • "Water is a metabolite" alone scores nothing — always name the reaction.

2 · Water as a solvent

  • Polarity lets water surround and separate charged/polar solutes.
  • Credited pairing: "so reactions can occur" OR "so substances can be transported" (blood, tissue fluid, xylem, phloem).

3 · High specific heat capacity

  • Lots of energy needed to break H-bonds before temperature rises → buffers changes in temperature (accept "resist").
  • Protects enzymes from denaturation, stable habitat/internal environment.
  • Never confuse with latent heat of vaporisation — different property, different qualifier ("high," not "large").

4 · Large latent heat of vaporisation

  • Lots of energy needed to evaporate water → cooling effect with little water lost.
  • Sweating, panting, transpiration.
  • Credited term is "vaporisation" — never "evaporation" in the property name.

5 · Cohesion & surface tension

  • H-bonds hold water molecules together → supports columns of water in xylem (accept transpiration stream / cohesion-tension theory) and produces surface tension.
  • Bare "cohesion" or bare "transpiration" alone ≠ the mark — state the consequence.
  • Bonus creditable extras: ice floats (insulates habitat below); water is transparent (light penetration for photosynthesis).

6 · Water potential & osmosis

  • Ψ, kPa. Pure water = 0 (maximum). Solute lowers it — always negative.
  • Osmosis: higher (less negative) → lower (more negative) Ψ, through a partially permeable membrane.
  • Never "water concentration" — always "water potential."

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Water as a metabolite

❓ Explain two ways in which water acts as a metabolite in cells.
Water is released as a product during condensation reactions, such as when monomers join to form polymers; water is used as a reactant during hydrolysis reactions, such as when polymers are digested back into their monomers.
❓ Name one process in photosynthesis and one process in respiration in which water is directly involved, and state its role in each.
In photosynthesis, water is split during photolysis, releasing protons, electrons and oxygen; in respiration, water is produced when oxygen accepts electrons and protons at the end of the electron transport chain.
❓ A student writes "water is a metabolite" as a complete answer for one mark. Explain why this would not score.
The statement names the property but gives no example of a reaction water is involved in; the credited answer must pair the property with a named reaction, such as condensation, hydrolysis, photolysis or respiration.

Water as a solvent

❓ Explain why water is described as an important solvent in cells.
Water is polar, so it can surround and separate charged and polar solute particles, keeping them dissolved; this allows metabolic reactions to occur in solution, and allows substances to be transported dissolved in fluids such as blood and tissue fluid.
❓ Name two body or plant fluids in which substances are transported dissolved in water, and one substance carried in each.
Blood plasma transports glucose and ions dissolved in water; xylem sap transports mineral ions such as nitrate dissolved in water.
❓ A question asks you to name three properties of water without explanation. State the correct wording for the solvent property.
Is a solvent (no explanation required for this version of the question — an unqualified "polar" would not be accepted as a substitute).

High specific heat capacity

❓ Explain why water has a high specific heat capacity, and why this matters to organisms living in it.
Much energy is needed to break the very large number of hydrogen bonds between water molecules before the temperature can rise; water therefore has a high (specific) heat capacity, so it buffers changes in temperature, providing a thermally stable habitat and protecting enzymes from denaturation.
❓ A student's answer states "water has a heat capacity." Explain why this would not score full marks, and correct it.
The qualifier "high" is missing, and no consequence is stated; the credited version is "water has a high (specific) heat capacity, so it buffers changes in temperature" — "resist" is also accepted for "buffers."
❓ Predict and explain the effect on a small rock pool's temperature stability if its water were replaced with a liquid of much lower specific heat capacity.
A liquid with a lower specific heat capacity would need less energy to change temperature, so the pool's temperature would fluctuate more rapidly and by a greater amount, making it a less thermally stable habitat and increasing the risk of enzyme denaturation in organisms living there.

Large latent heat of vaporisation

❓ Explain how water's latent heat of vaporisation makes sweating an effective cooling mechanism.
Water has a large latent heat of vaporisation, so much energy is needed to evaporate it; this energy is taken from the skin's surface as heat, so evaporating a relatively small volume of sweat removes a large quantity of heat, providing a cooling effect with little loss of water.
❓ A student writes "water has a large latent heat of evaporation." Identify and correct the error.
The correct term is "latent heat of vaporisation," not "latent heat of evaporation" — this is the most frequently penalised wording slip on this spec point.
❓ Explain why transpiration from a leaf surface has a cooling effect on the plant.
Water has a large latent heat of vaporisation, so evaporation of water from the leaf surface during transpiration removes heat energy from the leaf, cooling it, while only a small volume of water is lost in the process.

Cohesion & surface tension

❓ Explain how cohesion between water molecules allows water to be transported up a plant's xylem.
Hydrogen bonds hold water molecules to one another, giving strong cohesion; this allows a continuous, unbroken column of water to be pulled up the tube-like xylem vessels under tension without the column breaking — the transpiration stream, described by the cohesion-tension theory.
❓ Explain, in terms of cohesion, why a pond skater is able to stand on the surface of water without breaking through.
Cohesion between water molecules produces surface tension where water meets air, as hydrogen bonds pull surface molecules together; this creates a resistant surface strong enough to support the weight of a small organism such as a pond skater.
❓ A student answers "transpiration" when asked to explain why xylem can support a continuous water column. Explain why this alone would not score.
The bare word "transpiration" does not substitute for stating cohesion; the credited answer must name cohesion between water molecules (or describe the mechanism of the transpiration stream) as the reason the column is supported.
