Photosynthesis

How a chloroplast turns light energy into chemical energy — trapping light in the thylakoid membrane to build ATP and reduced NADP, then spending both in the stroma to fix carbon dioxide into sugar, plus the two required practicals that measure each half of the process in the lab.

The second most common loss: writing "glucose 3-phosphate" instead of glycerate 3-phosphate for GP. Section 1 · spec content point

🔆 The light-dependent reaction

The first half of photosynthesis happens entirely in the thylakoid membrane, and its whole job is to turn light energy into two portable products — ATP and reduced NADP — that the second half of the process completely depends on.

📖

What the specification says: "The light-dependent reaction in such detail as to show that: chlorophyll absorbs light, leading to photoionisation of chlorophyll; some of the energy from electrons released during photoionisation is conserved in the production of ATP and reduced NADP; the production of ATP involves electron transfer associated with the transfer of electrons down the electron transfer chain and passage of protons across chloroplast membranes and is catalysed by ATP synthase embedded in these membranes (chemiosmotic theory); photolysis of water produces protons, electrons and oxygen."

1
2
3

Picture a tiny solar power station with two collection points, called PSII and PSI. Sunlight hits the first collection point and knocks electrons loose — so hard that they fly off completely, leaving the collection point "short" of electrons. As those electrons zoom away down a wire (a chain of carrier proteins), they power a pump that pushes tiny charged particles (protons) through a wall into a small holding room. Those particles then rush back out through a tiny turbine, and the spinning turbine builds ATP — the cell's energy currency. Meanwhile, water is split apart nearby to replace the electrons that flew away, releasing oxygen as a leftover gas. A second collection point re-energises the electrons one more time, and they're used to build a second useful product, called reduced NADP.

  • Photoionisation = light knocking electrons out of chlorophyll completely
  • Electron transport chain = a line of carrier proteins the electrons hop along, step by step
  • Chemiosmosis = protons flowing through a turbine-shaped enzyme, building ATP
  • Photolysis = splitting water apart using light energy
  • Reduced NADP = a carrier molecule holding extra hydrogen/electrons, ready for the next stage

Light hits chlorophyll molecules at photosystem II (PSII) and excites electrons so strongly that they leave the chlorophyll altogether — this is photoionisation, and it leaves the chlorophyll positively charged. Those electrons travel along a chain of carrier proteins, the electron transport chain, releasing energy as they go. That energy actively pumps hydrogen ions (protons) from the stroma into the thylakoid lumen, building up a concentration gradient. The protons then flow back out through an enzyme called ATP synthase, and that flow drives the production of ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate — a process called chemiosmosis. At the same time, water molecules are split apart in a reaction called photolysis, which replaces the electrons chlorophyll lost and releases oxygen as a by-product. Light is absorbed again at a second site, photosystem I (PSI), re-exciting the electrons; these combine with protons and NADP⁺ to form reduced NADP. There's also a shorter backup route called cyclic photophosphorylation, which only makes ATP and doesn't involve NADP or photolysis at all.

The light-dependent reaction happens in the thylakoid membrane, because that's where chlorophyll and the photosystems are embedded — not in the stroma, which is where the second half of photosynthesis takes place instead.

  1. Photoionisation at PSII: chlorophyll absorbs light, exciting electrons so much that they are lost from the chlorophyll molecule altogether, leaving it positively charged.
  2. Electron transport chain (ETC): the lost electrons pass along a series of carrier proteins in the thylakoid membrane, releasing energy at each step.
  3. Proton pumping: that released energy actively pumps H⁺ across the thylakoid membrane, from the stroma into the thylakoid lumen, building a proton gradient.
  4. Chemiosmosis: protons diffuse back down that gradient, from the lumen into the stroma, through ATP synthase embedded in the membrane — this flow catalyses the synthesis of ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate.
  5. Photolysis at PSII: light splits water — 2H₂O → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ + O₂ — the electrons replace those lost from chlorophyll, the H⁺ adds to the lumen's proton gradient, and oxygen diffuses away as a by-product.
  6. Second light absorption at PSI: light re-excites electrons a second time; these combine with H⁺ and NADP⁺, catalysed by NADP reductase, to produce reduced NADP.
  7. Cyclic photophosphorylation: a genuinely separate short pathway sends electrons from PSI back into the ETC instead of on to NADP reductase — producing ATP only, with no reduced NADP and no photolysis involved at all.
Left to right, the main (non-cyclic) pathway: PSII → ETC → PSI → NADP reductase, producing ATP and reduced NADP. Photolysis happens only at PSII; NADP⁺ is reduced only at PSI — the two most commonly swapped facts on this diagram.
▶ 🚩 The detail that a "photoionisation" answer usually misses

A description that jumps straight to "photolysis" (splitting water) without first stating that chlorophyll itself absorbs light and loses electrons, becoming positively charged, is missing the actual credited idea — photolysis is a separate, later event that replaces those lost electrons, not the same thing as photoionisation.

▶ Precise phrasing worth reusing

Three phrases are worth learning close to word-for-word: "electrons are excited and lost from chlorophyll, so it becomes positively charged", "protons diffuse from the thylakoid lumen into the stroma through ATP synthase, synthesising ATP by chemiosmosis", and "light is absorbed again at PSI, and the re-excited electrons combine with protons and NADP⁺, forming reduced NADP". Naming the specific site (PSII or PSI) each time is worth more than a general description of "the photosystems".

▶ 🚩 Trap — NADP, never NAD

Writing "reduced NAD" or "NADH" anywhere in a photosynthesis answer is treated as a straightforward wrong-molecule error — that pair belongs to respiration (3.5.2). It is one of the most frequently repeated slips across this entire spec point, so it's worth deliberately double-checking every time you write the letters "NAD".

Section 2 · spec content point

🔗 Linking the two reactions

A short statement, but a frequently tested one — it's the "hinge" fact connecting everything in Section 1 to everything in Section 3, and it explains why the two reactions can never really be treated as independent of each other.

📖

What the specification says: "The light-independent reaction uses reduced NADP from the light-dependent reaction to form a simple sugar. The hydrolysis of ATP, also from the light-dependent reaction, provides the additional energy for this reaction."

1
2
3

The second half of photosynthesis can't do its job alone — it borrows two things made by the first half: a charged-up battery pack (ATP) and a delivery molecule carrying spare hydrogens (reduced NADP). The battery pack gets drained to release energy, and the delivery molecule drops off its hydrogens to help build sugar. If the deliveries stop, the second half stops too, almost straight away.

  • Hydrolysis = breaking a molecule apart using water, which releases energy
  • Simple sugar = the small sugar molecule this stage eventually builds

The light-independent reaction happens in the stroma and doesn't use light directly — but it completely depends on two products delivered fresh from the light-dependent reaction: ATP and reduced NADP. ATP is hydrolysed (split apart, releasing energy) to power the reaction; reduced NADP donates hydrogen and electrons, acting as the reducing agent that helps convert a 3-carbon acid into sugar. Without a constant supply of both, the light-independent reaction grinds to a halt within seconds, even if plenty of carbon dioxide is still available.

  • The Calvin cycle (light-independent reaction) runs in the stroma, using ATP and reduced NADP supplied continuously by the light-dependent reaction.
  • It does not use light directly, but it stalls almost immediately if the supply of light-dependent products stops — the two halves of photosynthesis are tightly coupled, not two processes running independently side by side.
  • Reduced NADP acts as the reducing agent: it donates hydrogen/electrons to convert glycerate 3-phosphate (GP) into triose phosphate (TP).
  • ATP is hydrolysed — releasing ADP and inorganic phosphate — to supply the additional energy this reduction step needs.
  • Once used, NADP⁺ and ADP+Pi are recycled straight back to the light-dependent reaction to be reused — so the exchange between the two reactions runs in both directions, not as a one-way handoff.
  • The "simple sugar" in the spec wording refers to the triose phosphate produced, and the hexose sugars ultimately built from it — not glucose specifically at the reduction step itself.
📌

Exam habit worth building: "the light-independent reaction doesn't need light" and "the light-independent reaction doesn't need the light-dependent reaction" are two very different claims — only the first one is true. Treat the second as a trap whenever it appears in a question or your own answer.

▶ 🚩 Trap — cut off light, and the Calvin cycle stops too

Because ATP and reduced NADP have no long-term storage in the chloroplast, removing light stops the light-independent reaction almost as quickly as it stops the light-dependent reaction itself — even though carbon dioxide may still be freely available. A strong answer states this dependency explicitly, rather than describing the two reactions as if they simply happen to occur one after the other.

Section 3 · spec content point

🔄 The light-independent reaction in detail — the Calvin cycle

Three steps, three named molecules, one enzyme — and the single most heavily tested reasoning skill on this whole spec point is predicting what happens to those three molecules when something runs out.

📖

What the specification says: "The light-independent reaction in such detail as to show that: carbon dioxide reacts with ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP) to form two molecules of glycerate 3-phosphate (GP). This reaction is catalysed by the enzyme rubisco; ATP and reduced NADP from the light-dependent reaction are used to reduce GP to triose phosphate; some of the triose phosphate is used to regenerate RuBP in the Calvin cycle; some of the triose phosphate is converted to useful organic substances."

1
2
3

Imagine a merry-go-round with three stops. At stop one, a CO₂ molecule from the air jumps on and joins a 5-part rider already on the ride, and together they split into two 3-part riders. At stop two, those two riders get topped up with energy and spare hydrogens, turning them into a slightly different 3-part rider. At stop three, most of those riders climb back onto the ride to rebuild the original 5-part rider — but a few hop off altogether, to go and help build sugar, fat or protein somewhere else in the plant.

  • RuBP = the 5-carbon starting rider
  • GP = the first 3-carbon rider, made in step one
  • TP (triose phosphate) = the 3-carbon rider made in step two
  • Rubisco = the enzyme that joins CO₂ onto RuBP

The Calvin cycle runs in three steps. Fixation: carbon dioxide combines with a 5-carbon molecule called RuBP, catalysed by the enzyme rubisco, producing two molecules of a 3-carbon compound called GP (glycerate 3-phosphate) — one CO₂ always makes two GP. Reduction: ATP and reduced NADP (both from the light-dependent reaction) convert GP into triose phosphate (TP), releasing ADP+Pi and NADP⁺ to be recycled. Regeneration and use: most of the TP is used, with more ATP, to regenerate RuBP so the cycle can keep running; a smaller portion of the TP leaves the cycle to build glucose and other organic molecules the plant needs, such as lipids and amino acids.

  1. Step 1 — fixation: carbon dioxide diffuses in from the air and combines with ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP, 5C), catalysed by the enzyme rubisco, to form two molecules of glycerate 3-phosphate (GP, 3C). The stoichiometry is fixed: one RuBP + one CO₂ → two GP, always doubling.
  2. Step 2 — reduction: ATP and reduced NADP (both supplied by the light-dependent reaction) reduce GP to triose phosphate (TP, 3C). ADP+Pi and NADP⁺ are released and recycled back to the light-dependent reaction.
  3. Step 3 — regeneration and use: most TP regenerates RuBP, using further ATP, so the cycle can continue; the remainder is converted to useful organic substances — glucose and other carbohydrates, lipids, and amino acids.

The numbers behind it: one full turn fixes one CO₂. Six turns (six CO₂ molecules) are needed to build one hexose sugar, because five out of every six TP molecules made must go back into regenerating RuBP — only one in six is spared to build the sugar.

One turn: 1 RuBP + 1 CO₂ → 2 GP → 2 TP. Most TP regenerates RuBP so the cycle continues; only a fraction is spared each turn to build organic substances — six turns are needed to yield one hexose sugar.
▶ 🚩 The reasoning trap — predicting the effect of removing light or CO₂

Remove light: less ATP and reduced NADP are produced, so GP accumulates (it can no longer be reduced), while triose phosphate and RuBP levels fall (TP is no longer being made, and existing TP keeps being used up to regenerate RuBP until it runs out).

Remove CO₂: fixation stops, so GP falls (no new GP is being made), while RuBP accumulates (it is still being regenerated from TP but is no longer being used up to fix carbon dioxide).

Trace the consequence mechanically through all three named molecules every time — one always rises and one always falls, as a genuine pair, never a vague "the rate decreases".

▶ Evaluating rubisco / rubisco-activase data

Where temperature-response data shows rubisco's own activity holding up at high temperature while the overall rate of CO₂ fixation falls, the credited explanation is that it is the activating enzyme (rubisco activase) — not rubisco itself — that becomes limiting at high temperature. The usual evaluation caveats also apply: data from only one species, enzymes tested in isolation rather than in an intact chloroplast, and no statistical test reported, so a firm causal conclusion can't yet be drawn from the data alone.

Section 4 · spec content point · required practical

🧪 Required practical 7 — chromatography of photosynthetic pigments

This practical is examined on precise technique detail, not general method knowledge — the named precautions below are worth more than a description of "separating pigments using chromatography".

📖

What the specification says: "Required practical 7: Use of chromatography to investigate the pigments isolated from leaves of different plants, eg, leaves from shade-tolerant and shade-intolerant plants or leaves of different colours."

1
2
3

Imagine dripping a splash of leaf juice onto the bottom of a strip of paper, then standing the paper in a shallow puddle of liquid, without the puddle touching the splash. As the liquid soaks up the paper, it carries the colours in the leaf juice with it — but some colours get carried further than others, so they spread out into separate bands. Comparing how far each colour travelled tells you which pigments were in the leaf.

A leaf extract is spotted onto a pencil line near the bottom of chromatography paper, which is then stood upright in a shallow layer of solvent (kept below the spotted line). As the solvent rises up the paper, it carries the different pigments with it — but because each pigment is a slightly different size and slightly differently soluble, they separate out into distinct spots at different heights. Comparing how far each pigment travelled, relative to how far the solvent travelled, gives a value called Rf that can be used to identify each pigment.

  • The origin line is drawn in pencil, not ink — ink itself contains pigments that would move up the paper and contaminate the chromatogram, and pencil keeps the origin's exact position fixed and visible.
  • The solvent level must stay below the origin line — if the solvent covers the spotted origin, the leaf pigments dissolve straight into the solvent instead of being carried up the paper.
  • The chromatogram is removed/stopped before the solvent front reaches the top of the paper, so the solvent front can still be marked and measured.
  • Only a named organic solvent is used (for example propanone, or an ether-based mixture) — water or any aqueous solution is rejected, because photosynthetic pigments are not water-soluble.
  • Method in outline: grind the leaf with a little solvent and sand; use a fine capillary tube to spot the concentrated extract onto the pencil origin repeatedly, letting each spot dry before adding the next to keep it small and concentrated; suspend the paper in the solvent inside a sealed container (to stop the solvent evaporating); mark the solvent front once the run is stopped, and let the paper dry.
📐

Calculating Rf: Rf = distance moved by the pigment ÷ distance moved by the solvent front, both measured from the origin — to the centre of the spot for the pigment. Rf has no units, and is always less than 1. Pigments are identified by comparing the calculated Rf against a reference table of known values, run under the same solvent conditions.

A leaf carries several pigments because different pigments absorb different wavelengths of light, so having a range of them lets the leaf capture a wider portion of the light spectrum. Shade-tolerant plants typically carry more, or different, accessory pigments than shade-intolerant plants, tuned to exploit the specific wavelengths that still penetrate a closed canopy — shade-intolerant plants are adapted instead to full, unfiltered sunlight.

▶ 🚩 Trap — a generic method answer scores poorly here

Simply describing "separate the pigments using chromatography" without the specific precautions above — why pencil, why the solvent level matters, why an organic solvent is required — reads as generic method knowledge rather than the named technique detail this practical is actually tested on.

Section 5 · spec content point · required practical

🔬 Required practical 8 — dehydrogenase activity in chloroplast extracts

This practical is a stand-in for the light-dependent reaction itself — DCPIP takes the place of NADP⁺ so that the rate of the light-dependent reaction can be watched happening, as a colour change, in real time.

📖

What the specification says: "Required practical 8: Investigation into the effect of a named factor on the rate of dehydrogenase activity in extracts of chloroplasts."

1
2
3

Imagine a blue dye acting as a stand-in "electron catcher" — if it catches electrons flying out of a working chloroplast, it changes from blue to colourless, like a mood ring flipping colour. The faster the blue fades, the faster the chloroplast's light-catching machinery must be working. Two extra "control" tubes make sure the colour change really is caused by working chloroplasts in the light, and nothing else.

DCPIP is a blue dye that acts as an artificial electron acceptor, standing in for NADP⁺. When chloroplasts are actively carrying out the light-dependent reaction, electrons reduce the DCPIP, turning it from blue to colourless — the faster this colour fades (measured with a colorimeter as falling absorbance), the faster the light-dependent reaction is running. Two control tubes are needed: one with DCPIP but no chloroplasts, kept in the light, to show light alone doesn't change the colour; and one with chloroplasts and DCPIP kept in the dark, to show chloroplasts are needed and nothing happens without light. The chloroplast suspension itself must be kept cold, isotonic and buffered so the chloroplasts stay intact and functional.

The DCPIP principle: DCPIP acts as an artificial electron acceptor in place of NADP⁺. It is reduced by electrons released during the light-dependent reaction, turning from blue to colourless; this is monitored using a colorimeter, where a falling absorbance reading indicates faster dehydrogenase activity — i.e. a faster light-dependent reaction.

Control tubeWhat it shows
DCPIP without chloroplasts, kept in the lightLight alone, with no chloroplasts present, does not change the colour of DCPIP
Chloroplasts with DCPIP, kept in the darkChloroplasts are required, and no colour change occurs without light

The suspension medium must be cold, isotonic and buffered:

  • Isotonic — so the medium's water potential matches that of the chloroplasts, meaning osmosis does not occur and the organelle does not burst, lyse, shrivel or shrink.
  • Cold — to slow enzyme and general metabolic activity, preventing damage to the chloroplasts during extraction.
  • Buffered — to keep pH constant, so enzymes involved are not denatured.
▶ 🚩 Trap — "the cell bursts" is the wrong wording here

A chloroplast is an organelle, not a cell — so cell-level language such as "the cell bursts" or describing it as "turgid" or "flaccid" is imprecise wording. The correct phrasing is that the organelle bursts, lyses, shrivels or shrinks, depending on the direction of net water movement.

Section 6 · exam skill

📊 Comparing the two reactions & what limits the rate

Two reference tools worth having ready: a side-by-side comparison of the two reactions, and the mechanistic link between each environmental factor and the specific stage of photosynthesis it actually restricts.

Quick reference: the two reactions, side by side

Light-dependent reactionLight-independent reaction (Calvin cycle)
SiteThylakoid membrane (grana)Stroma
Needs light directly?Yes — drives photoionisation at PSII and PSINo directly, but needs the light-dependent reaction's products continuously
InputsLight energy, water, ADP+Pi, NADP⁺CO₂, ATP, reduced NADP, RuBP (regenerated)
OutputsATP, reduced NADP, O₂ (by-product of photolysis)Triose phosphate → glucose & other organic substances; regenerated RuBP; ADP+Pi and NADP⁺ recycled
Key enzymeATP synthase (chemiosmosis)Rubisco (fixation)

The two reactions are genuinely linked, not independent: the light-independent reaction cannot proceed without a constant supply of ATP and reduced NADP, and the light-dependent reaction's ADP+Pi and NADP⁺ are only replenished if the light-independent reaction recycles them back. Cut off light, and the Calvin cycle stalls within seconds — even with plenty of CO₂ still available.

What limits the rate — name the stage, not just "less photosynthesis"

FactorThe mechanistic link
Light intensityLimits the light-dependent reaction — less photoionisation, so less ATP and reduced NADP are produced for the Calvin cycle.
CO₂ concentrationLimits the light-independent reaction — less CO₂ is available to combine with RuBP, so less GP is formed.
TemperatureAffects the enzymes of the light-independent reaction, especially rubisco — too low and the rate is slow; too high and the enzymes denature.
Water availabilityA shortage causes stomatal closure, cutting off the CO₂ supply and indirectly limiting the light-independent reaction.
While a curve is still rising, light intensity is the limiting factor; once it plateaus, a different factor has taken over — here, CO₂ concentration (the higher-CO₂ curve plateaus later, and at a higher rate).

Evaluating agricultural practices

Commercial glasshouse growers can control every limiting factor at once: artificial lighting extends the photoperiod and raises light intensity; paraffin heaters or direct CO₂ enrichment raise the carbon dioxide concentration (burning paraffin conveniently releases CO₂ and heat together); heating keeps temperature near the optimum for rubisco; irrigation prevents stomatal closure. The evaluation skill rewards reading where a yield curve plateaus from given data, then judging whether the extra yield gained justifies the extra cost of the intervention — a one-sided answer that only lists benefits, or only costs, is capped, since a genuine evaluation always weighs both sides.

Section 8 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

Photosynthesis content works well as a supporting paragraph inside a longer essay-style answer on more than one theme — the trick is naming the specific enzyme or molecule involved, then closing with a sentence that ties the mechanism back to whatever the title is actually about.

▶ Example paragraph — "the importance of photosynthesis to living organisms"

In the light-dependent reaction, photoionisation of chlorophyll releases electrons that move down the electron transfer chain, pumping protons across the thylakoid membrane; their movement back through ATP synthase generates ATP by chemiosmosis — the same underlying principle used in oxidative phosphorylation during respiration. ATP and reduced NADP are then used in the light-independent reaction to reduce GP to triose phosphate, the starting point for the organic molecules that ultimately build the biomass of almost every food chain.

Essay technique: whenever a paragraph reaches for photosynthesis, name the specific enzyme (rubisco, ATP synthase) or specific molecule (RuBP, GP, reduced NADP) rather than simply saying "photosynthesis happens" — named, specific biochemistry used to support the title's argument is what separates a strong essay paragraph from a vague one.

Real synoptic links worth having ready

  • Chlorophyll and NADP as nitrogen-containing molecules (3.1.6 ATP / 3.1.8 nucleic acids).
  • CO₂ diffusing from the mesophyll air spaces into chloroplasts, so photosynthesis is ultimately limited by diffusion (3.3.2 gas exchange).
  • Rubisco's active site being complementary to both CO₂ and RuBP (3.1.4.2 enzyme specificity).
  • Rising temperature raising the photosynthetic rate towards rubisco's optimum until another factor becomes limiting (3.5.3 energy and ecosystems).
  • Chloroplast ultrastructure and cell fractionation, reused directly from first-year cell biology (3.2 cells).
  • The same chemiosmotic mechanism reappearing at the inner mitochondrial membrane during respiration, alongside the frequently-confused Calvin/Krebs cycle pairing (3.5.2 respiration).

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · The light-dependent reaction

  • Photoionisation at PSII: chlorophyll absorbs light, electrons excited and lost, chlorophyll becomes positively charged.
  • Electrons pass down the ETC, pumping H⁺ into the thylakoid lumen — H⁺ diffuses back through ATP synthase, making ATP (chemiosmosis).
  • Photolysis at PSII only: 2H₂O → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ + O₂ — electrons replace those lost, O₂ is a by-product.
  • Light absorbed again at PSI only, re-excited electrons + H⁺ + NADP⁺ → reduced NADP (via NADP reductase).
  • Cyclic photophosphorylation is separate: PSI → ETC → PSI, ATP only, no reduced NADP, no photolysis.

2 · Linking the two reactions

  • The Calvin cycle uses reduced NADP as the reducing agent to help form a simple sugar.
  • ATP is hydrolysed to provide the extra energy the reduction step needs.
  • Both come from the light-dependent reaction — cut off light, and the cycle stalls within seconds, even with CO₂ still available.

3 · The Calvin cycle in detail

  • Fixation: RuBP (5C) + CO₂ → 2 GP (3C), catalysed by rubisco — always double the GP.
  • Reduction: ATP + reduced NADP reduce 2 GP → 2 TP (3C).
  • Regeneration & use: most TP regenerates RuBP; some TP leaves to build glucose and other organic substances.
  • Remove light → GP rises, TP & RuBP fall. Remove CO₂ → GP falls, RuBP rises. Always trace all three molecules.

4 · RP7 — pigment chromatography

  • Pencil origin (ink contains pigments); solvent kept below the origin; stop before solvent reaches the top.
  • Only a named organic solvent — water is rejected, pigments aren't water-soluble.
  • Rf = distance moved by pigment ÷ distance moved by solvent front, both from the origin. No units, always <1.

5 · RP8 — dehydrogenase activity

  • DCPIP replaces NADP⁺; reduced by electrons from the light-dependent reaction, blue → colourless; falling absorbance = faster activity.
  • Two controls: DCPIP + no chloroplasts + light (shows light alone doesn't change colour); chloroplasts + DCPIP + dark (shows chloroplasts are needed).
  • Medium must be cold, isotonic (no osmotic lysis) and buffered (pH constant).

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

The light-dependent reaction

❓ Explain what is meant by photoionisation of chlorophyll.
Light is absorbed by chlorophyll, exciting electrons; the electrons are lost from the chlorophyll molecule, which becomes positively charged.
❓ Describe how a proton gradient is built up and then used to synthesise ATP during the light-dependent reaction.
Electrons released by photoionisation pass along the electron transfer chain, losing energy at each carrier; this energy actively pumps protons from the stroma into the thylakoid lumen. Protons then diffuse back down their concentration gradient, from the lumen into the stroma, through ATP synthase embedded in the thylakoid membrane; this flow catalyses the production of ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate — chemiosmosis.
❓ State the products of photolysis, and explain what happens to each one.
Photolysis of water produces protons, electrons and oxygen (2H₂O → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ + O₂). The electrons replace those lost from chlorophyll at PSII; the protons add to the proton gradient in the thylakoid lumen; oxygen is released as a by-product and diffuses away.

Linking the two reactions

❓ Explain why the light-independent reaction stops almost immediately if a plant is placed in darkness, even though carbon dioxide is still available.
The light-independent reaction depends on a continuous supply of ATP and reduced NADP from the light-dependent reaction; in darkness, photoionisation stops, so ATP and reduced NADP are no longer produced, and GP can no longer be reduced to triose phosphate.
❓ State the roles of ATP and reduced NADP in the light-independent reaction.
ATP is hydrolysed to provide the additional energy needed to reduce GP to triose phosphate; reduced NADP acts as the reducing agent, donating hydrogen/electrons in that same reduction step.

The Calvin cycle in detail

❓ Describe the light-independent reaction, from carbon dioxide entering the cycle to the production of a useful organic substance.
Carbon dioxide combines with ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP), catalysed by the enzyme rubisco, producing two molecules of glycerate 3-phosphate (GP). GP is reduced to triose phosphate (TP), using reduced NADP and energy released by the hydrolysis of ATP. Most TP is used to regenerate RuBP, allowing the cycle to continue; some TP is converted to glucose or another named organic substance, such as a lipid or amino acid.
❓ Predict and explain the effect on the concentration of GP, TP and RuBP if the carbon dioxide supply to a leaf is suddenly removed.
Fixation stops, so GP concentration falls, as no new GP is being formed from RuBP and carbon dioxide. RuBP concentration rises, because it continues to be regenerated from triose phosphate but is no longer being used up in the fixation step.
❓ Data show that rubisco's own catalytic activity is maintained at high temperature, but the overall rate of CO₂ fixation still falls. Suggest an explanation, and evaluate the data.
This suggests it is the activating enzyme, rubisco activase, rather than rubisco itself, that is limited at high temperature. The data should be treated cautiously: it may come from only one species, the enzymes may have been tested in isolation rather than within an intact chloroplast, and no statistical test may have been applied to confirm the difference is significant.

RP7 — pigment chromatography

❓ Explain why the origin line in a chromatography investigation should be drawn in pencil rather than ink.
Ink itself contains pigments, which would move up the paper during the run and contaminate the chromatogram; pencil marks do not move, so the origin's position stays fixed and visible throughout.
❓ A student uses water as the solvent for a chromatography investigation of leaf pigments and obtains no separation. Explain why.
Photosynthetic pigments are not soluble in water, so they cannot dissolve into the solvent or be carried up the paper; a named organic solvent must be used instead.
❓ A pigment travels 6.2 cm from the origin, while the solvent front travels 9.8 cm. Calculate the Rf value and explain what it is used for.
Rf = 6.2 ÷ 9.8 = 0.63 (no units). The calculated Rf is compared against a reference table of known Rf values obtained under the same solvent conditions, to identify which pigment is present.

RP8 — dehydrogenase activity

❓ Explain the principle behind using DCPIP to investigate dehydrogenase activity in chloroplast extracts.
DCPIP acts as an artificial electron acceptor in place of NADP⁺. It is reduced by electrons released during the light-dependent reaction, changing colour from blue to colourless; a colorimeter is used to measure the falling absorbance over time, with a faster fall indicating greater dehydrogenase activity.
❓ Explain why two separate control tubes are needed in this investigation.
One tube contains DCPIP without chloroplasts, kept in the light, to show that light alone does not change the colour of DCPIP. The other contains chloroplasts with DCPIP, kept in the dark, to show that chloroplasts are required, and that no colour change occurs in the absence of light.
❓ Explain why the chloroplast suspension medium must have the same water potential as the chloroplasts themselves.
If the water potential is the same, no net osmosis occurs between the chloroplasts and the surrounding medium, so the organelle does not burst, lyse, shrivel or shrink during the investigation.
