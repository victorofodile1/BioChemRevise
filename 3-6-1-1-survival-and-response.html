Survival and Response

Why organisms have to respond to their environment to survive — how plants bend towards light and away from gravity using unequal auxin, how the simplest animals of all keep themselves in a favourable place using taxis and kinesis, and how a three-neurone reflex arc turns a stimulus into a fast, protective response.

⚠️

The single habit that protects the most marks on this spec point: IAA is never credited just for being somewhere. Saying it "accumulates" or is "present" on one side of a shoot or root scores nothing on its own — you must say it moves / diffuses there, and that the movement is what causes more cell elongation (never "cell division") on that side. Build the habit of writing the full chain — produced, then moves, then elongation, then bends — every single time.

Section 1 · spec content point

🧭 Why organisms respond to their environment

Everything else in this spec point — tropisms, taxes, kineses, reflexes — is the same underlying idea dressed up in different bodies: something changes, it gets detected, something decides what to do about it, and something acts. Get this vocabulary chain exact before anything else.

📖

What the specification says: "Organisms increase their chance of survival by responding to changes in their environment."

1
2
3

Think of a house with a smoke alarm system. Smoke in the air is the change that needs noticing — that's the stimulus. The little sensor in the alarm that actually notices the smoke is the receptor — and it only reacts to smoke, not to noise or light. The control box that decides "sound the siren now" is the coordinator. And the siren that actually makes the noise is the effector. Living things use exactly this same four-step chain to survive — noticing danger or opportunity, and then actually doing something about it.

  • Stimulus = the change that needs noticing (like the smoke)
  • Receptor = the part that notices it (like the smoke sensor)
  • Coordinator = the part that decides what to do (like the control box)
  • Effector = the part that actually does it (like the siren)

Every response, in any organism, follows the same chain: a stimulus (a change inside or outside the organism) is picked up by a receptor, which only reacts to one particular type of stimulus. A coordinator — the brain or nervous system generally, or just a single relay neurone in a simple reflex — works out an appropriate response. An effector, either a muscle or a gland, then actually carries that response out. Responding matters because it lets an organism move towards something useful or away from something harmful, which makes it more likely to survive and pass its genes on. Exam answers always want the specific benefit named, not just "it helps it survive" — for example, moving towards food, light, or a suitable temperature, or moving away from a predator or from drying out.

StimulusA change in the internal or external environment.Detected by a receptor.

CoordinatorFormulates a suitable response to a stimulus.The CNS/brain in general — or, in a simple reflex, just the relay neurone.

Stating survival value precisely

A question asking for the survival value of a response in an unfamiliar organism is never answered with a generic "it helps it survive" — that scores nothing. The credited answer always names the concrete benefit:

  • Moving towards food, light (for photosynthesis), or a suitable temperature — the organism reaches a resource it needs.
  • Moving away from predators, desiccation, or damaging light — the organism avoids harm.
  • A soil organism or seed dispersing away from the parent plant — it leaves the soil or reaches the surface, disperses to found new colonies, and avoids competing with the parent for resources.
▶ 🚩 Trap — the generic "it helps it survive" answer

A bare statement that a response "helps the organism survive" is not credited on its own, however true it is. The mark is for the specific, named advantage — what exactly the organism is moving towards or away from, and why that particular thing matters to it.

Section 2 · spec content point

🌿 Growth factors and directional stimuli in plants

Plants can't run away or turn to face the light, so they solve the same "detect and respond" problem using growth itself as the response. This section sets up the vocabulary that Sections 3 and 4 then apply in full detail.

📖

What the specification says: "In flowering plants, specific growth factors move from growing regions to other tissues, where they regulate growth in response to directional stimuli."

1
2
3

Imagine a head office (the growing tip of a shoot or root) that sends out messenger runners carrying instructions. The runners travel to different departments (other parts of the plant) and tell them how fast to grow — and which way to send those runners depends on which direction a signal, like sunlight or gravity, is coming from. That's really all a plant "response" is: growing faster on one side than the other.

  • Messenger runner = the growth factor / hormone (IAA, also called auxin)
  • Head office = the growing region, e.g. a shoot or root tip
  • Direction the signal is coming from = the directional stimulus (light or gravity)

A directional growth response of a plant to a directional stimulus is called a tropism. It's caused by a plant hormone — a "growth factor" — called indoleacetic acid (IAA), also known as auxin, which is made in actively growing regions such as the tips of shoots and roots. IAA then moves out into other tissues nearby, where it changes how much those cells grow, depending on which direction a stimulus like light or gravity is coming from. The two named tropisms on this spec point are phototropism (a growth response to light) and gravitropism (a growth response to gravity) — Sections 3 and 4 work through each in full.

"Specific growth factors" here refers to plant hormones — most importantly indoleacetic acid (IAA), an auxin (exam answers can use "auxin" and "IAA" interchangeably). IAA is produced in growing regions — actively dividing, meristematic tissue such as the tip of a shoot or the tip of a root — and then moves out into other tissues nearby, where it regulates the rate of cell elongation. Crucially, the amount and direction of that movement depends on a directional stimulus: light coming predominantly from one side (unilateral light) or the pull of gravity.

A tropism is defined as a directional growth response of a plant to a directional stimulus, caused by the unequal distribution of IAA across the responding tissue. This spec point names two: phototropism (light as the directional stimulus) and gravitropism (gravity as the directional stimulus) — both explained by exactly the same underlying idea, that IAA is redistributed unequally and that unequal distribution changes the rate of elongation unequally on each side.

TermMeaning here
Growth factorA plant hormone that regulates growth — on this spec point, indoleacetic acid (IAA / auxin).
Growing regionWhere IAA is produced — the tip of a shoot or root.
Directional stimulusA stimulus that comes from one particular direction — unilateral light, or gravity.
TropismA directional growth response of a plant to a directional stimulus, caused by unequal IAA distribution.
📌

Hold onto one idea from this section for the whole of the next two: it is never simply how much IAA is present overall that matters — light and gravity don't change how much IAA a plant makes. What changes is where that IAA ends up, and that unequal distribution is what unequal growth, and therefore bending, comes from.

Section 3 · spec content point

🔆 Phototropism — the auxin causal chain

This is the single most heavily tested causal chain on the whole spec point, and the exam almost never just wants the outcome — it wants every link in the chain, in order, with the movement step stated explicitly.

📖

What the specification says: "The effect of different concentrations of indoleacetic acid (IAA) on cell elongation in the roots and shoots of flowering plants as an explanation of gravitropism and phototropism in flowering plants." (This spec content point is dense enough to need two parts — phototropism here, gravitropism and its opposite-effect twist in Section 4.)

1
2
3

Have you ever noticed a houseplant on a windowsill leaning towards the window? Here's why. The tip of the shoot makes a "grow faster" chemical. When light shines mostly from one side, that chemical rushes over to the shady side of the stem — like everyone in a crowd shuffling away from a bright spotlight. Wherever there's more of the chemical, the cells there stretch out longer. So the shady side ends up longer than the sunny side, and a longer side on one edge makes the whole stem curve — towards the light.

  • The "grow faster" chemical = IAA (auxin)
  • Stretching out longer = cell elongation (not the cells multiplying — the existing cells just get longer)
  • Leaning towards the window = positive phototropism

IAA is made in the tip of a shoot. When light hits the shoot unevenly — more from one side than the other — IAA moves across to the shaded side. It's this movement that examiners specifically check for; simply saying IAA "builds up" there isn't enough. Once there's a higher concentration of IAA on the shaded side, the cells there elongate more than the cells on the lit side. Because one side is now longer than the other, the shoot bends — and because the elongation happened on the shaded side, the shoot ends up curving towards the light. This is useful because it lets the plant capture more light for photosynthesis.

  1. IAA is produced in the tip of the shoot.
  2. Under unilateral (one-sided) light, IAA moves / diffuses to the shaded side of the shoot — this is the step examiners are specifically checking has been stated, not just that IAA ends up there.
  3. The higher IAA concentration on the shaded side causes more cell elongation there than on the lit side — never write "cell division" for this mechanism.
  4. Because the shaded side elongates more than the lit side, the shoot bends towards the light — positive phototropism, which increases the light available for photosynthesis.
IAA is made in the tip and moves to the shaded side. The higher concentration there drives more cell elongation, so the shaded side grows longer and the shoot curves towards the light.
▶ 🚩 The costliest omission on this spec point

"IAA accumulates on the shaded side" scores nothing by itself — presence alone is never credited. You must state that IAA moves or diffuses to the shaded side. The other frequent slip is naming "cell division" as the mechanism instead of cell elongation — the cells already present get longer, they don't multiply.

Investigating IAA — the coleoptile / agar-block practical

This isn't a numbered required practical, but it's fully examinable practical-design content: you can be asked to design or evaluate an investigation into how IAA affects shoot growth.

  • Variables to control: the size and age of the shoots/tips used, the size and type of the agar block, how long the tip or block is left in place, temperature, and repeating with several shoots to calculate a reliable mean.
  • The tip is cut off (decapitated) because it's the source of IAA — a plain agar block containing a measured amount of IAA is then placed off-centre on the cut stump, giving a controlled, unequal dose that mimics natural unequal distribution. A decapitated shoot with no block added shows no further bending, confirming the tip really is the source.
  • Seedlings are kept in the dark throughout so that light itself doesn't become a second, uncontrolled directional stimulus — gravity (or the agar block) needs to be the only variable causing any bending.
  • A lid is placed over the dish holding the IAA solution or agar to stop evaporation from concentrating the solution and changing its water potential — not to keep out microbes, which is the answer most students give and which isn't credited.
▶ Straight from examiner feedback on this practical

The most common wrong answer for the Petri-dish-lid question is "to prevent contamination" — the credited reason is always evaporation changing the concentration/water potential of the IAA solution. Many answers also correctly identify that IAA stimulates growth but lose the mark for not specifying the mechanism as cell elongation.

Section 4 · spec content point

⬇️ Gravitropism — the opposite-effect twist

Same hormone, same "produced → moves → elongation" chain — but roots respond to it in the opposite way from shoots. This single twist is one of the highest-frequency mark losses on this spec point, because students over-generalise the shoot rule onto every organ.

📖

What the specification says: "The effect of different concentrations of indoleacetic acid (IAA) on cell elongation in the roots and shoots of flowering plants as an explanation of gravitropism and phototropism in flowering plants." (Same specification content point as Section 3 — this half covers gravitropism and the shoot/root contrast.)

1
2
3

Roots have a strange trick up their sleeve. In a shoot, sending extra "grow faster" chemical to one side makes that side stretch out more, like before. But roots are far more sensitive to the exact same chemical — so instead of growing more, that side actually grows less. Gravity pulls the chemical to the underside of a root; because the underside then grows less than the topside, the root bends — downwards, into the soil, where it can reach water.

Under gravity, IAA moves to the lower side of a root, in exactly the same way it moves to the shaded side of a shoot. But here's the twist: the very same concentration of IAA that speeds up cell elongation in a shoot actually slows it down in a root, because root cells are more sensitive to IAA. So the lower side of the root elongates less than the upper side, and the root bends downwards — towards gravity. In a shoot under gravity, the same "IAA to the lower side" movement happens, but because shoot cells respond the normal way, the lower side elongates more and the shoot bends upwards, away from gravity.

  1. Cells in the root tip detect the direction of gravity.
  2. IAA is produced in the tip and moves to the lower side of the root — the same movement step as in phototropism, just triggered by gravity instead of light.
  3. In roots specifically, a high concentration of IAA inhibits cell elongation, because root cells are more sensitive to IAA than shoot cells are.
  4. The lower side of the root therefore elongates less than the upper side, so the root bends downwards — positive gravitropism — which lets the root reach water and mineral ions and anchor the plant.
Organ & stimulusWhere IAA ends upEffect on elongation thereNet response
Shoot — phototropismShaded side (unilateral light)Promotes elongationBends towards the light (positive phototropism)
Shoot — gravitropismLower side (gravity)Promotes elongationBends upwards, away from gravity (negative gravitropism)
Root — gravitropismLower side (gravity)Inhibits elongationBends downwards, towards gravity (positive gravitropism)
⚠️

The trap examiners set again and again: students who can fluently describe the shoot mechanism often simply forget that the same concentration has the opposite effect in a root — they over-generalise "more IAA always means more elongation" onto every organ. Learn the root twist as its own stand-alone fact, not as an afterthought to the shoot rule.

▶ 🚩 Trap — treating IAA's effect as the same everywhere

Forgetting that the same IAA concentration promotes elongation in a shoot but inhibits it in a root is one of the most consistently penalised mistakes on this whole spec point. Whenever you write about IAA and elongation, name the organ first — the direction of the effect depends entirely on it.

Section 5 · spec content point

🐛 Taxes and kineses — simple animal responses

The simplest possible animal responses — no brain, no learning, just movement that keeps the organism somewhere favourable. The exam marks taxis and kinesis as two separate, linked ideas, never as one blended definition.

📖

What the specification says: "Taxes and kineses as simple responses that can maintain a mobile organism in a favourable environment."

1
2
3

Picture a woodlouse under a bright lamp. It doesn't think, plan, or remember a map — it just does one of two simple things. Either it heads in a specific direction, straight away from the light, like walking away from a person shining a torch at you — that's a taxis. Or, it doesn't pick a direction at all — it just scurries around faster and turns more often while it's in the bright, dry spot, and only slows down and turns less once it wanders into a dark, damp spot — so it ends up "trapped" there by accident, not on purpose. That's a kinesis.

A taxis is a directional response — the organism moves in a specific direction that is set by the direction the stimulus is coming from, for example a woodlouse moving directly away from a light source (negative phototaxis). A kinesis is a non-directional response — only the rate of movement or turning changes as the stimulus gets stronger or weaker; the organism doesn't aim anywhere in particular, but moves faster and turns more in unfavourable conditions and slows down in favourable ones, so it statistically ends up spending more time somewhere good. Both responses achieve the same goal — keeping a simple mobile organism in a favourable environment — without needing anything like a brain to plan a route.

The survival value of both is the same: they keep a simple mobile organism in a favourable environment — for a woodlouse, a damp, dark place that reduces water loss through its exoskeleton and offers protection from predators.

▶ Precise phrasing for a "distinguish" question

"Distinguish taxis from kinesis" is marked as two separate, linked statements, not one blended sentence. Write: "Taxis is a directional response, where the direction of movement is set by the stimulus." Then, separately: "Kinesis is a non-directional response, where only the rate of movement or turning changes with stimulus intensity." Blending these into a single comparative sentence risks losing a mark for each half not being stated clearly enough on its own.

Section 6 · spec content point

⚡ The simple reflex — three neurones, fast and protective

A reflex is the fastest, simplest possible version of the stimulus-to-response chain from Section 1 — and its speed is exactly what makes it protective. This section also covers why a measured "reaction time" is always longer than nerve conduction alone would predict.

📖

What the specification says: "The protective effect of a simple reflex, exemplified by a three-neurone simple reflex. Details of spinal cord and dorsal and ventral roots are not required."

1
2
3

Touch a hot pan by accident and your hand yanks back before you've even "decided" to move it — that's a reflex. It works like a very short relay race with only three runners: one runner carries the message from your skin (the sensory neurone), hands the baton to a middle runner inside your spinal cord (the relay neurone), who hands it straight to a third runner (the motor neurone) that reaches your arm muscle and makes it pull away. Because there are only two hand-offs in the whole race, it's over almost instantly — much faster than waiting for your brain to notice, think about it, and decide what to do.

A reflex follows the chain from Section 1 in its simplest possible form: stimulus → receptor → sensory neurone → relay neurone (inside the central nervous system) → motor neurone → effector → response. It's fast and protective for two reasons: the pathway is short, with very few synapses to cross — and crossing a synapse is the slowest step in any nerve pathway, so fewer synapses means less delay. It's also involuntary — it doesn't wait for a conscious decision from the brain, so it happens automatically and always produces the same reliable response. The brain does eventually find out what happened, but only afterwards, through a separate, slower pathway running alongside the reflex.

The credited order for describing the arc is exact: stimulus → receptor → sensory neurone → relay neurone (in the central nervous system) → motor neurone → effector → response. A generator potential is established at the receptor, and if it reaches threshold, an action potential is passed along the sensory neurone (the detailed ion mechanism is developed in 3.6.1.2). The specification is explicit that spinal cord and dorsal/ventral root detail isn't required — don't spend time on it.

Drawn as a schematic pathway, not an anatomical cross-section — spinal cord/root detail isn't required. The relay neurone's two synapses are the only synapses in the whole arc, which is why it's so fast. The brain only finds out afterwards, via a separate, slower pathway.

Why the reflex is protective

  • The pathway is short, with very few synapses — crossing a synapse is the slowest step in any neural pathway, so fewer synapses means a much faster overall response and less time for damage to occur.
  • It is involuntary — it doesn't require a conscious decision by the brain, so it doesn't need to be learned, works from birth, and happens even if the person is distracted or not paying attention.
  • Because it doesn't involve the brain directly, it also leaves the brain free to carry on with other, higher-level tasks.
  • It is always the same, reliable response to the same stimulus — the brain does eventually become aware of what happened, but only afterwards, through a separate, slower pathway running in parallel.
▶ 🚩 Trap — the neurone sequence, vague or out of order

Naming the three reflex-arc neurones in the wrong order, or just vaguely ("nerves carry the message"), instead of the exact sequence sensory → relay → motor is one of the highest-frequency mark losses on this spec point. "Relay" and "intermediate" neurone are both accepted names for the middle one.

Reaction time — the synoptic chain

A measured reaction time is always longer than the time nerve conduction along an axon alone would predict, because conduction along the axon is comparatively fast — it isn't the main source of delay. The full delay is really a chain of several separate steps, each pulling in ideas from elsewhere in this topic:

  1. Time for stretch-mediated sodium channels at the receptor to open and a generator potential to build up (developed in 3.6.1.2).
  2. Time for that potential to reach threshold and trigger an action potential in the sensory neurone.
  3. Synaptic delay at each synapse the impulse crosses (developed in 3.6.2.2) — this is usually the slowest single step.
  4. Time for transmission at the neuromuscular junction and for the muscle itself to contract (3.6.3).
🔎

The classic ruler-drop reaction-time test is not a true reflex — catching the ruler involves a conscious decision to close the hand, so it measures a voluntary reaction time, not reflex time. Common sources of error in that practical: the subject closing their hand pre-emptively before the ruler is released, the ruler not being held or dropped perfectly vertically, and the ruler sticking to the fingers rather than falling freely.

Section 7 · spec content point

🧪 Required practical 10 — the choice-chamber / maze investigation

This is the practical-skills partner to Section 5: an investigation into how an environmental variable affects the movement of an animal, using a choice chamber or a maze. Every design choice needs its specific, named justification — "to control variables" alone scores nothing.

📖

What the specification says: "Required practical 10: investigation into the effect of an environmental variable on the movement of an animal using either a choice chamber or a maze."

1
2
3

Imagine a plastic dish split into two halves — one damp and dark, one dry and bright — with a mesh floor so a woodlouse can freely wander between them. You put some woodlice in the middle, wait, then count how many ended up on each side. If far more end up on the damp, dark side, that tells you woodlice prefer it. You'd repeat this a few times with fresh woodlice to make sure it wasn't a fluke, and you'd always put the woodlice back where you found them afterwards, unharmed.

A choice chamber is set up with two contrasting conditions — for example light versus dark, or damp versus dry — with a mesh floor so the animals can move freely between the two sides. A known number of animals is placed in the centre, given time to settle, and then counted in each half at set time intervals. Every other variable is kept the same between the two sides (temperature, species, size and age of the animals, time in the chamber), the investigation is repeated with fresh animals to calculate a mean, and a chi-squared test is used to check whether the distribution is significantly different from an even 50:50 split. Every design choice needs a specific reason, not just "to control variables" — for example, dim light is used instead of bright light because bright light could dry out or even kill the animals, and because it could act as an unwanted second directional stimulus.

  1. Set up a choice chamber with two contrasting conditions (e.g. light/dark, or damp/dry) and a mesh floor, so the organisms are free to move between the two sides.
  2. Place a known number of organisms in the centre of the chamber, allow time for them to settle and respond, then count how many are in each half at set time intervals.
  3. Control every other variable between the two sides — the same temperature, the same light intensity (other than the variable being tested), the same species, size and age of organism, and the same total time in the chamber.
  4. Repeat with fresh organisms and calculate a mean; use a chi-squared test to determine whether the distribution differs significantly from an even split.
📌

Justify every design choice with the specific reason, never a generic one:

  • Use dim rather than bright light — bright light risks creating a second, uncontrolled light gradient that could trigger an unwanted phototaxis response, and heat from a bright lamp could dry out or even kill the organisms.
  • Use a mesh floor — so the organisms can move freely and be counted without escaping.
  • Starve the organisms beforehand (where relevant) — so they are motivated to respond to the stimulus actually being tested, rather than to hunger.
🧬

Ethical requirement, worth its own mark: live organisms must be returned to their original habitat unharmed once the investigation is complete, and must never be exposed to lethal or otherwise harmful extremes of the variable under test.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "to control variables" with nothing else added

Giving a bare "to control variables" for any individual design choice, without the specific survival-value or methodological reason attached, scores nothing — this is one of the most common traps on this whole spec point. Always finish the sentence: control it because of the specific confound it would otherwise introduce.

Section 8 · exam technique

🎯 Common mistakes & precise wording

The content on this spec point is genuinely short — a handful of definitions, two causal chains, one arc sequence — but almost every mark rides on getting a precise causal sequence exactly right, not just knowing a fact in isolation. These are the slips that come up again and again.

Vague / incorrect versionWhat to write instead
"IAA accumulates on the shaded/lower side"IAA moves / diffuses to that side — presence alone is never enough
"IAA causes cell division"IAA causes cell elongation — existing cells get longer, they don't multiply
"More IAA always means more growth"True in shoots, but the same concentration inhibits elongation in roots
"The lid stops contamination" (IAA/agar practical)The lid stops evaporation changing the concentration / water potential of the solution
Taxis and kinesis blended into one sentenceTwo separate statements: directional response vs. non-directional, rate-only response
Reflex-arc neurones named vaguely or out of orderThe exact sequence: sensory → relay → motor
"To control variables" as a complete justification (RP10)Name the specific confound each control choice actually prevents
▶ Why this spec point is harder than its length suggests

The memorisation load here is genuinely low — there just isn't that much content. What makes it hard is that the exam angle is almost never "state the fact"; it's "write the exact causal chain." Dropping the movement step in a tropism answer, or reversing the shoot/root effect, or naming the reflex neurones out of order, are all sequencing errors rather than knowledge gaps — which means the fix is drilling the chains to word-perfect, in-order recall, not re-reading the facts again.

▶ Command words used on this spec point

Explain is the dominant command word here — "explain phototropism/gravitropism" wants the full causal chain (produced → moves → elongation → bends), not just the final outcome. Describe ("describe the reflex arc") wants the named sequence of structures in order, without necessarily justifying why each step happens. Distinguish ("distinguish taxis from kinesis") is marked as two separate, linked statements, not one blended definition. State wants short, precise recall — a named survival advantage, or a bare definition. Design / describe how you would investigate (used for RP10 and the IAA/coleoptile practical) wants controlled variables plus a specific justification for each one, never a generic method.

Section 9 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

This spec point comes up as a supporting paragraph inside longer essay-style questions more often than it comes up alone. Two essay titles are built directly on it — the trick each time is closing the paragraph with a line that ties the mechanism back to the essay's actual theme.

▶ Example paragraph — "the importance of responses to environmental change"

Plants respond to directional stimuli through tropisms: IAA is produced at a growing tip and moves to one side of the shoot or root, causing differential cell elongation there. In a shoot under unilateral light, IAA moving to the shaded side promotes more elongation there, bending the shoot towards the light and increasing the light available for photosynthesis. Animals show a faster, more immediate version of the same principle: a three-neurone reflex arc — receptor, sensory neurone, relay neurone, motor neurone, effector — produces a rapid, protective response because the pathway is short, crosses few synapses, and does not require a conscious decision by the brain. Both examples show the same underlying value of a fast, appropriate response: it increases an organism's chance of both survival and reproduction.

▶ Example paragraph — "the importance of adaptations to the environment"

Taxis and kinesis are simple behavioural adaptations that keep a mobile organism in a favourable environment without requiring any learning. A woodlouse's negative phototaxis moves it directly away from light, while its faster, more erratic movement in dry air — a kinesis — statistically increases the time it spends in damp, favourable conditions. Both reduce water loss and predation risk, giving the organism a better chance of surviving long enough to reproduce, which is exactly the same underlying benefit that a rapid, protective reflex or a light-seeking tropism provides in other organisms.

Treat this content as the "mechanism" paragraph in a longer answer: state the causal chain precisely — the movement step in a tropism, or the neurone sequence in a reflex — then add one sentence explicitly linking it back to whatever the essay title is actually about.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Why organisms respond

  • Stimulus (change in/out) → receptor (detects, specific to one stimulus) → coordinator (formulates response) → effector (muscle contracts / gland secretes) → response.
  • Survival value must always be the specific named benefit — never "it helps it survive" alone.

2 · Growth factors & tropisms

  • Growth factor = IAA (auxin), made in growing regions (shoot/root tips), moves to other tissues to regulate elongation.
  • A tropism = directional growth response to a directional stimulus, caused by unequal IAA distribution — never a change in total amount.

3 · Phototropism

  • IAA produced in tip → moves to shaded side → more cell elongation there → shoot bends towards light (positive phototropism).
  • Never "accumulates" alone; never "cell division" — it's cell elongation.
  • Coleoptile practical: tip removed = IAA source; kept dark = light not a 2nd variable; lid = stop evaporation/water-potential change, not contamination.

4 · Gravitropism

  • IAA moves to lower side of root under gravity — same movement, opposite effect: inhibits elongation in roots (roots more sensitive).
  • Lower root side elongates less → bends down (positive gravitropism). Lower shoot side elongates more → bends up (negative gravitropism).

5 · Taxes and kineses

  • Taxis = directional response, direction set by stimulus (e.g. negative phototaxis).
  • Kinesis = non-directional, only rate of movement/turning changes with stimulus intensity.
  • Both keep a mobile organism in a favourable environment — state as two separate points, not blended.

6 · The simple reflex

  • Exact order: stimulus → receptor → sensory neurone → relay neurone (CNS) → motor neurone → effector → response.
  • Protective because: short pathway, few synapses (slowest step) → fast; involuntary → no conscious decision needed, reliable.
  • Reaction time > nerve conduction alone because of generator-potential build-up, synaptic delay, and neuromuscular-junction/muscle-contraction time. Ruler-drop test ≠ true reflex (conscious catch).

7 · RP10 choice chamber

  • Contrasting conditions + mesh floor → count organisms per half at set intervals → control other variables → repeat, mean, chi-squared test.
  • Dim light (not bright) = avoids 2nd light gradient + prevents heat harming organisms, not just "control variables."
  • Ethical rule: return organisms unharmed, never expose to lethal extremes.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Why organisms respond

❓ A marine snail moves away from a rock pool that is warming rapidly in the sun. Suggest the survival value of this response.
Moving away from the warming rock pool means the snail avoids a temperature that could damage its cells or exceed its thermal tolerance, increasing its chance of survival and therefore its chance of reproducing.
❓ Define the terms receptor and coordinator, and state how they are linked in a response pathway.
A receptor detects a stimulus and is specific to one particular type of stimulus. A coordinator formulates a suitable response to the stimulus that has been detected. The receptor passes information about the stimulus on so that the coordinator can determine the appropriate response.
❓ "This organism responded to the change, so it will survive." Explain why this statement alone would not gain credit, and rewrite it so that it would.
The original statement gives no specific benefit, so it cannot be credited. A creditable version names the concrete advantage, e.g. "the organism moved towards a source of food, increasing its chance of survival and of reproducing."

Growth factors & tropisms

❓ Explain what is meant by the term "tropism," using the correct name for the growth factor involved.
A tropism is a directional growth response of a plant to a directional stimulus, such as light or gravity. It is caused by the unequal distribution of the growth factor indoleacetic acid (IAA), also called auxin, across the responding tissue.
❓ State where IAA is produced in a flowering plant, and explain why this location makes sense for its role.
IAA is produced in growing regions, such as the tip of a shoot or root. This makes sense because these actively growing tissues are where directional growth responses need to be regulated, and IAA can then move outward from there to the tissues where growth actually needs to change.

Phototropism

❓ A shoot is lit from one side only. Explain, in order, how this results in the shoot bending towards the light.
IAA is produced in the tip of the shoot; under unilateral light, IAA moves to the shaded side of the shoot; the higher concentration of IAA on the shaded side causes more cell elongation there than on the lit side; because the shaded side elongates more, the shoot bends towards the light — positive phototropism.
❓ A student writes: "IAA builds up on the dark side, so the plant grows towards the light." Identify the missing idea and correct the sentence.
The sentence is missing the idea of movement — presence or "build up" alone is not enough. Corrected: IAA moves/diffuses to the shaded side, and it is this higher concentration that causes more cell elongation there, bending the shoot towards the light.
❓ In the coleoptile agar-block investigation, explain why the shoot tip is removed and why the dish is covered with a lid.
The tip is removed because it is the source of IAA, so a decapitated shoot with a measured agar block placed off-centre on the cut stump gives a controlled, unequal dose of IAA. The dish is covered with a lid to prevent evaporation from changing the concentration and water potential of the IAA solution — not to prevent microbial contamination.

Gravitropism

❓ A root is placed horizontally. Explain, in order, why it bends to grow downwards.
Cells in the root tip detect the direction of gravity; IAA is produced in the tip and moves to the lower side of the root; in roots, a high concentration of IAA inhibits cell elongation; the lower side therefore elongates less than the upper side, so the root bends downwards — positive gravitropism.
❓ A student assumes that a horizontal shoot and a horizontal root will bend in the same direction, because IAA moves to the lower side in both. Explain why this assumption is wrong.
Although IAA does move to the lower side in both organs, the same concentration has opposite effects: in a shoot it promotes elongation, so the lower side grows more and the shoot bends upwards (negative gravitropism); in a root it inhibits elongation, so the lower side grows less and the root bends downwards (positive gravitropism).
❓ Suggest the survival value of positive gravitropism in a root.
Growing downwards means the root is more likely to reach water and mineral ions in the soil, and it also helps anchor the plant firmly in place.

Taxes and kineses

❓ Distinguish between a taxis and a kinesis, and give a named example of each in a woodlouse.
A taxis is a directional response, where the direction of movement is set by the direction of the stimulus, e.g. negative phototaxis — a woodlouse moving directly away from light. A kinesis is a non-directional response, where only the rate of movement or turning changes with the intensity of the stimulus, e.g. a woodlouse moving faster and turning more often in dry air, which results in it spending more time in damp areas.
❓ Explain how a kinesis can keep an organism in a favourable environment without the organism moving directly towards it.
In unfavourable conditions the organism moves faster and turns more often, so it spends less time there; in favourable conditions it slows down and turns less, so it spends more time there — the organism ends up concentrated in the favourable area purely as a statistical outcome of changing its rate of movement, not by heading towards it directly.

The simple reflex

❓ Describe the pathway of a three-neurone reflex arc, from stimulus to response.
Stimulus → receptor → sensory neurone → relay neurone (in the central nervous system) → motor neurone → effector → response. A generator potential is established at the receptor, and if threshold is reached, an action potential passes along the sensory neurone.
❓ Explain why a reflex response is both fast and protective.
The pathway is short and crosses very few synapses, and synaptic transmission is the slowest step in any neural pathway, so fewer synapses means a much faster response. It is also involuntary, so it does not require a conscious decision by the brain, meaning it happens automatically and reliably even if the person is distracted, limiting the time available for harm to occur.
❓ A student's measured reaction time in a ruler-drop test is much longer than the time predicted for nerve conduction alone. Suggest three reasons why, and explain why this test is not a true reflex.
Time is needed for stretch-mediated sodium channels at the receptor to open and a generator potential to build up, for synaptic transmission at each synapse crossed, and for the neuromuscular junction and muscle contraction itself — conduction along the axon is comparatively fast and not the main source of delay. The test is not a true reflex because catching the ruler involves a conscious decision, so it measures voluntary reaction time rather than reflex time.

RP10 choice chamber

❓ Describe how you would use a choice chamber to investigate the effect of humidity on the movement of woodlice.
Set up a choice chamber with one damp and one dry side and a mesh floor; place a known, equal number of woodlice in the centre, allow time to settle, then count the number on each side at set time intervals; control temperature, light intensity, species, size/age of woodlice and total time in the chamber; repeat with fresh woodlice, calculate a mean, and use a chi-squared test to see whether the distribution differs significantly from an even split.
❓ A student uses a bright lamp to create the "dry" side of a choice chamber. Explain why this is a poor design choice, beyond simply "it isn't controlled."
A bright lamp could act as an unwanted second directional stimulus, triggering a phototaxis response instead of, or as well as, the response to humidity being tested; the heat produced could also dry out or even harm the woodlice, which is both a confound and an ethical issue.
❓ State one ethical requirement that applies to RP10, and explain why it matters.
The organisms used must be returned to their original habitat unharmed once the investigation is finished, and must never be exposed to conditions extreme enough to kill or seriously harm them — this is a requirement for the ethical treatment of living organisms used in an investigation.
