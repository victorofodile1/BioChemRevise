Regulation of Transcription and Translation

A-level-only content on three separate ways a eukaryotic cell controls which of its genes actually get expressed: transcription factors — with oestrogen as the worked steroid-hormone example — epigenetic control by DNA methylation and histone acetylation, and control after transcription has already happened, at the translation stage, by RNA interference.

⚠️

The two rules that decide almost every mark on this spec point. First: a transcription factor binds to DNA/the promoter and stimulates or inhibits RNA polymerase — it is not an enzyme, so "active site" and any enzyme–substrate/induced-fit language is always wrong here. Second, and the single most exam-critical pairing in the whole topic: methylation always acts on DNA, acetylation always acts on histones — swap that pairing and the mark is gone no matter how good the rest of the answer is. Get the direction right too: increased DNA methylation decreases transcription; increased histone acetylation increases it. This entire spec point is widely reported as one of the hardest-to-score topics on the whole course, mostly because of precision slips like these two.

Section 1 · spec content point

🔀 Transcription factors — the general mechanism

Before oestrogen, drugs, or any named example, this is the underlying machinery every later section in this spec point builds on: what a transcription factor actually is, and the one habit — never calling it an enzyme — that protects the most marks.

📖

What the specification says: "In eukaryotes, transcription of target genes can be stimulated or inhibited when specific transcriptional factors move from the cytoplasm into the nucleus. The role of the steroid hormone, oestrogen, in initiating transcription."

1
2
3

Think of a gene as a light switch, and a transcription factor as a finger that presses it. But this particular finger only fits one specific switch, because the finger and the switch are shaped to match each other exactly, like a key in a lock. Press that switch "up," and copying of that gene speeds up; some fingers press switches "down" instead, and slow copying down. Either way, this finger isn't a tool that cuts or builds anything — that's what an enzyme does — it just touches the switch and changes how much the copying machine gets used.

  • The gene's "switch" = the promoter region, the bit of DNA just before the gene
  • The copying machine = RNA polymerase, the enzyme that actually builds the RNA copy
  • Copying a gene = transcription
  • The finger = a transcription factor — a protein, not an enzyme

A transcription factor is a protein whose job is to control whether a gene gets transcribed. It attaches to DNA close to the start of a gene, at a region called the promoter, and this changes how easily RNA polymerase — the enzyme that actually carries out transcription — can bind and start working there. Depending on which transcription factor it is, this can make transcription happen more (it stimulates it) or less (it inhibits it) — it is never a one-way "on switch" only. Each transcription factor recognises just one gene, or a small set of genes, because its 3D shape only fits that gene's particular promoter sequence — exactly the same "shape fits shape" idea used for enzymes and antibodies elsewhere in the course, just applied to a DNA-binding protein rather than a catalyst.

ConceptDetail
What a transcription factor isGenerally a protein that binds to DNA and controls the rate of transcription of one or more specific genes.
Where it bindsAt or near the promoter region of the target gene — never described vaguely as "binding to bases" or "binding to the gene."
Effect on transcriptionBinding stimulates or inhibits RNA polymerase binding/initiation, so it increases or decreases the rate of transcription of that gene — it genuinely works both ways, not just as an "on switch."
Its specificityComes from a tertiary structure/binding site complementary to that one gene's promoter sequence.
📌

The habit worth building now: whenever a question asks for the general mechanism of a transcription factor, give all three links in one go — it binds DNA at/near the promoter; this stimulates or inhibits RNA polymerase; so the rate of transcription increases or decreases. A bare "it switches the gene on" loses most of the marks even when the general idea is correct.

▶ 🚩 Trap — a transcription factor is not an enzyme

Even though the "one shape fits one target" logic is genuinely similar to how enzymes recognise a substrate, a transcription factor is not an enzyme and DNA is not "its substrate." Words like active site, substrate, and induced fit are all specifically wrong here — this precision point comes up in almost every question on this topic, so it is worth training yourself to reach for "binding site" and "complementary shape" instead, every single time.

▶ Why this matters for the rest of the spec point

In eukaryotic cells specifically, many transcription factors sit inactive in the cytoplasm until something activates them — a hormone binding, for instance — and then move into the nucleus, where the DNA actually is, before they can do their job. That movement from cytoplasm to nucleus is itself part of the mechanism the specification names explicitly, and Section 2 turns it into a full worked example using the steroid hormone oestrogen.

Section 2 · spec content point

🧪 Oestrogen — a steroid hormone that switches on transcription directly

Oestrogen is the specification's single named worked example of a transcription factor in action — a six-link chain from a hormone diffusing into a cell to a specific gene being transcribed faster, with no membrane relay step needed anywhere along the way.

📖

What the specification says: "In eukaryotes, transcription of target genes can be stimulated or inhibited when specific transcriptional factors move from the cytoplasm into the nucleus. The role of the steroid hormone, oestrogen, in initiating transcription." (Same content point as Section 1 — this half covers oestrogen as the named worked example.)

1
2
3

Oestrogen is small and can just walk straight through the cell's outer wall — no need to knock or ring a doorbell first. Once inside, it finds a waiting helper (a receptor sitting on a sleepy, switched-off "switch-presser") and links up with it, which wakes the helper up. The now-awake helper walks into the control room (the nucleus) and presses the right switch (the gene's promoter), turning up copying of that gene. Compare that to a hormone like glucagon, which is too big and the wrong kind of molecule to walk through the wall — it has to knock on the door from outside (a surface receptor) and pass a message inward instead of ever going in itself.

  • Steroid hormone = a hormone small enough to slip straight through the cell's outer wall
  • Receptor = a protein shaped to link up with only one particular molecule
  • The nucleus = the cell's control room, where the DNA lives

Oestrogen is a small, lipid-soluble steroid hormone, so unlike most hormones it can diffuse straight through the phospholipid bilayer of a target cell — it doesn't need a surface receptor at all. Once inside, it binds to a receptor sitting on an inactive transcription factor in the cytoplasm, and this binding changes the shape of the transcription factor's DNA-binding site, switching it on. The now-active transcription factor moves from the cytoplasm into the nucleus, binds to the promoter region of its target gene, and stimulates RNA polymerase, so that gene is transcribed faster. This is completely different from a protein hormone like glucagon, which can't cross the membrane and instead has to bind a surface receptor and trigger a second-messenger cascade inside the cell.

▶ 🚩 Trap — never give oestrogen a surface receptor

Mixing up the two mechanisms — giving oestrogen a surface receptor and second-messenger cascade, or having a protein hormone like glucagon diffuse straight into the cell — is a specifically documented, heavily tested error. Oestrogen needs none of the surface-receptor machinery precisely because it is lipid-soluble; glucagon needs all of it precisely because it is not.

Contrast — a protein hormone (e.g. glucagon, 3.6.4.2)

Glucagon is a protein/protein-derived hormone, not lipid-soluble, so it cannot cross the phospholipid bilayer directly. It must bind a receptor on the outside of the target cell's membrane, then relay the signal inward via a second-messenger (cAMP) cascade. Oestrogen needs none of this — being lipid-soluble lets it act directly on gene expression, with no relay step at all.

Explaining how a drug could block this pathway

A drug with a shape complementary to the oestrogen receptor can bind to that receptor instead of oestrogen, preventing oestrogen from binding and so preventing activation of the transcription factor — transcription of the target gene is not stimulated. This is the mechanism behind some anti-oestrogen breast-cancer drugs, and links forward directly to how abnormal oestrogen concentration is treated as a cancer risk factor later in this topic.

🔎

Why only some cells respond: only cells that possess the specific oestrogen receptor can respond to it at all — receptor specificity determines which cells a hormone can act on, exactly as with any other receptor system in the specification.

Section 3 · spec content point

🏷️ What epigenetics actually is

Before the exam-heavy methylation/acetylation direction table in Section 4, it's worth being able to define epigenetics precisely — because the single most missed mark on this whole spec point is a phrase, not a fact.

📖

What the specification says: "Epigenetic control of gene expression in eukaryotes."

1
2
3

Imagine your DNA is a giant cookbook, and every cell in your body has an identical copy of the whole book. Epigenetics is like sticky notes stuck onto certain recipes (genes), saying "don't cook this one today" or "cook this one extra." The recipe itself — the actual words in the book — never changes, and you can always peel a sticky note back off later. That's completely different from a mutation, which is like someone scribbling out and permanently changing a word in the recipe itself — that can't just be peeled off.

  • Epigenetics = sticky notes on genes, changing how much they're used, without touching the words underneath
  • Mutation = an actual permanent change to the words themselves (contrast, not part of epigenetics)
  • Heritable = passed on to new cells when a cell divides

Epigenetics is about genes being switched further on or further off in ways that don't involve changing the DNA code itself. Instead, chemical tags get added to the DNA, or to the histone proteins DNA is wrapped around, and these tags control how accessible a gene is to being copied. Because the underlying DNA sequence is never touched, these changes are, in principle, reversible — completely unlike a mutation, which permanently alters the code and can't simply be undone. These tags can also be copied and passed on to daughter cells when a cell divides, which is what makes the change "heritable."

Epigenetics involves heritable changes in gene function, without any change to the base sequence of DNA. These changes are caused by changes in the environment, and they work by altering how physically accessible a gene is to the transcription machinery — not by altering what the gene's code actually says.

ConceptDetail
EpigenomeThe full set of chemical tags on the DNA and histones of a cell that, between them, determine which of that cell's genes are actually expressed.
ReversibilityBecause the base sequence itself is untouched, an epigenetic change can, in principle, be reversed — a mutation cannot.
HeritabilityEpigenetic tags are copied at mitosis, so they can be inherited by daughter cells — and in some documented cases, across generations of an organism.
📌

The phrase worth memorising word for word: "without a change to the base sequence of DNA." This exact idea is what separates epigenetics from a mutation, and it is the single most frequently missed mark whenever a question asks for a definition of epigenetics.

▶ Why this matters before Section 4's mechanism detail

Section 4 gives the two specific named mechanisms — methylation of DNA and acetylation of histones — and the direction each one pushes transcription. Both of them are simply specific examples of this general idea: a chemical tag changes chromatin structure, which changes how accessible a gene is, which changes how much it's transcribed, all without altering a single base in the DNA sequence.

Section 4 · spec content point

🧲 The methylation/acetylation direction rule

This is the single most exam-critical pairing on the whole spec point: which molecule is modified, and which direction the effect on transcription runs. Two facts have to both be correct — get either one wrong and the mark is lost.

📖

What the specification says: "Epigenetics involves heritable changes in gene function, without changes to the base sequence of DNA. These changes are caused by changes in the environment that inhibit transcription by: increased methylation of the DNA or decreased acetylation of associated histones."

1
2
3

Picture DNA wound tightly around a set of spools (histone proteins), like thread wound round bobbins. Methylation is like gluing little tags directly onto the thread — it makes the whole bundle wind up tighter and harder to unravel, so that gene is hard to reach and gets copied less. Acetylation is different — it's like putting little tags onto the spools themselves, which makes the spools let go of the thread a bit, so it unwinds and becomes easier to reach, and that gene gets copied more. Remember: methylation tags go on the thread (DNA); acetylation tags go on the spools (histones) — mixing those up is the single most common mistake on this whole topic.

  • Methylation = tags added to DNA → winds tighter → gene copied less
  • Acetylation = tags added to histones (spools) → unwinds → gene copied more
  • Chromatin = the whole DNA-plus-spools bundle

DNA methylation means a methyl group is added directly to the DNA itself, usually at a cytosine base in or near a gene's promoter — this makes the DNA condense more tightly around its histone proteins, physically blocking transcription factors and RNA polymerase from reaching it, so increased methylation decreases transcription. Histone acetylation is the opposite kind of tag in every sense: an acetyl group is added to the histone proteins DNA is wrapped around, not to the DNA itself, and this loosens rather than tightens the DNA-histone grip, making the gene more accessible — so increased acetylation increases transcription. The pairing is fixed: methylation only ever happens to DNA, acetylation only ever happens to histones, and examiners specifically reject the two being swapped.

The mechanism behind each

Methylation: a methyl group is added to cytosine bases of the DNA itself, typically in or near the gene's promoter region. This physically prevents transcription factors from binding to the promoter, so RNA polymerase cannot bind either, and it also condenses the surrounding chromatin — both effects block transcription of that gene.

Acetylation: histone proteins are positively charged and DNA is negatively charged, so under normal conditions they attract each other strongly, keeping chromatin tightly condensed. Adding acetyl groups to the histones (increased acetylation) neutralises some of that positive charge, so the histones bind DNA less tightly — chromatin decondenses, and the DNA becomes more accessible to transcription factors and RNA polymerase. Removing acetyl groups (decreased acetylation) does the reverse: histones become more positively charged, grip DNA more tightly, and the DNA becomes less accessible.

The four-combination direction table

ChangeActs onEffect on transcription

Methylation adds methyl groups (Me) directly to DNA and condenses chromatin around the histones; acetylation adds acetyl groups (Ac) to the histone proteins themselves and loosens the grip on DNA. Both work by changing how physically accessible the gene is — never merge the two mechanisms.
▶ 🚩 Trap — the pairing is fixed, and marked strictly

Methylation is only ever credited on DNA; acetylation is only ever credited on histones. Swapping the pairing — "methylation of histones" or "acetylation of DNA" — scores nothing, no matter how correct the stated direction is. All four combinations in the table above have been examined as a table-completion task requiring both the correct molecule and the correct up/down direction in the same answer.

🧭

How to tackle a table-completion question on this: before writing anything, silently sort the given change into one of the four rows of the table above — decide which molecule, then which direction — and only then write the effect. Never answer "increases/decreases transcription" without first re-stating which molecule and which direction produced it.

Section 5 · spec content point

🎗️ Epigenetics in disease — cancer, environment & data skills

The same two mechanisms from Section 4, applied to a named context: how abnormal methylation can cause cancer, why epigenetic drugs are a genuinely active treatment route, and how to reason through genetic-versus-environmental data questions properly.

📖

What the specification says: "The relevance of epigenetics on the development and treatment of disease, especially cancer."

1
2
3

Imagine a tumour suppressor gene as a brake pedal for cell division. If a sticky note (methylation) gets stuck over the brake pedal so it can't be pressed, the cell just keeps dividing without stopping — that's how methylation can help cause cancer. Some cancer drugs work by peeling that sticky note back off, letting the brake work again. The opposite problem happens if a sticky note peels off an accelerator pedal (an oncogene) that's supposed to stay mostly covered — then the accelerator gets stuck down instead.

  • Tumour suppressor gene = a "brake pedal" gene that normally slows cell division
  • Oncogene = an "accelerator pedal" gene that, if stuck on, drives cancer

If a tumour suppressor gene — a gene that normally puts the brakes on cell division — gets abnormally heavily methylated, it can be silenced almost as completely as if it had been mutated, and the cell loses that brake. If a proto-oncogene instead loses methylation it should normally have, it can become permanently overactive, turning it into a cancer-driving oncogene. Both routes lead to the same outcome: uncontrolled, rapid cell division. Because epigenetic changes are reversible, drugs that reduce methylation of a silenced tumour suppressor gene, or increase methylation of an overactive oncogene, are a real and active cancer treatment strategy — not just a mutation, which can't be undone this way.

Applying methylation and acetylation to cancer

  • Abnormal increased methylation of a tumour suppressor gene can silence it, just as a mutation could — removing its normal brake on the cell cycle.
  • Abnormal decreased methylation of a proto-oncogene can permanently over-activate it, turning it into an oncogene.
  • Both routes converge on the same outcome: uncontrolled, rapid cell division.

Because epigenetic changes, unlike mutations, are reversible, drugs that reduce methylation of a tumour suppressor gene (re-enabling its expression) or increase methylation of an oncogene (silencing it) are an active, examined treatment mechanism — the direct route by which this spec point connects to cancer treatment more broadly.

Explaining age-of-onset or environmental variation properly

Always finish the full chain, not just half of it: an environmental factor (diet, smoking, stress, toxins, alcohol, exercise) → changes DNA methylation or histone acetylation → changes gene expression → phenotype (for example, age of onset of a disease) varies — all without the DNA sequence itself ever changing. Naming the environmental factor alone, without naming the epigenetic mechanism it acts through, only earns partial credit at best.

▶ 🚩 Trap — naming the factor without naming the mechanism

A recurring weak-answer pattern names an environmental factor correctly (say, smoking, or stress) but never states the actual mechanism — describing vague "levels of a substance" instead of specifically naming epigenetics, methylation, or acetylation. Precision here is worth more than breadth: one fully explained mechanism scores better than several factors listed with no mechanism at all.

Practical-skills link — no wet-lab practical, a data-handling skill

No required practical is tied to this spec point directly — the named skills are data-handling ones: interpreting data provided from investigations into gene expression, and evaluating appropriate data for the relative influences of genetic and environmental factors on phenotype.

In practice, this means being given mRNA, protein, or fluorescence-level data and using it to work out which level of control is acting. More protein made with unchanged mRNA points to control acting after transcription, not at it — the key clue for spotting Section 6's RNA-interference mechanism in data, rather than a transcriptional mechanism from this section.

The genetic-versus-environmental evaluation task is structured like other evaluation essays in this course: weigh evidence for a genetic contribution (twin/family studies, an identified allele) against evidence for an environmental one (diet, stress, smoking, toxins, exercise), then explicitly link the environmental evidence to an epigenetic mechanism — naming diet or smoking alone, without naming methylation or acetylation as the route, drops the final mark.

Section 6 · spec content point

✂️ RNA interference — control after transcription, at translation

Transcription factors and epigenetics both control how much mRNA gets made in the first place. RNA interference is a genuinely different kind of control — it acts on mRNA that has already been made, silencing a gene at the translation stage instead.

📖

What the specification says: "In eukaryotes and some prokaryotes, translation of the mRNA produced from target genes can be inhibited by RNA interference (RNAi)."

1
2
3

A gene is like a recipe in a cookbook, and mRNA is a photocopy of that recipe sent to the kitchen to be cooked from. RNA interference is like someone shredding that photocopy before the kitchen can ever use it — the original recipe book (the gene, the DNA) is completely untouched, it's just that this particular copy never survives long enough to be cooked from. Because the copy is destroyed, no dish (protein) ever gets made from it, even though the recipe itself was copied out perfectly correctly in the first place.

  • mRNA = the "photocopy" of a gene, sent off to be used
  • Translation = "cooking" — building a protein from that copy
  • siRNA = the short RNA "shredder" that destroys one specific copy

A double-stranded RNA molecule is cut by an enzyme into short fragments called siRNA, roughly 20–25 nucleotides long. One strand of an siRNA fragment binds, by complementary base pairing, to a specific mRNA molecule whose sequence matches it. This marks the mRNA for destruction — it's cut up before it can ever reach a ribosome. Because the mRNA never survives to be translated, the protein that gene codes for is never made, even though the gene itself was transcribed completely normally and the DNA is structurally undamaged. This is a translational control mechanism, not a transcriptional one — it never changes how much mRNA gets made, only whether the mRNA that already exists gets used.

A double-stranded RNA molecule is cut by an enzyme into short fragments called short interfering RNA (siRNA), roughly 20–25 nucleotides long. One strand of an siRNA fragment binds, by complementary base pairing, to a specific target mRNA molecule whose sequence matches it. This marks the mRNA for destruction: it is cut/degraded into fragments before it can reach a ribosome. Because the mRNA is destroyed, it cannot be translated into a specific sequence of amino acids — the protein that gene codes for is never made, even though the gene itself was transcribed normally and is structurally undamaged.

A double-stranded RNA molecule is cut into short siRNA fragments. One strand binds a complementary target mRNA by base pairing, marking it for cutting/degradation — the mRNA is destroyed before a ribosome can translate it, so no protein is made even though the gene itself was transcribed correctly.
▶ 🚩 Trap — RNAi acts after transcription, never on it

An answer that says siRNA "prevents transcription" does not score — RNAi acts entirely after transcription has already happened, on the mRNA that transcription already produced. Keep the vocabulary strict: RNAi is a translational control mechanism, not a transcriptional one.

Transcriptional vs translational control — the distinction tested with data

Transcription factors and epigenetics change how much mRNA a gene produces in the first place. RNA interference leaves mRNA production completely untouched but destroys the mRNA before it can be used — so in gene-expression data, unchanged mRNA level alongside reduced or absent protein level is the signature of translational (siRNA-type) control, not transcriptional control.

Section 8 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

This spec point works well as the "molecular endpoint" paragraph in a longer essay about hormones, receptors, or differences between cells — the trick is closing each paragraph with a sentence that ties the mechanism back to the actual essay title, not a generic summary.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around hormones and receptors

Oestrogen shows the most direct possible hormonal action on a cell: because it is a small, lipid-soluble steroid hormone, it diffuses straight through the phospholipid bilayer and binds a receptor on an inactive transcription factor already inside the cytoplasm, activating it. The activated transcription factor moves into the nucleus and binds the promoter of its target gene, stimulating RNA polymerase and increasing transcription directly. This is a clear demonstration that a hormone does not always need a membrane-surface receptor and a relay cascade to have an effect — it can act on gene expression itself, with the receptor sitting inside the very cell it changes.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around differences between cells

Every cell in a multicellular organism carries an essentially identical copy of the genome, so differences between cell types must come from differences in which genes are actually expressed, not from differences in the DNA itself. This spec point supplies three distinct mechanisms behind that: transcription factors that switch specific genes up or down, epigenetic tags — DNA methylation and histone acetylation — that change how physically accessible a gene is without ever altering its sequence, and RNA interference, which can silence a gene's product even after that gene has been transcribed completely normally. Together, these three mechanisms are the finest level of detail behind why two genetically identical cells can look and behave completely differently.

Whichever essay theme this content supports, treat it as the mechanism paragraph: state the process precisely — naming the transcription factor, the specific epigenetic tag, or RNAi, depending on what the title is actually asking about — then add one closing sentence that links it explicitly back to that theme, rather than simply restating the mechanism a second time.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Transcription factors

  • Binds DNA at/near the promoter; stimulates or inhibits RNA polymerase — increases or decreases transcription.
  • Specific to one gene because of a tertiary structure/binding site complementary to that gene's promoter.
  • NOT an enzyme — never "active site," "substrate," or "induced fit."

2 · Oestrogen & transcription

  • Lipid-soluble steroid hormone → diffuses straight through the membrane, no surface receptor.
  • Binds receptor on inactive TF in cytoplasm → shape changes → activates → moves to nucleus → binds promoter → stimulates RNA polymerase.
  • Contrast: glucagon (protein hormone) needs a surface receptor + second messenger (cAMP) — never let oestrogen use this route.
  • Drug blocking: binds the oestrogen receptor instead, so oestrogen can't bind and the TF isn't activated.

3 · What epigenetics is

  • Heritable changes in gene function, without changes to the base sequence of DNA — the phrase most often missed.
  • Caused by changes in the environment; reversible (unlike mutation); heritable at cell division.

4 · Methylation vs acetylation

  • Methylation acts on DNA; acetylation acts on histones — this pairing is fixed and strictly marked.
  • Increased methylation → transcription down. Increased acetylation → transcription up.
  • Both change how physically accessible the gene is to transcription factors and RNA polymerase.

5 · Epigenetics & disease

  • Increased methylation of a tumour suppressor gene silences it; decreased methylation of a proto-oncogene over-activates it — both → uncontrolled division.
  • Epigenetic drugs work because the change is reversible, unlike a mutation.
  • Environmental factor → changes methylation/acetylation → changes gene expression → phenotype varies. Always name the mechanism, not just the factor.

6 · RNA interference

  • dsRNA cut into siRNA (~20–25 nt) → binds complementary mRNA by base pairing → mRNA cut/degraded before translation.
  • Acts AFTER transcription, on translation — never say it stops transcription.
  • Data signature: unchanged mRNA + reduced/absent protein = translational (RNAi-type) control.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Transcription factors

❓ Describe the general mechanism by which a transcription factor controls the expression of a gene.
The transcription factor binds to DNA at or near the promoter region of a specific gene; this stimulates, or inhibits, the binding/initiation of RNA polymerase; so the rate of transcription of that gene increases, or decreases.
❓ Explain why a particular transcription factor only affects one specific gene.
The transcription factor has a specific tertiary structure, giving it a binding site that is complementary in shape to the promoter sequence of that one gene; no other gene's promoter fits this binding site.
❓ A student describes a transcription factor as having "an active site that binds to its substrate, the gene." Identify what is wrong with this description.
A transcription factor is not an enzyme, so it does not have an active site and DNA is not its substrate; the correct terms are a binding site, complementary in shape to the promoter region of the gene.

Oestrogen & transcription

❓ Describe the role of oestrogen in initiating transcription of a target gene.
Oestrogen is lipid-soluble, so it diffuses directly through the phospholipid bilayer into the cell; it binds to a receptor on an inactive transcription factor in the cytoplasm; this changes the shape of the transcription factor's DNA-binding site, activating it; the transcription factor moves from the cytoplasm into the nucleus; it binds to the promoter region of the target gene; this stimulates RNA polymerase, so the gene is transcribed.
❓ Suggest why glucagon cannot act on gene expression in the same way as oestrogen.
Glucagon is a protein hormone and is not lipid-soluble, so it cannot diffuse through the phospholipid bilayer; instead it must bind a receptor on the outside of the cell membrane and act via a second-messenger cascade, rather than binding a transcription factor directly inside the cell.
❓ A newly developed drug binds tightly and permanently to the oestrogen receptor. Explain the effect this would have on transcription of oestrogen's target gene.
The drug binds to the oestrogen receptor, preventing oestrogen from binding; the transcription factor is not activated, so it cannot move into the nucleus or bind the promoter region; RNA polymerase is not stimulated, so transcription of the target gene is not increased.

What epigenetics is

❓ Define the term "epigenetics."
Epigenetics involves heritable changes in gene function, without any change to the base sequence of DNA, caused by changes in the environment.
❓ Explain why an epigenetic change is described as reversible, whereas a mutation is not.
An epigenetic change alters chemical tags on the DNA or histones, without altering the base sequence itself, so the tags can in principle be removed and the original level of gene expression restored; a mutation permanently alters the base sequence, which cannot simply be reversed.

Methylation vs acetylation

❓ Complete the following: state the effect on transcription of (a) increased methylation of DNA and (b) increased acetylation of histones.
(a) Increased methylation of DNA decreases/inhibits transcription, because it blocks transcription-factor and RNA-polymerase binding and condenses chromatin. (b) Increased acetylation of histones increases/stimulates transcription, because it loosens the DNA–histone interaction and makes chromatin more accessible.
❓ Explain, in terms of charge, why decreased histone acetylation reduces the rate of transcription.
Histones are positively charged and DNA is negatively charged, so removing acetyl groups makes the histones more positively charged; this increases their attraction to DNA, so DNA binds more tightly to the histones, chromatin condenses, and transcription factors/RNA polymerase cannot access the gene.
❓ A student writes "acetylation of the DNA increases transcription." Identify and correct the error.
Acetylation acts on histone proteins, not on DNA itself — methylation is the modification that acts on DNA. The corrected statement should read "acetylation of the histones increases transcription."

Epigenetics & disease

❓ Explain how abnormal DNA methylation could lead to uncontrolled cell division.
Increased methylation of a tumour suppressor gene can silence it, removing its normal inhibitory effect on the cell cycle; alternatively, decreased methylation of a proto-oncogene can over-activate it into an oncogene; either route can result in uncontrolled, rapid cell division.
❓ Suggest how a drug that reduces DNA methylation could be used to treat cancer.
If the drug reduces methylation of a silenced tumour suppressor gene, the gene can be expressed again, restoring its normal control over the cell cycle and helping to prevent uncontrolled cell division.
❓ A study finds that identical twins raised in different environments show different ages of onset of a particular disease, despite having identical DNA sequences. Explain this observation.
Different environmental factors experienced by each twin (such as diet, smoking, or stress) can cause different amounts of DNA methylation or histone acetylation; this changes gene expression without altering the DNA base sequence, which can account for the different ages of onset despite identical genotypes.

RNA interference

❓ Describe how siRNA can prevent a specific protein from being made.
A double-stranded RNA molecule is cut by an enzyme into short siRNA fragments; one strand of an siRNA fragment binds, by complementary base pairing, to a specific target mRNA; this marks the mRNA for cutting/degradation before it can reach a ribosome; because the mRNA is destroyed, it cannot be translated, so the corresponding protein is not made.
❓ A gene is transcribed normally, but almost no corresponding protein is detected in the cell. Explain how RNA interference could account for this.
Since transcription occurs normally, the gene itself and the process producing mRNA are unaffected; siRNA complementary to this mRNA could bind to it and mark it for degradation before translation, so despite mRNA being produced, little or no protein is made — the signature of control acting at translation, not transcription.
❓ Explain why it would be incorrect to describe RNA interference as "reducing the amount of mRNA transcribed."
RNA interference does not affect transcription at all — the full amount of mRNA is transcribed normally; siRNA instead binds to and degrades the mRNA that has already been made, so it is translation, not transcription, that is inhibited.
