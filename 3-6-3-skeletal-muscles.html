Skeletal Muscles as Effectors

How a nerve impulse becomes a physical pull: antagonistic muscle pairs, the structural chain from whole muscle down to a single sarcomere, the calcium-triggered cross-bridge cycle that actually shortens it, phosphocreatine's rapid ATP top-up, and why slow- and fast-twitch fibres are built so differently.

Section 1 · spec content point

🦴 Why muscles must work in antagonistic pairs

A short, low-tariff idea that everything else in this page assumes is already understood — muscles are pullers, never pushers, so moving a joint both ways always takes two of them.

📖

What the specification says: "Muscles act in antagonistic pairs against an incompressible skeleton."

1
2
3

Imagine two dogs on leads, standing on opposite sides of a door — each dog can only pull its own lead, never push it, and it can't get longer once it's short. To open the door one way, dog A pulls; to swing it back, dog A has to let go and dog B pulls instead. Your muscles work in exactly these pulling pairs across your joints.

  • Flexor = the muscle that bends a joint
  • Extensor = the muscle that straightens it again
  • Antagonistic pair = two muscles pulling against each other
  • Incompressible = can't be squashed or bent out of shape

Muscles are like ropes: they can only pull, never push, and they can't actively stretch themselves back out once they've shortened — relaxing is a passive process. So a joint that needs to move in two directions needs two muscles working as a tug-of-war pair on opposite sides of it. One muscle (the flexor) contracts to pull the joint one way while its partner relaxes; then, to reverse the movement, the partner (the extensor) contracts while the first one relaxes. The bones in between have to stay rigid and unyielding, otherwise the pulling force would just bend the skeleton instead of moving the joint.

  • A muscle can only actively contract and pull — it cannot push, and it cannot actively lengthen itself; any lengthening back out is passive.
  • Moving a joint in two directions therefore needs an antagonistic pair: one muscle (the flexor) contracts to move the joint one way while its partner relaxes; the second muscle (the extensor) then contracts to reverse the movement while the first relaxes.
  • Classic worked example: at the elbow, the biceps is the flexor — it contracts to bend the arm — and the triceps is the extensor — it contracts to straighten it again.
  • The skeleton itself must be incompressible (rigid, non-deformable) so that a contracting muscle's pulling force is transmitted efficiently into moving the bone at the joint, rather than being absorbed by the skeleton itself bending or compressing.
📌

This is a short, low-tariff point, but it's the assumption every later section in this page rests on: a muscle is a one-way puller, so the "return stroke" always needs a second, separate muscle.

Section 2 · spec content point

🔬 Skeletal muscle structure — from whole muscle to sarcomere

A structural hierarchy that has to be learned strictly in order, ending on the labelled resting sarcomere almost every micrograph question on this spec point is based on.

📖

What the specification says: "Gross and microscopic structure of skeletal muscle. The ultrastructure of a myofibril."

1
2
3

Think of a muscle like a thick cable made of thinner ropes, which are themselves made of even thinner threads. The whole muscle is the cable; bundles of muscle fibres are the ropes; the threads inside each fibre are the myofibrils; and each myofibril is really just a row of tiny beads strung end to end — those beads are called sarcomeres, and it's inside them that the actual squeezing happens.

  • Sarcolemma = the fibre's outer "skin"
  • T-tubule = a tunnel folding in from that skin, carrying the signal deep inside
  • Sarcoplasmic reticulum = a tiny calcium storage tank wrapped around each thread
  • Myofibril = one of the threads
  • Sarcomere = one bead/segment along the thread

Muscle is built like a set of nesting dolls. A whole muscle is made of bundles of long fibre cells. Each fibre cell packs together hundreds of thinner strands called myofibrils, all sitting in a shared cytoplasm (the sarcoplasm) that also contains many mitochondria. Each myofibril is a repeating chain of segments called sarcomeres, and it's inside these that the pulling actually happens, using thin protein strands (actin) sliding past thicker ones (myosin). The fibre's outer membrane — the sarcolemma — dips inward at intervals as tunnels called T-tubules, so a nerve signal can reach deep inside the cell almost instantly, and a network called the sarcoplasmic reticulum wraps around every myofibril, ready to release stored calcium the moment it's needed.

The structural hierarchy — learn this chain in order

Whole muscle → bundles of muscle fibres → one muscle fibre (a single, long cylindrical cell — multinucleate, with cytoplasm called the sarcoplasm and a cell membrane called the sarcolemma, which folds inward at intervals as T-tubules to carry depolarisation deep into the cell) → many myofibrils per fibre, each surrounded by sarcoplasmic reticulum (stores and releases Ca²⁺) → each myofibril built from a chain of repeating sarcomeres → each sarcomere built from overlapping actin (thin) and myosin (thick) filaments.

SarcolemmaThe muscle fibre's cell membrane; folds inward at intervals to form T-tubules.

The I-band (actin only) sits either side of it; the H-zone (myosin only, centre of the A-band) sits inside it. Section 3 shows what happens to each width during contraction.

Identifying bands and zones from a micrograph or diagram

Use the diagram above as the reference: the I-band (actin only) is the lighter region either side of the Z-line; the H-zone (myosin only) is the lighter central strip within the darker A-band; the darkest, overlapping region is where both actin and myosin are present together. Comparing a micrograph showing only actin present with one showing both actin and myosin present is itself used as direct visual evidence for the sliding-filament model.

▶ 🚩 Trap — vague descriptions of bands and zones

A vague description such as "the light bit" or "the middle bit" earns nothing on a labelling question — you must use the precise term: A-band, I-band, H-zone or Z-line. If you're less sure which named region a description points to, "actin only" or "actin and myosin present" style descriptions are usually still accepted as long as they distinguish the regions correctly — but the named term is always the safer, stronger answer.

Section 3 · spec content point

⚙️ The roles of actin, myosin, calcium ions and ATP in myofibril contraction

The single highest-mark set piece in this spec point: a strict, repeating four-step cycle, with two genuinely separate ATP-dependent steps that are very easy to conflate into one vague "ATP is used" statement.

📖

What the specification says: "The roles of actin, myosin, calcium ions and ATP in myofibril contraction."

1
2
3

Picture pulling in a rope hand-over-hand: one hand grabs the rope (bind), pulls it towards you (power stroke), lets go (detach), and swings back to grab further along (re-cock) — and you keep repeating that to reel the rope in. That's basically what a tiny "hand" on the myosin filament does to the actin filament, over and over, using little bursts of energy from ATP each time.

  • Cross bridge = the moment a myosin "hand" is attached to actin
  • Power stroke = the pulling motion
  • ATP = the muscle's energy currency, spent to let go and to reset the "hand"

Once the actin binding sites are exposed (see Section 4), contraction runs through a four-step, repeating cycle. First, a myosin head that's already primed with stored energy grabs onto actin — forming a cross bridge. Second, the head bends over, like a rower's stroke, and that bending drags the actin filament along — the power stroke. Third, a fresh ATP molecule lands on the myosin head and makes it let go of actin. Fourth, that same ATP is broken apart, and the energy released cocks the head back into position, ready to grab actin again further along. This cycle repeats for as long as calcium is around; each repeat means actin slides a little further under myosin, so the segments that contain only actin, or only myosin, get squeezed narrower — even though neither filament itself gets any shorter.

🔗

The trigger for all of this comes from outside the myofibril itself: an action potential reaching the neuromuscular junction (see Topic 3.6.2.2) depolarises the sarcolemma, and this depolarisation spreads down the T-tubules, deep into the fibre — where it triggers the calcium release covered in Section 4.

The cross-bridge cycle

Click a numbered dot, or use Next / Back above, to step through the cycle — bind → power stroke → detach → re-cock, repeating further along the actin filament while calcium ions remain bound.
▶ 🚩 The two separate ATP-dependent steps students conflate

ATP binding to the myosin head (step 3, detach) and ATP hydrolysis of that same molecule (step 4, re-cock) are two separate events, at two separate moments in the cycle — conflating them into one vague "ATP is used" loses marks even when the general idea is right. ATP binding causes detachment; the following, separate hydrolysis of that ATP is what re-cocks the head.

State precisely what ATP is needed for

Four distinct uses — never collapse them into one vague "contraction needs ATP":

  1. Cross-bridge formation/attachment — requires an already-cocked, pre-energised myosin head.
  2. The power stroke itself — powered by energy stored from an earlier hydrolysis, with ADP and inorganic phosphate released as it happens.
  3. A new ATP molecule binding — causes detachment of the myosin head from actin.
  4. Hydrolysis of that ATP — re-cocks the head; separately, ATP is also used for the active transport of Ca²⁺ back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum for relaxation.
💡

Why ATP, rather than another energy source, is suitable here: ATP releases a relatively small, near-instantaneous quantity of energy exactly where and when it is needed, and can be rapidly re-synthesised — unlike a bulk fuel store, which would release energy too slowly and too non-specifically for the fine, rapid control a cross-bridge cycle needs.

What happens to each band and zone during contraction

The sarcomere, the I-band and the H-zone all decrease in length. The A-band stays the same length, because the filaments slide past one another rather than shortening themselves — it is the degree of overlap between actin and myosin that changes, never the length of either filament.

Band / zoneWhat happens on contraction
Z-lineAdjacent Z-lines are pulled closer together — the sarcomere as a whole shortens.
A-bandStays constant — the single most commonly tested "does not change" fact on this spec point.
I-bandShortens — actin slides further under the (fixed-length) myosin filaments.
H-zoneShortens, and can disappear entirely with a strong contraction, as actin fills more of the myosin-only zone.
Same A-band width in both rows, because it is fixed by myosin filament length. The I-band and H-zone are both visibly narrower after contraction, because actin has slid further inward, increasing the overlap zone at the expense of the actin-only and myosin-only regions. With a strong enough contraction, the H-zone can disappear completely.
▶ 🚩 Trap — "the A-band shortens"

This is the single most heavily tested misconception on the whole spec point. The A-band is fixed by the length of the myosin filaments and is the one region that never changes — do not write that it shortens, narrows, or decreases, under any circumstances.

▶ Precise wording worth reusing for the ATP/enzyme terminology

Never write that ATP hydrolysis "produces" energy — it releases energy that was already stored in the molecule. And never call the enzyme that catalyses this hydrolysis in the myosin head "ATP synthase" — that enzyme runs the reverse (resynthesis) reaction; the correct name is ATP hydrolase, also called myosin ATPase.

Section 4 · spec content point

🔑 The roles of calcium ions and tropomyosin in the cycle of actinomyosin bridge formation

The specific trigger step that unlocks Section 3's cycle: how the myosin-binding site on actin goes from covered to exposed. Troponin is not required by the specification — build the answer around calcium ions and tropomyosin instead.

📖

What the specification says: "The roles of calcium ions and tropomyosin in the cycle of actinomyosin bridge formation. (The role of troponin is not required.)"

1
2
3

Imagine a row of light switches (the binding sites) with a curtain (tropomyosin) pulled across, hiding them. Calcium ions arriving pull that curtain back, so the switches become reachable and myosin can flick them. No calcium arriving means the curtain stays shut, the switches stay hidden, and nothing happens.

  • Tropomyosin = the "curtain" protein sitting on actin
  • Binding site = the "light switch" myosin needs to reach
  • Calcium ions = the signal that pulls the curtain back

Actin filaments have "on switches" studded along them — the myosin-binding sites — but at rest, a helper protein called tropomyosin sits right over the top of them, blocking myosin from reaching them. When calcium ions flood in from the sarcoplasmic reticulum, they cause tropomyosin to shift position and slide out of the way, uncovering the binding sites. Only once they're uncovered can myosin heads actually grab on and form a cross bridge. Take calcium away again, and tropomyosin slides back to cover the sites, so no new cross bridges can form.

  • Following depolarisation of the T-tubules, Ca²⁺ diffuses out of the sarcoplasmic reticulum and into the sarcoplasm surrounding the myofibrils.
  • Calcium ions cause tropomyosin to move / change position — shifting it away from the myosin-binding site on actin.
  • This movement exposes the binding site on the actin filament.
  • With the site exposed, myosin heads can now attach, forming an actinomyosin cross bridge — the starting point of the cross-bridge cycle in Section 3.
Without calcium, tropomyosin sits over the binding site and no cross bridge can form. Calcium ions cause tropomyosin to move, exposing the binding site, so a myosin head can attach.
📌

A note on troponin: mentioning troponin in an answer is not penalised, but it earns nothing on its own — the specification explicitly excludes it, so build written answers around calcium ions and tropomyosin. Exam questions are marked identically whichever direction they're framed: the calcium-present framing (calcium present → tropomyosin moves → binding sites exposed → cross bridges form) and the calcium-absent framing (calcium absent → binding sites stay covered → no cross bridges form) are both credited equally, so be ready to argue either direction.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "active site" instead of "binding site"

Never write "active site" for where a myosin head attaches to actin — actin has a binding site; an active site specifically belongs to an enzyme. This is one of the most frequently repeated wording errors on this spec point, even among students who clearly understand the mechanism.

Section 5 · spec content point

🔋 The roles of ATP and phosphocreatine in muscle contraction

A short, high-yield point that's often extended into an application question about fibre type or age — phosphocreatine is a rapid, short-lived top-up, not a long-term energy store.

📖

What the specification says: "The roles of ATP and phosphocreatine in muscle contraction."

1
2
3

Think of phosphocreatine as a spare battery kept right next to your muscle's main power supply. The instant the main battery (ATP) runs a bit low, the spare battery hands over some charge to top it back up immediately. But the spare battery is small, so it only lasts a few seconds before the muscle needs a proper recharge from breathing (respiration).

  • ADP = the "used up" energy molecule
  • Phosphocreatine = the spare battery, a phosphate-storing molecule
  • Resynthesis = rebuilding something (ATP, here) after it's been used

Phosphocreatine is like a small emergency top-up tank of energy sitting right there in the muscle fibre. It hands over one of its phosphate groups to a spent ADP molecule, instantly rebuilding it into ATP — no need to wait for respiration to catch up. But the tank is tiny, so it only buys a few seconds of maximal effort before the muscle has to switch over to breaking down glucose, first anaerobically and then aerobically, to keep making ATP.

  • Phosphocreatine phosphorylates ADP to rapidly regenerate ATP: ADP + phosphocreatine → ATP + creatine.
  • This reaction needs no oxygen and no multi-step pathway, so it is the fastest way to replenish ATP at the very start of intense exercise, before anaerobic and then aerobic respiration catch up.
  • The phosphocreatine store is limited — enough to sustain only a few seconds of maximal contraction — so anaerobic and then aerobic respiration must take over ATP resupply once it is exhausted.
🎯

Application — fibre type and age: fast-twitch fibres hold larger phosphocreatine stores, matching their need for rapid, powerful, short-duration contraction; phosphocreatine resynthesis slows with age, reducing the capacity for very short, powerful bursts of activity in older individuals.

▶ Why phosphocreatine only helps briefly — the fuller version

Phosphocreatine is used up rapidly during contraction to remake ATP, and fast-twitch fibres in particular rely on it because they're built for rapid, brief, powerful contractions. As people age, phosphocreatine takes longer to resynthesise — partly reflecting a slower overall metabolic rate — which is part of why short, explosive bursts of activity become harder to sustain with age even though endurance capacity may be less affected.

Section 6 · spec content point

🏃 Slow- and fast-twitch muscle fibres

A comparison built almost entirely from mechanistic reasons, not bare facts — plus the mechanism behind muscle fatigue, and how to evaluate a muscle-force investigation.

📖

What the specification says: "The structure, location and general properties of slow and fast skeletal muscle fibres."

1
2
3

Think of slow-twitch fibres like a marathon runner — built to keep a steady pace for ages without stopping, fuelled by plenty of oxygen. Fast-twitch fibres are like a sprinter — explosive and powerful, but they run out of steam fast.

  • Myoglobin = a protein that stores oxygen in muscle, like a tiny oxygen tank
  • Capillary = a tiny blood vessel
  • Fatigue = when a muscle can't keep contracting as strongly

Slow-twitch fibres are built for the long haul: lots of mitochondria, a dense blood supply, and a high store of the oxygen-holding pigment myoglobin — which is why they look dark red — let them keep going on aerobic respiration for a long time, even though each contraction is relatively weak and slow. Fast-twitch fibres are built for short, powerful bursts: fewer mitochondria and capillaries, but a big glycogen store to fuel fast, if inefficient, anaerobic respiration — so they fatigue quickly. Fatigue itself happens partly because aerobic ATP production slows, so fewer actin-myosin cross bridges can be sustained, and partly because the muscle leans more on anaerobic respiration, causing lactate to build up.

Explaining muscle fatigue mechanistically

Fatigue develops for two linked reasons: reduced ATP production by aerobic respiration means fewer actin-myosin interactions can be sustained; and increased reliance on anaerobic respiration causes lactate to build up, which contributes to the sensation of fatigue.

Evaluating experimental data on muscle force

Common valid criticisms of a small-scale muscle-force investigation: a limited species or sample tested (results cannot be generalised); uncontrolled body temperature; too few pH (or other variable) values tested; the use of isolated tissue rather than a whole organism (may not reflect in-vivo behaviour); and no statistical test applied to support a claimed difference.

▶ 🚩 Trap — assuming there's no valid comparison

A question interpreting muscle-force data across a pH or temperature range is often poorly answered because students assume there is no valid comparison or control available, without checking what the typical/baseline value for that tissue actually represents. Always check for a baseline before concluding a comparison isn't possible.

▶ Where this point sits in difficulty terms

This is A-level-only content with no AS-tier equivalent, and it's ranked medium–high difficulty: memorisation load is medium–high (a long causal chain with a strict order), application depth is high (unfamiliar contexts are explicitly examined — see Section 8), misconception frequency is high, and terminology precision risk is high. The sliding-filament mechanism is usually recalled reasonably well in outline, but answers lose marks steadily on precision — naming an "active site" instead of a binding site, describing ATP's role only vaguely, or omitting the tropomyosin-binding-site shift where the fuller version of the mechanism is being tested.

Section 8 · exam technique

🧭 Applying the sliding-filament principle to unfamiliar contexts

Examiners specifically flag unfamiliar application contexts as a weak point. The fix is always the same: strip the mechanism down to its transferable core, then re-apply it.

A well-known unfamiliar scenario describes myosin pulling actin to move an attached mitochondrion along a nerve axon — an intracellular transport role for the same actin–myosin machinery, entirely outside a sarcomere. Many students revert to a rote-learnt sarcomere-shortening answer instead of applying the general sliding-filament principle — myosin heads binding, pulling, detaching, re-cocking — to the specific, novel scenario described.

🧠

How to tackle any unfamiliar sliding-filament question: strip the mechanism down to its transferable core — a myosin head binds a binding site on an actin (or actin-like) filament, uses ATP-derived energy to pull or move relative to it, then detaches and re-cocks to repeat — and re-apply that core to whatever is described, whether it's a sarcomere shortening or an organelle being hauled along a track.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "mitochondria produce energy" in an unfamiliar-context answer

Where ATP's role appears in an unfamiliar context — for example, mitochondria providing ATP for an organelle's movement — state that mitochondria supply energy/ATP, never that they "produce" energy. This is a common wording error even when the underlying idea is otherwise correct.

Section 9 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

This content works well as the "mechanism" paragraph inside a longer essay-style answer — the trick is closing each paragraph with a sentence that ties the mechanism back to whatever the title is actually about.

Three plausible essay titles this spec point could anchor: "The importance of muscles and muscle contraction to organisms," "The importance of electrical events in living organisms," and "Cells communicate with one another to coordinate the activities of the whole organism." In a muscle-contraction title, 3.6.3 is naturally the core paragraph, drawing together ATP's role in each step, aerobic/anaerobic respiration supplying that ATP and explaining fibre-type differences, the neuromuscular junction as the trigger, and skeletal muscle as an effector at the end of a reflex arc. In an electrical-events title, muscle contraction is a clean example of an electrical event — depolarisation of the sarcolemma — being converted, through a defined chain, into macroscopic mechanical movement.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around electrical-to-mechanical conversion

An action potential arriving at the neuromuscular junction triggers the same calcium-dependent process as any cholinergic synapse, ultimately causing calcium ions to diffuse into the myofibril from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Calcium ions cause tropomyosin to move, exposing binding sites on actin so that myosin heads can attach, forming actinomyosin cross bridges; ATP hydrolysis then drives the power stroke. Muscle contraction is therefore the direct mechanical consequence of an electrical event being converted first into a chemical trigger, and then into a physical one.

Essay technique: whenever a later paragraph needs an organism to move, respond, or transport something internally, name the specific mechanism — actin–myosin cross bridges, ATP hydrolysis, Ca²⁺-tropomyosin — rather than leaving "the muscle contracts" implicit, and always trace the chain back one more step to what triggered it: a nerve impulse, an action potential at the sarcolemma.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Antagonistic pairs

  • A muscle only pulls — never pushes, never actively lengthens.
  • Flexor (e.g. biceps) bends a joint; extensor (e.g. triceps) straightens it — always a pair.
  • The skeleton must be incompressible so the pull is transmitted, not absorbed.

2 · Structure — muscle to sarcomere

  • Muscle → fibres (multinucleate, sarcolemma with T-tubules) → myofibrils (surrounded by sarcoplasmic reticulum) → sarcomeres → actin + myosin.
  • I-band = actin only; H-zone = myosin only (inside A-band); overlap = darkest region.
  • Always use exact terms — A-band, I-band, H-zone, Z-line — never "the light/middle bit."

3 · Actin, myosin, Ca²⁺ & ATP

  • Cycle: bind → power stroke → detach → re-cock, repeating while Ca²⁺ is bound.
  • ATP binding causes detachment; a separate ATP hydrolysis re-cocks the head — don't conflate them.
  • A-band never changes; I-band and H-zone shorten (H-zone can vanish) because overlap increases.
  • Never "active site" (say binding site); never "ATP synthase" (say ATP hydrolase); ATP "releases," never "produces," energy.

4 · Calcium & tropomyosin

  • Ca²⁺ (from the sarcoplasmic reticulum) causes tropomyosin to move, exposing the binding site on actin.
  • Troponin is not required — build the answer around calcium ions and tropomyosin only.
  • Calcium-present and calcium-absent framings are marked identically.

5 · ATP & phosphocreatine

  • Phosphocreatine + ADP → ATP + creatine — the fastest possible ATP top-up, no oxygen needed.
  • Store only lasts a few seconds — then anaerobic, then aerobic, respiration take over.
  • Fast-twitch fibres store more phosphocreatine; resynthesis slows with age.

6 · Slow- vs fast-twitch fibres

  • Slow: aerobic, many mitochondria/capillaries, high myoglobin, fatigue-resistant, postural/endurance use.
  • Fast: anaerobic, few mitochondria, high glycogen, fatigues quickly, sprinting/power use.
  • Fatigue = less aerobic ATP (fewer cross bridges sustained) + lactate build-up from anaerobic respiration.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Antagonistic pairs

❓ Explain why moving a joint in two directions requires two separate muscles rather than one.
A muscle can only actively contract and pull — it cannot push, and it cannot actively lengthen itself; so one muscle (the flexor) is needed to move the joint one way, and a second, separate muscle (the extensor) is needed to reverse the movement, contracting while the first relaxes.
❓ Explain why the skeleton needs to be incompressible for muscles to move the body effectively.
If the skeleton could be compressed or deformed, the pulling force generated by a contracting muscle would be absorbed by the skeleton bending rather than being transmitted into moving the bone at the joint; an incompressible skeleton transmits the force efficiently instead.

Structure — muscle to sarcomere

❓ Describe the structural hierarchy of a skeletal muscle, from the whole muscle down to a single sarcomere.
A whole muscle is made of bundles of muscle fibres; each fibre is a single, multinucleate cell containing many myofibrils in a shared sarcoplasm, bounded by a sarcolemma that folds inward as T-tubules; each myofibril is surrounded by sarcoplasmic reticulum and built from a chain of repeating sarcomeres; each sarcomere, running Z-line to Z-line, contains overlapping actin (thin) and myosin (thick) filaments.
❓ A micrograph of a myofibril shows three distinct regions of shading. Identify each region and state what it contains.
The lightest region either side of the Z-line is the I-band, containing actin filaments only; the lighter central strip within the darker band is the H-zone, containing myosin filaments only; the darkest region is where actin and myosin overlap, containing both filament types together.
❓ State the function of the sarcoplasmic reticulum and explain why it needs to surround every myofibril individually.
The sarcoplasmic reticulum stores calcium ions and releases them when the adjacent T-tubule depolarises; surrounding every myofibril individually means calcium can be released close to each one simultaneously, allowing the whole fibre to contract together rather than gradually.

Actin, myosin, Ca²⁺ & ATP

❓ Describe the sequence of events in the cross-bridge cycle, from cross-bridge formation to the myosin head being ready to bind again.
An energised myosin head binds to the exposed binding site on actin, forming a cross bridge; the myosin head bends, pulling the actin filament past the myosin filament — the power stroke — releasing ADP and inorganic phosphate; a new ATP molecule then binds to the myosin head, causing it to detach from actin; hydrolysis of that ATP molecule re-cocks the myosin head, ready to bind again further along the actin filament.
❓ State precisely what ATP is needed for during a single cross-bridge cycle, naming each use separately.
Cross-bridge attachment requires an already-energised myosin head; the power stroke is powered by energy from an earlier hydrolysis; a new ATP molecule binding causes detachment of the myosin head from actin; hydrolysis of that ATP re-cocks the head; ATP is also used separately for the active transport of calcium ions back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum during relaxation.
❓ A student writes "the A-band gets shorter during contraction." Assess whether this statement is correct and explain your reasoning.
The statement is incorrect. The A-band is fixed by the length of the myosin filaments and does not change during contraction; it is the I-band and H-zone that shorten, because the actin filaments slide further across the myosin filaments, increasing the degree of overlap — neither filament itself becomes any shorter.

Calcium & tropomyosin

❓ Explain the roles of calcium ions and tropomyosin in allowing a cross bridge to form.
Calcium ions, released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum, cause tropomyosin to move on the actin filament; this movement exposes the myosin-binding site that was previously covered; with the binding site exposed, a myosin head can now attach to actin, forming an actinomyosin cross bridge.
❓ Explain what happens to cross-bridge formation when calcium ion concentration falls, in terms of tropomyosin.
As calcium ion concentration falls, tropomyosin moves back to cover the myosin-binding sites on actin; fewer, or no, actinomyosin cross bridges can then form, since myosin heads can no longer attach; contraction therefore stops and the muscle can relax.

ATP & phosphocreatine

❓ Explain the role of phosphocreatine in supplying ATP at the start of vigorous exercise.
Phosphocreatine phosphorylates ADP to rapidly regenerate ATP, releasing creatine as a by-product; because this reaction needs no oxygen and involves no multi-step pathway, it is the fastest available way to resupply ATP before anaerobic, and then aerobic, respiration take over.
❓ Explain why an athlete's performance in a very short, powerful event declines with age, in terms of phosphocreatine.
Phosphocreatine resynthesis becomes slower with age, so the phosphocreatine store cannot be replenished as quickly between efforts; since fast-twitch fibres rely on a large, rapidly available phosphocreatine store for short, powerful bursts, a slower resynthesis rate reduces the capacity for repeated maximal effort.

Slow- vs fast-twitch fibres

❓ Compare the mitochondrial density and myoglobin content of slow- and fast-twitch fibres, and explain the functional significance of each difference.
Slow-twitch fibres have many mitochondria and a high myoglobin content, supporting a high rate of sustained aerobic respiration and giving a local oxygen store for prolonged activity; fast-twitch fibres have few mitochondria and low myoglobin, reflecting their reliance on anaerobic respiration for short, powerful bursts rather than sustained aerobic output.
❓ Explain the mechanism of muscle fatigue during prolonged exercise.
Reduced ATP production by aerobic respiration means fewer actin-myosin cross bridges can be sustained, reducing the force generated; increased reliance on anaerobic respiration causes lactate to build up in the muscle, which contributes to the sensation of fatigue.
❓ A student concludes from a graph of muscle force against pH that "there is no valid baseline for comparison." Suggest why this conclusion might be wrong, and give two other valid criticisms of a small-scale muscle-force investigation.
The conclusion may be wrong because the student has not checked what the typical/baseline pH for that tissue actually is before assuming no comparison exists. Other valid criticisms: a limited number of species or samples were tested, so results cannot be generalised; isolated muscle tissue was used rather than a whole organism, which may not reflect in-vivo behaviour; and no statistical test was applied to support any claimed difference.
