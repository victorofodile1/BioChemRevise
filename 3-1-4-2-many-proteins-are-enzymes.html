Many Proteins Are Enzymes

⚠️

The single habit that protects the most marks on this spec point: the active site is complementary to the substrate — never "the same shape as," which gets rejected outright. And never let the sentence "the enzyme lowers the activation energy" go missing from a mechanism answer — it's the easiest available mark on this whole topic, and also the one candidates leave out most often.

Section 1 · spec content point

🔓 Activation energy & the induced-fit model

This is the single most-tested mechanism question on this spec point, and almost every mark lost here is a wording mark, not a biology mark — the ideas themselves are simple once the exact phrasing is locked in.

📖

What the specification says: "Each enzyme lowers the activation energy of the reaction it catalyses. The induced-fit model of enzyme action."

1
2
3

Think of the enzyme's active site as a glove and the substrate as a hand. In induced fit, the glove isn't already hand-shaped — it moulds itself snugly around the hand the moment the hand pushes in. That moulding is exactly what makes the job (the reaction) easier and quicker to get started. The old idea, called lock-and-key, was more like a rigid glove that was already perfectly hand-shaped before anyone put a hand in — it could only ever fit one exact hand, and never adjusted.

  • Substrate — the molecule the enzyme works on
  • Active site — the pocket on the enzyme where the substrate fits
  • Complementary — shaped to fit exactly, like two jigsaw pieces (not "the same shape")
  • Activation energy — the energy "push" needed to get a reaction started
  • Enzyme-substrate complex — the enzyme and substrate joined together

An enzyme speeds up a reaction by making it easier to get started. The substrate fits into a pocket on the enzyme called the active site, and the two combine into what's called an enzyme-substrate complex. In the induced-fit model, that pocket isn't a rigid, ready-made shape: as the substrate arrives, the active site moulds around it and becomes complementary to it, tugging on — distorting — the bonds inside the substrate. That moulding is exactly what makes the reaction need less energy to start: it lowers the activation energy. The older lock-and-key model pictured the active site as already the perfect fixed shape, like a key sliding into a lock; induced-fit replaced it because a fixed-shape active site couldn't explain how one enzyme can handle more than one type of substrate, or how some inhibitors work by changing the active site's shape from a completely different part of the enzyme.

The three-part mechanism examiners test relentlessly

  1. The substrate binds to the enzyme's active site — equally well described as an enzyme-substrate complex forming (accept "fits" for "binds").
  2. As it binds, the active site changes shape slightly so that it becomes complementary to the substrate — this is equally creditable as describing the active site distorting, stretching, or breaking bonds within the substrate.
  3. That shape change is what lowers/reduces the activation energy needed to start the reaction — the mark most often left out, so never skip it.
📌

Marking habit: all three active-site points can be written as one flowing sentence, with "complementary" dropped in wherever it fits naturally. You don't have to explain what physically causes the shape change — only that it happens. It's equally valid to describe the active site, before binding, as "not complementary to" or "not fitting" the substrate.

The active site (blue) starts non-complementary to the substrate (amber), moulds around it as an enzyme-substrate complex forms, then releases the products (green) unchanged and ready to bind again. The gap between the two energy curves is the activation energy the enzyme saves.

Contrasting lock-and-key with induced-fit

Point of comparisonLock-and-keyInduced-fit
Active site shapeFixed, rigid — already complementary to one substrate before bindingFlexible — not initially complementary, moulds around the substrate as it binds
Does the active site change shape?NoYes, slightly, as the substrate binds

Similarity, worth stating for any "contrast" question: in both models the substrate fits/binds to the active site and an enzyme-substrate complex is formed — that shared step is what a "contrast" answer needs stated as well as the difference.

▶ 🚩 Trap — reversing who changes shape

Never write "the substrate changes shape" — this is the reverse of the correct idea. It is always the active site that changes shape, never the substrate.

▶ 🔬 Why the model changed — a named "how science works" skill

The lock-and-key model couldn't explain how a single enzyme catalyses reactions with more than one substrate, or how non-competitive inhibitors manage to reduce activity without ever touching the active site itself. New evidence — including images of enzymes actually bound to their substrates — showed that the active site does change shape, so the model was revised to fit the new evidence. This is a good, concrete example of a scientific model being updated as evidence accumulates, and it's directly examinable in its own right.

Section 2 · spec content point

🧩 Enzyme specificity

Every specificity question is testing the same chain of logic: tertiary structure gives the active site its exact shape, and only a complementary substrate can bind it. Learn the chain once and every version of this question becomes recognisable.

📖

What the specification says: "The properties of an enzyme relate to the tertiary structure of its active site and its ability to combine with complementary substrate(s) to form an enzyme-substrate complex. The specificity of enzymes."

1
2
3

Think of a jigsaw puzzle: only one exact piece slots into a particular gap, because of its precise shape and bumps. The enzyme's active site is like the gap, and only the one substrate that's shaped to match — complementary — is the piece that fits. If you carve that piece into a different shape (like modifying the substrate chemically), it no longer slots in at all, and nothing happens.

  • Tertiary structure — the enzyme's final, folded 3D shape, which builds the active site
  • Complementary — shaped to fit exactly (not "the same shape")
  • Specificity — an enzyme only working on one substrate, or a small family of very similar ones

Every enzyme's active site is a particular 3D shape, built by the enzyme's tertiary structure. Only one substrate — or a small family of very similar substrates — has a matching, complementary shape that can actually bind there; that's why enzymes are so picky about what they'll react with. When two completely different enzymes happen to act on the same substrate, it's because their active sites have ended up with very similar or identical tertiary structures — effectively the same amino acid sequence in that region — so both are complementary to it. And if a substrate is chemically altered even slightly, its shape stops matching the active site, so it can no longer bind, and the enzyme simply can't act on it any more.

Why an enzyme only acts on one substrate

  1. The enzyme's active site has a specific tertiary structure.
  2. Because of that structure, only the correct substrate has a shape that is complementary to it — able to bind or fit.
  3. Binding forms an enzyme-substrate complex; without one forming, no reaction is catalysed.
🚩

Two ways this gets capped hard: writing "same shape" instead of "complementary," and describing the active site as being on the substrate rather than on the enzyme. The active site always belongs to the enzyme; the substrate is what's complementary to it.

When two different enzymes act on the same substrate

Both enzymes' active sites have similar or identical tertiary structures — you can equally say identical amino acid sequences in the region that forms the active site — so both are able to form an enzyme-substrate complex with the same substrate. It's also acceptable to describe this as both enzymes having active sites complementary to different parts of the same, usually larger, substrate molecule.

When a substrate is modified so it's no longer recognised

If a substrate is chemically altered, it becomes a different or wrong shape — no longer complementary to the active site — so it is unable to fit or bind to it, and no enzyme-substrate complex forms, meaning the enzyme can no longer act on it.

📌

A slip that's forgiven once: if you name the wrong type of bond involved (for example writing "peptide" where "ester" is meant), it's only marked down once — a single naming slip doesn't cost you the equivalent point every time it's implied later in the same answer.

Section 3 · spec content point

⚙️ Six factors that change reaction rate, and the two inhibitors

Every one of these explanations ends the same way: more, or fewer, enzyme-substrate complexes forming. Learn that link and each factor becomes a short, structured answer rather than something to memorise separately.

📖

What the specification says: "The effects of the following factors on the rate of enzyme-controlled reactions — enzyme concentration, substrate concentration, concentration of competitive and of non-competitive inhibitors, pH and temperature."

1
2
3

Imagine enzymes as seats at a busy café and substrate molecules as customers looking for a seat. More seats (more enzyme) or more customers (more substrate) means more people get seated per minute — up to a point, after which one of the two runs out and adding more of the other doesn't help any more. A competitive inhibitor is like an impostor who sits down in an actual seat, so a real customer can't — bring enough real customers and eventually they win seats back. A non-competitive inhibitor is sneakier: it's like someone bending a chair out of shape from across the room, so no customer can sit properly there at all, no matter how many customers turn up.

  • Optimum — the temperature or pH at which an enzyme works fastest
  • Denatured — permanently lost its normal 3D shape, so it can no longer work
  • Allosteric site — a binding site elsewhere on the enzyme, not the active site itself

Whatever the question — the amount of enzyme, the amount of substrate, the pH, the temperature, or an inhibitor — trace it back to the same idea: does this change make it easier or harder for enzyme and substrate to meet and bind? More successful meetings means a faster rate; fewer means a slower one. Raising temperature (up to the optimum) speeds particles up, so they collide and bind more often; raising it past the optimum breaks the bonds holding the enzyme's shape together, so the active site stops fitting the substrate at all — the enzyme is denatured, permanently. Moving pH away from the optimum does the same thing through a different route: it changes the charges on the enzyme, breaking the same kind of bonds. A competitive inhibitor physically sits in the active site itself, so more substrate can shove it back out; a non-competitive inhibitor binds somewhere else entirely and warps the active site's shape from a distance, so no amount of extra substrate can fix a mis-shaped active site.

The reference table — every row ends in more or fewer enzyme-substrate complexes

FactorMechanism
Enzyme concentration (excess substrate)Rate rises in proportion to enzyme concentration — more active sites are available; the rate plateaus once substrate becomes the limiting factor instead.
Substrate concentration (excess enzyme)Rate rises as more enzyme-substrate complexes form; it then plateaus because every active site is occupied/saturated — enzyme concentration is now limiting.
Competitive inhibitor concentrationThe inhibitor is complementary to the active site and binds it directly, so fewer or no ES complexes form. This is overcome by raising substrate concentration.
Non-competitive inhibitor concentrationThe inhibitor binds an allosteric site (not the active site), changing the tertiary structure so the active site is no longer complementary. This is not overcome by more substrate.
pH away from optimumChanges the charges on R groups, breaking ionic/hydrogen bonds → tertiary structure changes → active site no longer complementary → the enzyme is denatured.

🔑

The phrase every row needs: "…so more/fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form." Write that link explicitly — don't stop at "more collisions happen" or "the bonds break," always finish the sentence back to ES complexes and rate.

The two types of inhibitor, in full detail

Competitive: the inhibitor has a shape similar to, or complementary to, the active site, so it fits into and binds with the active site itself, preventing or reducing substrate binding — fewer or no enzyme-substrate complexes form. This effect is overcome by increasing substrate concentration, because there is then more substrate competing for the same active sites.

Non-competitive: the inhibitor binds at a site other than the active site — the allosteric site — changing the enzyme's tertiary structure so the active site is no longer complementary to the substrate. Increasing substrate concentration does not overcome this, because the active site itself is now the wrong shape, regardless of how much substrate is present.

Competitive: the inhibitor (purple) is complementary to the active site itself, competing directly with the substrate — more substrate wins the active site back. Non-competitive: the inhibitor binds elsewhere (the allosteric site), warping the active site's shape via the enzyme's tertiary structure — no amount of extra substrate fixes a mis-shaped active site.
▶ 🚩 Trap — "same shape" for a competitive inhibitor

Never write "same shape/same structure" for a competitive inhibitor — it is complementary to the active site, not identical to it. Also avoid implying the active site or enzyme is damaged, destroyed, or made permanently useless by an inhibitor — inhibition (especially competitive inhibition) can be a temporary, reversible block, not destruction.

📌

A strong inhibitor answer covers three things every time: (1) the inhibitor's shape, (2) where it binds, and (3) the consequence for enzyme-substrate complex formation.

Reading a rate-against-time curve

The rate of increase slows as the reaction proceeds because substrate is being used up — there's a high initial rate because plenty of substrate is available and many enzyme-substrate complexes can form at once. The curve then levels off and shows no further increase because there is no substrate left to react.

▶ 🚩 Trap — never say the enzyme is "used up"

Enzymes are catalysts and are not consumed by the reaction — never describe an enzyme as being "used up." If a curve levels off, name the substrate running out as the cause, not a vague reference to it being "a limiting factor."

Calculating pH from hydrogen ion concentration

pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]. A solution with a hydrogen ion concentration of 1 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ therefore has a pH of 5. Rearranged, [H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ. Because the scale is logarithmic, a fall of just one pH unit represents a ten-fold increase in hydrogen ion concentration — practise converting in both directions.

📈

Temperature and pH curves share one underlying reason for failing: past the optimum, changed conditions break the ionic/hydrogen bonds holding the active site's tertiary structure together, so it stops being complementary to the substrate and the enzyme is denatured — permanently. Below the optimum temperature, the curve rises because of more kinetic energy and more successful collisions; there's no equivalent "rising" explanation on the low-pH side of a pH curve.

Section 4 · spec content point

🧪 Required practical 1 — investigating enzyme reaction rate

This is the practical that decides how well this whole topic is understood, because the marks are won or lost on precision — naming exactly what's controlled and how, not just promising to "keep things the same."

📖

What the specification says: "Required practical 1: Investigation into the effect of a named variable on the rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction."

1
2
3

It's like a fair cooking trial: you only ever change one ingredient's amount at a time, keep every other ingredient and condition exactly the same, and you always run one extra batch with a "broken" version of the key ingredient (an enzyme that's been boiled, left out, or swapped for plain water) so you know any difference you see afterwards is really down to what you changed — not something else sneaking in.

  • Independent variable — the one thing you deliberately change
  • Control variable — everything you deliberately keep the same
  • Initial rate — how fast the reaction is going right at the very start

You're always changing exactly one thing at a time — temperature, pH, substrate amount, or enzyme amount — and keeping everything else the same, including running a fair control that has no active enzyme in it. You then measure how fast the reaction goes, either by timing until something visible happens (like a colour disappearing), or by tracking a continuous measurement over time and reading off its steepest early slope, right at the start, as the initial rate. Working out that slope means drawing a tangent to the graph — a straight line that just touches the curve at one point — and using its gradient. You also need to think about how precise your measuring equipment actually is: uncertainty.

The four workable independent variables, and how rate is measured

AQA names four workable independent variables for this practical: temperature, pH, substrate concentration and enzyme concentration — investigate any one, holding the others constant. Rate is measured either by timing a fixed, visible end-point (for example, the time for a starch-iodine mixture to stop turning blue-black, or the time for a cross to disappear through a cloudy suspension), or by measuring a continuous quantity against time (for example, the volume of oxygen produced from catalase and hydrogen peroxide, or colorimeter absorbance) and finding the initial rate from the resulting graph.

Identifying the variables to control

  • The size, dimensions or mass of the tissue used
  • The temperature of the solution
  • The pH of the solution — the same volume of the same buffer/pH solution each time
  • The same concentration or mass of substrate at the start
  • The same concentration or mass of enzyme
🚩

Vague control statements score badly. Never simply write "same pH" — state precisely what would be kept the same, and how: "use a buffer solution of a known, stated pH," not just "keep pH the same." Describing a proper control for an enzyme investigation is a well-known discriminator between weak and strong answers on this practical, and the usual failure mode is changing the independent variable itself (for example swapping the enzyme, or adding an inhibitor) instead of genuinely controlling every other variable.

Designing the control

The control tube must contain buffer and substrate, plus one of the following in place of active enzyme:

  • Buffer + substrate + denatured enzyme (for example, boiled first)
  • Buffer + substrate + no enzyme at all
  • Buffer + substrate + water in place of the enzyme

Finding the initial rate using a tangent

Draw a straight line that just touches the curve at the chosen point (a tangent), extend it, then take two widely separated points on that line and calculate the change in y divided by the change in x to find its gradient. For the initial rate, the tangent is always drawn at time zero — because that's the point at which substrate concentration is at its highest and hasn't yet started to fall, giving the fairest comparison between different conditions being tested.

Calculating and expressing rate

Where the measurement is a time taken, rate = 1 ÷ time (or a fixed volume ÷ time). Always give the answer to a consistent number of significant figures — a correct value given to an inconsistent or excessive number of significant figures is only worth partial credit.

📎

Uncertainty: for a single measurement, the uncertainty is ± half the smallest division of the instrument used; percentage uncertainty = (uncertainty ÷ measured value) × 100. Reduce it by using an instrument with finer graduations, or by measuring a larger quantity overall.

Stopping the reaction at a chosen point

Each method needs its explanation paired with it — stating one without the other loses the mark:

MethodWhy it stops the reaction
Boil the mixture, or add a strong acid/alkaliDenatures the enzyme
Put it in ice, a fridge, or a freezerLowers kinetic energy so no further enzyme-substrate complexes form
Add a high concentration of inhibitorEnzyme-substrate complexes no longer form

Turning a colour-based method into a quantitative one

Make up a series of solutions of known, different concentrations of the coloured substance and carry out the same colour test on each. Use a colorimeter to measure each one and plot a calibration curve — with concentration on the x-axis and colorimeter reading on the y-axis — then read the concentration of the unknown reaction sample off that curve.

Section 5 · exam technique

🎯 Exam skills: why this is one of the harder topics, and the command words it uses

This spec point is genuinely one of the harder ones — not because the biology itself is conceptually difficult, but because so much of the credit depends on exact wording.

Why students "know the biology" here but still lose marks

This spec point carries a high memorisation load and, on top of that, a high risk of losing marks purely on terminology and wording precision — the distinction between "complementary" and "same shape," or "the active site changes shape" versus "the substrate changes shape," is exact and unforgiving. At the earlier stage of the course the recall-level demand (reproducing the three-mark mechanism, interpreting a rate curve) is moderate; later on the demand becomes higher, requiring genuine application — contrasting two models, explaining why two different enzymes can share a substrate, and designing and describing a control from scratch rather than recalling one.

Describing a proper control for an enzyme investigation is a well-known discriminator: many answers change the independent variable itself instead of controlling it, or write something too vague to credit, like "keep conditions the same." Separately, "the enzyme lowers the activation energy" is consistently the easiest available mark and also the single most commonly missed phrase — many answers stop at a GCSE-level description of the substrate simply "fitting into" the active site, without ever mentioning activation energy at all.

🧭

How to fix it: don't revise this as "I understand enzymes" and move on. Drill the exact three-step mechanism (bind → active site changes shape/becomes complementary → activation energy lowered) until it's automatic, then separately rehearse writing a control as a precise recipe — naming the exact substance and stating exactly how it's controlled — rather than a vague promise to "keep it the same."

⚠️ Common mistakes & precise wording

Almost all the difficulty on this spec point comes down to swapping a vague phrase for a precise one. These are the slips that come up again and again.

Vague or wrong versionWhat to say instead
"Same shape" for the active site and substrateComplementary — shaped to fit together, not identical
"The substrate changes shape"It is always the active site that changes shape, never the substrate
The active site described as being on the substrateThe active site belongs to the enzyme; the substrate is complementary to it
"Same shape/structure" for a competitive inhibitorComplementary to the active site — not identical to it
Non-competitive inhibition explained using competitive-style language (binding directly to/blocking the active site)A non-competitive inhibitor binds an allosteric site, changing the tertiary structure so the active site is no longer complementary

▶ Where students tend to half-answer a "contrast" question

The lock-and-key versus induced-fit "state the difference" question is a recurring one that's usually half-answered: the similarity (that the substrate binds the active site) is well known, but far fewer answers state the precise difference — writing something like "the enzyme changes shape" instead of "the active site changes shape," and failing to state that the lock-and-key active site is a fixed, unchanging shape from the start. Always write both halves of a contrast explicitly, in the exact terms the sentence needs.

Section 7 · exam technique

✍️ Using this spec point in a 25-mark essay

This content works especially well as the opening, "founding" paragraph of a longer essay — establishing complementary tertiary structure as vocabulary you can then reuse whenever the essay needs another example of specific binding.

Two essay titles are built directly on this spec point: "Enzymes control the reactions that take place in living organisms", and "The importance of complementary shapes of molecules in organisms." It also supplies a strong founding paragraph for essays on the importance of specificity in biology, or on the control of chemical reactions in organisms — anywhere the essay needs its first, concrete example of a complementary, tertiary-structure interaction.

▶ Model founding paragraph

An enzyme is a globular protein whose active site — built by its tertiary structure — is complementary to a specific substrate, allowing an enzyme-substrate complex to form. In the induced-fit model, the active site changes shape slightly as it binds, distorting bonds within the substrate and lowering the activation energy needed for the reaction, without the enzyme itself being used up in the process. This same mechanism explains why an enzyme is specific to just one substrate, why raising temperature or moving pH away from the optimum denatures it by breaking the bonds holding its active site's shape together, and why competitive and non-competitive inhibitors reduce rate through two genuinely different routes.

🧭

Essay technique: use this spec point as the paragraph that establishes "complementary tertiary structure" as vocabulary, then reuse those exact words later whenever a receptor, antibody, or another binding site needs to bind something specifically. Examiners credit an explicit vocabulary link carried across paragraphs, not just correct biology explained in isolation each time.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Activation energy & induced fit

  • Substrate binds active site → enzyme-substrate complex forms.
  • Active site changes shape slightly, becomes complementary to the substrate (distorts bonds in it).
  • This lowers the activation energy — the easiest mark to forget, never leave it out.
  • Lock-and-key: active site rigid, already complementary. Induced-fit: active site starts non-complementary, moulds/flexible.
  • Never say "the substrate changes shape" — it's always the active site.

2 · Enzyme specificity

  • Active site has a specific tertiary structure.
  • Only a complementary substrate can bind → forms an enzyme-substrate complex.
  • "Complementary," never "same shape"; the active site is on the enzyme, never the substrate.
  • Two enzymes on one substrate: similar/identical tertiary structures (or amino acid sequences).
  • Modified substrate = wrong shape/not complementary → can't bind → no ES complex, no reaction.

3 · Six factors & two inhibitors

  • Every explanation ends in more/fewer enzyme-substrate complexes forming.
  • Enzyme/substrate concentration: rate rises, then plateaus once the other becomes limiting.
  • Competitive inhibitor: complementary to active site, blocks it directly — overcome by more substrate.
  • Non-competitive inhibitor: binds allosteric site, distorts active site shape — not overcome by more substrate.
  • pH/temperature past optimum: bonds break → tertiary structure changes → active site no longer complementary → denatured.

4 · Required practical 1

  • Four workable independent variables: temperature, pH, substrate concentration, enzyme concentration.
  • Measure rate via a fixed visible end-point, or a continuous measurement — then find the initial rate.
  • Control = buffer + substrate + denatured enzyme / no enzyme / water — never just "keep it the same."
  • Draw the tangent at time zero for initial rate — substrate concentration is highest there.
  • Rate = 1 ÷ time; keep significant figures consistent; % uncertainty = (½ smallest division ÷ measured value) × 100.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Activation energy & induced fit

❓ A student is asked to explain how an enzyme catalyses a reaction. Write a full-credit three-point answer.
The substrate binds to the enzyme's active site, forming an enzyme-substrate complex; the active site changes shape slightly as it binds so that it becomes complementary to the substrate, distorting bonds within it; this change lowers the activation energy needed for the reaction to begin.
❓ Contrast the lock-and-key model with the induced-fit model of enzyme action.
Similarity: in both models the substrate binds to the active site and an enzyme-substrate complex forms. Difference: in the induced-fit model the active site changes shape (is flexible) as the substrate binds, whereas in the lock-and-key model the active site is already a fixed, complementary shape and does not change (is rigid).
❓ Explain why the lock-and-key model was replaced by the induced-fit model.
The lock-and-key model could not explain how a single enzyme catalyses reactions involving more than one substrate, or how non-competitive inhibitors reduce activity without binding the active site itself. New evidence, including images of enzymes bound to their substrates, showed that the active site does change shape as it binds — so the model was revised to fit the new evidence.

Enzyme specificity

❓ Explain why an enzyme will only catalyse a reaction involving one particular substrate.
The enzyme's active site has a specific tertiary structure; only the correct substrate has a shape that is complementary to that active site and is able to bind or fit; binding forms an enzyme-substrate complex, and without one forming the reaction cannot be catalysed.
❓ Two different enzymes are both found to act on the same substrate molecule. Explain how this is possible.
Both enzymes' active sites have similar or identical tertiary structures — equally describable as identical amino acid sequences in the region forming the active site — so both are able to form an enzyme-substrate complex with the same substrate molecule.
❓ A substrate molecule is chemically modified before being mixed with its normal enzyme. Explain why the enzyme is no longer able to act on it.
The modified molecule is now a different, wrong shape and is no longer complementary to the enzyme's active site, so it is unable to bind or fit into it; without an enzyme-substrate complex forming, the enzyme cannot catalyse the reaction.

Six factors & two inhibitors

❓ Explain the difference between how a competitive inhibitor and a non-competitive inhibitor reduce the rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction.
A competitive inhibitor has a shape complementary to the active site, so it binds directly to the active site itself, preventing or reducing substrate binding and so reducing the number of enzyme-substrate complexes formed — this effect is overcome by increasing substrate concentration, since more substrate molecules then compete for the same active sites. A non-competitive inhibitor binds at a different site, the allosteric site, changing the tertiary structure of the enzyme so that the active site is no longer complementary to the substrate — increasing substrate concentration does not overcome this, because the active site itself is now the wrong shape.
❓ A graph shows the rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction rising then levelling off as substrate concentration increases. Explain this shape.
At low substrate concentrations, rate rises because more substrate molecules mean more enzyme-substrate complexes form per unit time; the curve then plateaus because, at high substrate concentration, all of the enzyme's active sites are occupied or saturated, so enzyme concentration — not substrate concentration — becomes the limiting factor.
❓ Explain why raising the temperature above an enzyme's optimum causes the rate of reaction to decrease sharply.
Above the optimum, the extra kinetic energy causes hydrogen and ionic bonds holding the enzyme's tertiary structure in shape to break; this changes the shape of the active site so it is no longer complementary to the substrate, so fewer enzyme-substrate complexes can form — the enzyme is denatured, and this change cannot be reversed.

Required practical 1

❓ Describe a valid control for an investigation into the effect of pH on the rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction.
The control should contain the same buffer solution and the same concentration or mass of substrate as the test tubes, but with the enzyme either denatured (for example by boiling it first), left out entirely, or replaced with an equal volume of water — this shows that any change in rate seen in the test tubes is due to the pH, and not simply due to something else changing.
❓ Explain how you would use a graph of product formed against time to find the initial rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction.
Draw a tangent to the curve at time zero — the point where substrate concentration is highest and has not yet started to fall, giving the fairest comparison between different conditions — then take two widely separated points on that tangent line and calculate the change in the y-value divided by the change in the x-value to find the gradient, which is the initial rate.
❓ A student uses a colorimeter to measure the progress of an enzyme-controlled reaction that produces a coloured product, but their raw absorbance readings alone don't tell them the concentration. Describe how they could make this method quantitative.
Make up a series of solutions of known, different concentrations of the coloured product and take a colorimeter reading of each one to plot a calibration curve, with concentration on the x-axis and colorimeter reading on the y-axis; the absorbance reading from the actual reaction mixture can then be read against this calibration curve to find its concentration.
