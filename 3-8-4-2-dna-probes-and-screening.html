DNA Probes, Screening & Personalised Medicine

How a labelled DNA probe uses complementary base pairing to locate one exact allele — and how that single mechanism feeds three exam-tested applications: screening patients for heritable conditions, drug responses and health risks, genetic counselling, and personalised medicine.

⚠️

The single habit that protects the most marks on this spec point: never stop at "the probe is complementary to the target allele." A full answer needs all three parts locked together — the complementary base sequence, binding/hybridisation by specific base pairing, and detection of the label (autoradiography or UV fluorescence). The second most costly habit: writing that a patient "will develop" a condition when a health-risk allele only shows an increased risk/probability of developing it.

Section 1 · spec content point

🧬 The DNA probe mechanism — locating one exact allele

Every question on this page, however it's dressed up, is really testing whether you can hold three ideas together at once: sequence, binding, detection. Lock those into one habit and most of this spec point falls into place.

📖

What the specification says: "The use of labelled DNA probes and DNA hybridisation to locate specific alleles of genes."

1
2
3

Imagine a huge tangled ball of wool made of two threads twisted together — that's your DNA, double-stranded. A DNA probe is like a tiny fishing hook with its own name tag that glows in the dark, cut to catch onto one exact stretch of thread and no other. Before the hook can catch anything, you have to unzip the wool into two single threads (denature it) so the pattern on each thread is exposed. Drop the glowing hook in, and it only catches on if the pattern matches perfectly — even one wrong stitch and it won't hold. Wash away every hook that didn't catch, then look for the glow (or slide in an X-ray film) to see exactly where your target thread was hiding.

  • DNA probe = the glowing, name-tagged hook
  • Denature = unzipping the double thread into two single ones
  • Complementary = "shaped to fit exactly"
  • Hybridise = the hook catching on because the pattern matches
  • Detect = spotting where the glow, or the dark patch, shows up

A DNA probe is a short, single strand of DNA that's been given a label — either something radioactive, or something that glows under UV light — and its base sequence is built to be the exact complementary match of one specific allele. Before it can be used, the double-stranded target DNA has to be denatured, usually by heating it, which breaks the hydrogen bonds holding the two strands together and exposes the bases. The probe is then added and hybridises: it only pairs up and sticks where its sequence is complementary, and because even a single base that doesn't match is enough to stop it binding properly, the same probe can tell a mutant allele apart from the normal version of the same gene. Anything that hasn't bound is washed off, and whatever's left is detected — either as a dark band on X-ray film (if the label was radioactive) or as a glow under UV light (if it was fluorescent).

The three-part probe answer — what each part actually needs

PartWhat must be stated
1. SequenceThe probe is a short, single-stranded, labelled (radioactive or fluorescent) piece of DNA whose base sequence is complementary to part of the target allele's sequence.
2. BindingOnce the target DNA has been made single-stranded, the probe binds/hybridises to it by specific complementary base pairing (new hydrogen bonds form).
3. DetectionUnbound probe is washed away, and the remaining label is detected — by autoradiography (dark band on X-ray film) or under UV light (fluorescence).

Stating "complementary" on its own, with no reference to binding or to how the label is actually revealed, only earns partial credit — all three parts are needed together.

A probe is single-stranded, labelled, and complementary to the target allele. Target DNA is denatured to expose its bases, the probe hybridises only where a full match exists (one base mismatch stops binding), unbound probe is washed away, and the label is then detected.

The full procedure for locating one specific allele

  1. Extract the DNA from the sample.
  2. Cut it into fragments using restriction enzymes (restriction endonucleases).
  3. Separate the fragments by gel electrophoresis.
  4. Make the DNA single-stranded — denature it, usually by heating.
  5. Add the labelled probe; it hybridises (binds by specific complementary base pairing) only to the target allele.
  6. Wash away any unbound probe, then detect the label — by autoradiography or under UV light.
▶ 🚩 The step most often left out

The single-strand (denaturation) step is the one most often missing from a "describe the full procedure" answer. A double-stranded fragment already has its own bases paired to each other, so it cannot bind a probe at all until it has been denatured — this is a distinct, separately creditable step, not just part of "using a probe."

Screening for several mutations at once

A sample can be screened for several possible mutations simultaneously: the DNA is amplified by PCR, cut with restriction enzymes, and separated by electrophoresis, then several different labelled probes are applied together — each specific allele present is identified either by its fluorescence/radioactivity, or by comparing band positions against a sample of known mutations. A DNA microarray is an accepted alternative to running a gel: thousands of different probes are fixed in known positions on a plate, labelled DNA from the patient is washed over it, and wherever a probe binds, that spot lights up — its fixed position identifying exactly which allele is present.

Why a heterozygote gives a weaker signal than a homozygote

A heterozygote carries only one copy of the target allele, compared with two copies in a homozygote, so there are fewer complementary target sequences available for the probe to bind — the heterozygote therefore produces roughly half the signal intensity of a homozygote for that same allele.

📌

This idea is usually tested as quantitative data interpretation — being handed a graph or a set of signal-intensity readings and asked to explain the pattern — rather than recalled as an isolated fact. Expect it dressed up as "interpret this result," not "define this term."

▶ 🚩 The weakest national answer

DNA-probe questions are recorded as weak nationally — many answers only state that a probe "detects specific DNA sequences," without ever mentioning complementary base pairing, or the single-stranded, labelled nature of the probe itself. Define a probe fully every time: short, single-stranded, labelled, with a base sequence complementary to the target.

Section 2 · spec content point

🩺 Screening for heritable conditions, drug responses & health risks

The same probe-and-hybridisation mechanism from Section 1 is reused for three distinct clinical purposes. Naming which one a question is describing — and choosing the right register of certainty — is what actually earns the marks.

📖

What the specification says: "The use of labelled DNA probes that can be used to screen patients for heritable conditions, drug responses or health risks."

1
2
3

Picture three different questions a doctor could ask the very same glowing probe to answer. First: "does this person carry the faulty thread for an inherited illness?" Second: "will this person's body break this particular medicine down too fast, too slowly, or just right?" Third: "is this person more likely — not certain, just more likely — to develop an illness like a certain cancer later in life?" Same tool, three completely different jobs.

  • Heritable condition = an inherited illness caused by a faulty allele
  • Drug response = how someone's body handles a particular medicine
  • Health risk = a raised chance, not a guarantee, of getting something later

Labelled probes are used to screen patients for three distinct things. For heritable conditions, a probe detects a disease-causing allele — sometimes before any symptoms appear, or even in a fetus or embryo. For drug responses, a probe detects an allele that affects how quickly or effectively someone metabolises a particular drug, so the dose or choice of drug can be tailored to them — this is called pharmacogenomics. For health risks, a probe detects an allele that raises the probability, not the certainty, of developing a condition later — for example, certain cancers. That last distinction — probability versus certainty — is specifically tested, and mixing the two up is a real, examined error, not just a style choice.

CategoryWhat is being detected
Heritable conditionsDisease-causing alleles — including pre-symptomatic, fetal or embryonic testing, before any symptoms appear.
Drug responsesAlleles that affect how a drug is metabolised, used to guide the dose or choice of drug for an individual (pharmacogenomics).
Health risksAlleles that raise the probability, not the certainty, of developing a condition — e.g. of certain cancers.
▶ 🚩 "At increased risk of" vs "will develop"

Whether an answer distinguishes "at increased risk of developing" (probabilistic, correct) from "will develop" (deterministic, wrong) is specifically examined. A health-risk allele only ever shows a raised probability — overstating what a screening result actually shows is a real, credited-against error, not a stylistic nuance.

Three direct medical uses of a probe result

  • Identifying carriers of a recessive disease allele — people who show no symptoms themselves, but could pass the allele on to their children.
  • Identifying which specific allele/gene is present in a patient.
  • Identifying the most effective treatment for that patient, based on their genotype.
📌

Naming habit worth building now: if a question describes a scenario, identify precisely which of the three categories it belongs to before writing anything else — a "heritable condition" answer and a "health risk" answer are marked to different standards of certainty, and mixing them up costs marks even if the underlying biology is otherwise correct.

Section 3 · spec content point

🧭 Genetic counselling, personalised medicine & evaluating screening

The clinical pathway from a probe result to patient care, plus AQA's own named evaluation skill for this spec point, balanced both ways rather than argued one-sidedly.

📖

What the specification says: "The use of this information in genetic counselling and personalised medicine." (AQA also expects you to be able to evaluate information relating to screening individuals for genetically determined conditions and drug responses — covered in this section too.)

1
2
3

Think of a genetic counsellor like a travel guide who lays out every possible route on a map and explains exactly what each one involves — but never tells you which road to take. That's their whole job: give information, not orders. Personalised medicine is more like a tailor: instead of handing every patient the exact same medicine at the exact same dose, it alters the choice and the amount to fit one person's body exactly, based on their genes. And when you're asked to weigh up whether all this screening is a good idea, you always need one real "for" and one real "against" — never just one side of the story.

Genetic counselling is a process that advises people on the probability of inheriting or passing on a genetic condition, may involve screening other family members, and explains the treatment or reproductive options available — but it supports rather than makes the individual's or couple's own decision; it is informative, not directive. Personalised medicine uses a patient's genetic profile to choose the drug and dose most likely to work for them, and to avoid drugs predicted to be ineffective or to cause an adverse reaction, because alleles affect both how quickly someone breaks a drug down and how strongly their receptors respond to it. When asked to evaluate screening, a strong answer names a genuine benefit — like earlier diagnosis — against a genuine consideration — like the psychological impact of a positive result — rather than just describing screening as straightforwardly good or bad.

Genetic counselling — informative, not directive

Genetic counselling advises patients and families on the probability of inheriting or passing on a condition, may involve screening other family members to identify carriers, and explains the nature, treatment or reproductive options available — but it supports, rather than instructs, the individual's or couple's own decision. This "informative, not directive" framing is the precise idea examined here, not a vaguer description of simply "giving advice."

Personalised medicine

Personalised medicine uses an individual's genetic profile to select the drug and dose most likely to work for that specific person, and to avoid drugs predicted to be ineffective or to cause an adverse reaction — because alleles affect both drug metabolism (how quickly a drug is broken down) and receptor expression (how strongly a drug binds its target). It also allows earlier intervention for conditions a person is shown to be at increased risk of.

Evaluate: screening for genetically determined conditions and drug responses

AQA's own command here is evaluate: a balanced answer weighs named benefits against named considerations for the specific condition or drug in the question — a one-sided answer, however long, is capped.

FOR — potential benefitsCONSIDER — real drawbacks

Judging statistical significance from named-group screening data

When screening data is presented for named groups, the credited phrasing is precise: "the probability that the difference between [named group] is due to chance is less than 5% (0.05), so the difference is significant" — you must state which specific group shows, or does not show, a significant association.

▶ 🚩 "The results are significant" is not enough

A bare "the results are significant," without naming the specific group and without the "less than 5%/0.05 probability due to chance" phrasing, is rejected wording for this skill — always name the group, the direction of the finding, and give the numerical threshold.

▶ Where this point sits in difficulty terms

The underlying mechanism itself is short — one core technique, reused three ways — but it is tested as a complete multi-step procedure, paired with quantitative signal-interpretation data and a genuinely two-sided evaluation, so the memorisation load is low-to-medium while the application depth and terminology-precision risk are both high. DNA-probe questions are recorded as weak nationally: the complementary base pairing mechanism is frequently missing from otherwise plausible-sounding answers, and the single-stranded step is the detail most often left out of the full procedure. The risk-vs-certainty language distinction, and the precise statistical-significance phrasing, are both specifically examined precision points rather than generic recall.

▶ Don't confuse this with gel electrophoresis of protein

Gel electrophoresis reappears throughout this area of the specification, and it is easy to default to discussing DNA fragments and base sequences even when a question specifies protein separation instead. The general principle — charged fragments move through a gel under an electric current, and smaller fragments travel further — applies to both, but a question about protein separation is not a DNA-probe question, and should not be answered as one.

Section 5 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

This content works well as a supporting paragraph inside a longer essay-style answer on more than one theme — the trick is closing each paragraph with a sentence that ties the mechanism back to whatever the title is actually about, rather than repeating a generic summary.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around specificity in biological processes

A DNA probe is a short, single-stranded, labelled section of DNA complementary to part of a target allele; it binds by DNA hybridisation, forming new hydrogen bonds through complementary base pairing, and because even a single base mismatch is enough to prevent stable binding, the probe locates only its one intended target. This is one of the clearest applied examples in the specification of specificity through base-sequence complementarity — the entire technique depends on binding only one target allele among an entire genome.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around evaluating a technology

Labelled DNA probes allow patients to be screened for heritable conditions, drug responses and health risks, informing genetic counselling — which explains probabilities and options to families without directing their decision — and personalised medicine, which chooses the drug and dose most likely to work for an individual based on their genotype. Earlier diagnosis and better-targeted treatment must be weighed against genuine drawbacks: psychological harm from a positive result, confidentiality and discrimination risks, and the fact that a "risk" allele is never a certainty.

📌

A real AQA essay title has been built directly around DNA as an information-carrying molecule and its use in gene technologies — this spec point supplies a ready-made worked example for that kind of title. A plausible predicted title along similar lines: "the importance of specificity in biological processes."

Essay technique: whichever title is set, complete the probe mechanism through all three parts — sequence, binding, detection — before moving on to its applications; a description that stops at "complementary" is under-developed. For an evaluation-flavoured title, name at least one genuine benefit and one genuine consideration rather than asserting that screening is simply "good" or "bad."

Where 3.8.4.2 reappears across the rest of the specification

  • 3.8.1 Gene mutation — the base-sequence changes that create the disease-causing or normal alleles a probe distinguishes.

Rule of thumb: whenever "DNA hybridisation" appears elsewhere in the specification, check what is actually being compared — a labelled probe against one target allele (this spec point, and genetic fingerprinting), or the DNA of two whole species against each other (species and taxonomy). The base-pairing principle is identical, but the purpose and the credited wording differ.

🧠

Memory hook — sequence, bind, detect. The probe's base sequence is complementary to the target allele; it binds (hybridises) only where that match exists, once the target DNA is single-stranded; its label is then detected — autoradiography for radioactive, UV glow for fluorescent. And for the applications: counsel, don't command — genetic counselling informs, it does not instruct; personalised medicine matches the drug to the genome, not the genome to the drug.

Self-check before moving on

  • I can define a DNA probe fully every time — short, single-stranded, labelled (radioactive or fluorescent), complementary base sequence to the target allele — and never stop at "complementary" alone.
  • I can describe the full locating procedure in order: extract → cut with restriction enzymes → separate by electrophoresis → denature to single strands → hybridise with a labelled probe → wash away unbound probe → detect the label.
  • I can explain why a single base mismatch can prevent hybridisation, and why this lets a probe distinguish a mutant allele from the normal allele.
  • I can name and distinguish the three screening categories — heritable conditions, drug responses, health risks — and never write "will develop" when the correct phrasing is "at increased risk of developing."
  • I can define genetic counselling (informative, not directive) and personalised medicine (genotype-matched drug/dose choice) precisely, and give a genuinely balanced, named evaluation of genetic screening rather than a one-sided answer.
  • I can use the credited statistical-significance phrasing — "the probability that the difference between [named group] is due to chance is less than 5%/0.05" — instead of a bare "the results are significant."

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · The DNA probe mechanism

  • Probe = short, single-stranded, labelled DNA, base sequence complementary to the target allele.
  • Denature the target DNA first (breaks H-bonds, exposes bases) — this step is the one most often left out.
  • Hybridise = probe binds only where the sequence fully matches; one base mismatch = no binding.
  • Wash away unbound probe, then detect: autoradiography (radioactive, X-ray film) or UV fluorescence.
  • Full order: extract → cut (restriction enzymes) → separate (electrophoresis) → denature → hybridise → detect.

2 · Screening: conditions, drugs & risk

  • Heritable conditions: disease alleles, testable pre-symptomatically or in a fetus/embryo.
  • Drug responses: alleles affecting drug metabolism → guide dose/choice (pharmacogenomics).
  • Health risks: raised probability, not certainty (e.g. some cancers) — never "will develop."
  • Other direct uses: identify carriers, identify the allele present, identify the most effective treatment.

3 · Counselling & personalised medicine

  • Genetic counselling: informs about probability and options, supports the decision — does not instruct.
  • Personalised medicine: matches drug + dose to genotype (metabolism + receptor expression).
  • Evaluate = a named benefit (e.g. early diagnosis) weighed against a named consideration (e.g. psychological impact, confidentiality, cost, consent) — one-sided answers are capped.
  • Significance: name the group + "probability due to chance is less than 5%/0.05" — never a bare "significant."

The DNA probe mechanism

❓ Explain how a labelled DNA probe locates a specific allele within a sample of extracted DNA.
The probe's base sequence is complementary to the base sequence of the target allele; the target DNA is first denatured (e.g. by heating), breaking the hydrogen bonds between the two strands and exposing the bases; the probe then binds by hybridisation, forming new hydrogen bonds through specific complementary base pairing, only where a fully matching sequence exists; any unbound probe is washed away, and the label is then detected — as a dark band on X-ray film (autoradiography) if radioactively labelled, or as fluorescence under UV light if fluorescently labelled.
❓ Describe, in order, the complete procedure used to locate one specific allele, starting from a blood sample.
Extract the DNA from the sample; cut it into fragments using restriction enzymes; separate the fragments by gel electrophoresis; denature the DNA to form single strands, exposing the bases; add a labelled probe, which hybridises by complementary base pairing only to the target allele; wash away any unbound probe; detect the bound label by autoradiography or under UV light.
❓ A heterozygous patient and a homozygous patient are both tested with the same labelled probe for a particular allele. Explain why the heterozygote's signal is weaker.
The heterozygote has only one copy of the target allele, compared with two copies in the homozygote, so there are fewer complementary target sequences available for the probe to bind; the heterozygote therefore produces roughly half the signal intensity of the homozygote for that allele.

Screening: conditions, drugs & risk

❓ State the three categories of information that labelled DNA probes can be used to screen a patient for.
Heritable conditions — disease-causing alleles, including pre-symptomatic, fetal or embryonic testing; drug responses — alleles that affect how a patient metabolises a drug, used to guide the dose or choice of drug (pharmacogenomics); health risks — alleles that raise the probability, not the certainty, of developing a condition, e.g. certain cancers.
❓ A patient is told they carry an allele associated with an increased risk of a certain cancer. Explain why the statement "this patient will develop cancer" would not be given credit.
A health-risk allele only shows an increased probability/risk of developing the condition, not certainty; other genetic and environmental factors also affect whether the condition actually develops, so "will develop" overstates what the screening result actually shows — the credited phrasing is "at increased risk of developing."
❓ Give three direct medical uses of a DNA probe result, other than simply naming the screening category.
Identifying carriers of a recessive disease allele who show no symptoms themselves; identifying which specific allele/gene a patient carries; identifying the most effective treatment for that patient, based on their genotype.

Counselling & personalised medicine

❓ Define genetic counselling and explain why it is described as "informative, not directive."
Genetic counselling is a process in which people are advised about the probability of inheriting or passing on a genetic condition; it may involve screening other family members to identify carriers, and explaining the nature, treatment and reproductive options available; it is "informative, not directive" because the counsellor supports the individual's or couple's own decision rather than instructing them what to do.
❓ Explain the role of personalised medicine in prescribing, referring to both drug metabolism and receptor expression.
Personalised medicine uses an individual's genetic profile to select the drug and dose most likely to work for that person, because alleles affect drug metabolism (how quickly a drug is broken down) and receptor expression (how strongly a drug binds its target); this avoids prescribing drugs predicted to be ineffective or to cause an adverse reaction for that patient.
❓ In a screening study, one named group showed a statistically significant association with an allele and another did not. Write the conclusion the mark scheme would credit.
For [named group A], the probability that the difference is due to chance is less than 5% (0.05), so the association is significant; for [named group B], the probability that the difference is due to chance is greater than 5% (0.05), so the association is not significant.
