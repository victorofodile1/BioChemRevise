Nerve Impulses

How a neurone generates, propagates and speeds up an electrical impulse — the resting potential, the action potential, the all-or-nothing principle, the refractory period, and what actually makes some axons faster than others.

⚠️

The one habit that protects the most marks on this whole spec point: every mechanism here is either channel-mediated facilitated diffusion or active transport by the sodium–potassium pump — never confuse the two. Depolarisation and repolarisation are channel events only; the pump's job is to help set up and restore the resting potential between impulses, never to cause the spike itself. Say "ions" explicitly wherever they're involved, and always write "action potential" or "impulse" — never "message" or "signal."

Section 1 · spec content point

🔬 The structure of a myelinated motor neurone

Everything else in this topic is a story about specific, named parts of this one cell. Learn the structure once, with each part's job attached, and every later mechanism becomes much easier to describe precisely.

📖

What the specification says: "The structure of a myelinated motor neurone."

1
2
3

Think of a neurone like an electrical cable running from your spinal cord to a muscle. The cell body is the "factory" end where everything the cell needs gets made. The long wire itself is the axon. Wrapped around most of the wire is a rubbery insulating coating — that's the myelin sheath, made by helper cells called Schwann cells. But the coating isn't continuous — there are small bare patches every so often, called nodes of Ranvier, where the wire is exposed.

  • Cell body = the factory end, keeps the cell alive
  • Axon = the long wire that carries the signal
  • Myelin sheath = the insulating coating (it's electrical insulation, not a woolly jumper — it has nothing to do with keeping the cell warm)
  • Nodes of Ranvier = the bare gaps in the coating

A motor neurone has a cell body containing the nucleus and the machinery for making proteins, with short branching dendrites bringing signals in. A single long axon carries the impulse away towards the muscle it controls. Schwann cells wrap around the axon in a series of segments, and the many layers of their membrane form the myelin sheath — a fatty layer that electrically insulates the axon. Between adjacent Schwann cells are small exposed gaps called the nodes of Ranvier, and — as later sections build on — these gaps are the only places along a myelinated axon where the impulse can actually be regenerated.

📌

Marking habit worth building now: if you're asked to label an unfamiliar diagram or micrograph, give each structure with its function from the table above — a bare name on its own scores less than a name paired with what it does. The most tested confusion is mixing up the axon membrane itself with the Schwann cell/myelin wrapped around it — they're drawn touching, but they're two separate structures with two separate jobs.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "thermal" insulation

The myelin sheath gives electrical insulation, not thermal insulation. Writing "thermal insulation" is a persistent, explicitly penalised error — and this vocabulary underpins every resting-potential, action-potential and saltatory-conduction answer that follows, so it's worth getting exactly right here first.

Section 2 · spec content point

🔋 The resting potential

Before any impulse fires, an axon is already doing active work to sit at a stable, negative internal voltage. Three separate mechanisms combine to produce it — keep them distinct rather than blending them into one vague sentence.

📖

What the specification says: "The establishment of a resting potential in terms of differential membrane permeability, electrochemical gradients and the movement of sodium ions and potassium ions."

1
2
3

Imagine two rooms — inside the axon and outside it — each with a different crowd of two types of people: sodium people and potassium people. Even at rest, sodium people are mostly stuck outside and potassium people are mostly stuck inside, but the wall between the rooms leaks potassium people out faster than it lets sodium people in. There's also a tireless doorman (the pump) constantly pushing three sodium people out for every two potassium people he lets back in. All this leaking and pushing leaves the inside of the axon slightly short of positive charge compared to outside — like a battery, charged up and ready to go.

At rest, there's more potassium inside the axon than outside, and more sodium outside than inside — these are the concentration gradients. The membrane is naturally leakier to potassium than to sodium, through permanently open "leak" channels, so potassium ions drift out faster than sodium ions drift in. On top of that, the sodium–potassium pump actively pumps 3 sodium ions out for every 2 potassium ions it pumps in, using ATP, working against both gradients. The net effect of the leakiness difference and the pump together is that the inside of the axon ends up negative relative to the outside — a resting potential of roughly −70 mV.

  1. Concentration gradients: K⁺ concentration is higher inside the axon; Na⁺ concentration is higher outside it.
  2. Differential membrane permeability: the membrane is more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺, via potassium leak channels, so K⁺ diffuses out faster than Na⁺ diffuses in.
  3. Active transport by the sodium–potassium pump: 3 Na⁺ are pumped out for every 2 K⁺ pumped in, per cycle, against their concentration gradients, using ATP.

Points 2 and 3 together leave the inside of the axon negative relative to the outside — the resting potential. This is heavily synoptic with active transport and membrane structure from earlier in the course.

🔢

Quick numbers: the resting potential is typically quoted as around −70 mV (inside negative relative to outside), though some sources give values closer to −65 mV — always use the exact figure given in a question rather than quoting one unprompted.

▶ 🚩 The single costliest omission

Two or three otherwise-correct points but with no reference to ions anywhere caps the answer very low — the word "ions" itself needs to appear, not just "sodium" and "potassium" used loosely without that word. Keep the permeability difference (point 2) and the active pumping (point 3) clearly separate — merging them into one vague "the pump maintains the potential" statement loses credit that keeping them distinct would earn.

Section 3 · spec content point

📈 Depolarisation, repolarisation & the action potential

This is where the resting potential gets deliberately, temporarily overturned. Every step from here on is channel-driven facilitated diffusion — the pump plays no part in the spike itself.

📖

What the specification says: "Changes in membrane permeability lead to depolarisation and the generation of an action potential. The all-or-nothing principle." (This section covers depolarisation, repolarisation and the action potential graph — Section 4 covers the all-or-nothing principle, the second half of the same specification content point.)

1
2
3

Picture a row of doors along the wire, all shut. A strong enough knock (a stimulus reaching threshold) makes the first door fly open, and sodium people rush in — that makes the inside less negative, which makes the next door fly open too, and so on, like dominoes, until the inside actually flips positive for a moment. Then those doors slam shut, and a different set of doors open, letting potassium people rush back out, which brings the voltage back down — sometimes dipping slightly too far down before it settles back to normal, ready to fire again.

If a stimulus is strong enough to push the membrane to around −55 mV (threshold), voltage-gated sodium channels open and sodium ions flood in — this is depolarisation, and because more channels open the more the membrane depolarises, it snowballs (positive feedback) right up to about +40 mV. At that peak, the sodium channels close and voltage-gated potassium channels open instead, so potassium ions flow back out — this is repolarisation, bringing the potential back down. Because those potassium channels are a little slow to shut, slightly too much potassium leaves, so the membrane briefly dips below resting — hyperpolarisation — before settling back at −70 mV.

Depolarisation

  • A stimulus depolarises the membrane; if it reaches threshold (≈ −55 mV), voltage-gated sodium channels open.
  • Na⁺ diffuses in down its electrochemical gradient, through the open channels — facilitated diffusion, never active transport.
  • The membrane becomes less negative, then positive — more channels open as it does so (positive feedback), driving the potential up towards +40 mV (an overshoot past 0 mV).

Repolarisation and hyperpolarisation

  • At the peak, voltage-gated sodium channels close.
  • Voltage-gated potassium channels open, and K⁺ diffuses out down its electrochemical gradient.
  • The membrane potential falls back towards resting; hyperpolarisation follows because the potassium channels are slightly slow to close, so slightly too much K⁺ leaves before they shut — the sodium–potassium pump and K⁺ leak channels then restore the resting potential, ready to fire again.

See it happen: play each scenario

Threshold reached — full action potential
Sub-threshold stimulus — no action potential

▶ 🚩 The "channels, not pumps" rule

Active transport is explicitly rejected for both depolarisation and repolarisation — both are channel-driven facilitated diffusion. "The sodium–potassium pump causes depolarisation/repolarisation" scores nothing at this stage; the pump's role is restricted to establishing and restoring the resting potential between impulses, never to the spike itself.

Section 4 · spec content point

🎚️ The all-or-nothing principle

Size is fixed once threshold is crossed — every action potential in a given neurone looks identical. Intensity is coded a completely different way.

📖

What the specification says: "Changes in membrane permeability lead to depolarisation and the generation of an action potential. The all-or-nothing principle." (Same specification content point as Section 3 — this half covers the all-or-nothing principle specifically.)

1
2
3

A gun either fires or it doesn't — you can't half-pull the trigger and get a smaller bang. Push a neurone hard enough and it fires a full, identical impulse every time; push it too gently and absolutely nothing happens. Want to send a "louder" message? You don't fire a bigger bang — you just pull the trigger more times, more quickly.

Below threshold, a stimulus produces no action potential at all — not a smaller one. At or above threshold, every action potential produced is identical in size and shape. So how does the body signal "stronger" versus "weaker"? Not by the size of any individual impulse, which can't vary once triggered, but by how often action potentials fire (their frequency) and/or how many neurones are firing at once.

  • A stimulus below threshold produces no action potential at all — not a smaller one.
  • A stimulus at or above threshold always produces an action potential of the same size (same peak, same shape).
  • Stimulus strength is coded by the frequency of action potentials (more impulses per second) and/or the number of neurones firing — never by the size of any individual action potential, which cannot vary once triggered.
▶ 🚩 Trap — "a smaller action potential"

A sub-threshold stimulus does not produce "a smaller action potential" — it produces no impulse at all. The all-or-nothing graph above shows exactly this: the sub-threshold trace returns to resting without ever spiking.

Applying it to an unfamiliar drug or toxin

A recurring exam format describes a novel drug or toxin that keeps sodium channels open (rather than closing normally), and asks for the consequence, or for an explanation of resistance to it.

▶ 🧪 Try one yourself — click to reveal the reasoning

Scenario: a newly isolated marine toxin locks voltage-gated sodium channels permanently open once triggered. Predict the effect on the axon.

Reasoning: sodium ions continue to diffuse in through the open channels, so the membrane cannot repolarise back towards resting; without repolarisation the sodium channels cannot reset from their inactivated state, so no further action potential can be generated in that region — the axon effectively stops conducting impulses normally at the affected site.

▶ 🚩 Trap — restating "immune" without reasoning

For a resistance question, never just restate that the organism is "immune" — the reasoning must go through the changed channel-protein shape that prevents the drug binding, not simply assert resistance as a fact.

Section 5 · spec content point

⚡ Saltatory vs continuous conduction

Myelin doesn't speed up conduction directly — it insulates, which changes where along the axon the impulse actually has to be regenerated.

📖

What the specification says: "The passage of an action potential along non-myelinated and myelinated axons, resulting in nerve impulses."

1
2
3

On an insulated wire, the impulse can only "touch down" at the bare patches, so it effectively leapfrogs from gap to gap — quick. On a bare wire with no coating at all, the impulse has to restart at every single point along its whole length, one after another — much slower, like walking every step of a path instead of jumping between stepping stones.

In a myelinated axon, the myelin sheath electrically insulates the membrane, so depolarisation can only happen at the nodes of Ranvier — the impulse appears to "jump" from node to node, which is called saltatory conduction. In a non-myelinated axon there's no insulation, so depolarisation has to occur along the whole length of the membrane, one adjacent point at a time — much slower, because far more of the membrane has to be depolarised in sequence.

  • In a myelinated axon, the myelin sheath gives electrical insulation, so depolarisation can only occur at the nodes of Ranvier — the action potential appears to "jump" from node to node (saltatory conduction).
  • In a non-myelinated axon, depolarisation occurs along the whole length of the membrane, at every adjacent point in turn — much slower.
  • Myelin does not speed up conduction directly — it insulates, so depolarisation is confined to the nodes and the impulse appears to jump between them, which is faster than regenerating the action potential at every point along an unmyelinated axon.
The myelin sheath electrically insulates the axon, so ion movement — and therefore depolarisation — can only occur at the nodes of Ranvier; the impulse appears to jump between them. In a non-myelinated axon, every point along the membrane must depolarise in turn.

Applying it: a demyelinating disease

A common application context (e.g. Guillain–Barré syndrome) asks for the effect of a damaged or absent myelin sheath:

  • With the myelin sheath damaged or absent, saltatory conduction is reduced or lost.
  • Depolarisation spreads over a greater area of exposed axon membrane instead of jumping cleanly between nodes.
  • Conduction is slowed, and impulses may fail to reach the neuromuscular junction/target cell at all.

This application format is the priority — stating the consequence of losing saltatory conduction, not just defining what saltatory conduction is.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "messages"/"signals", and oversimplifying demyelination

The "messages"/"signals" rule is applied particularly strictly on this point — always write "action potential" or "impulse", never "messages" or "signals". Separately, a demyelinated axon does not simply "conduct slower" everywhere evenly — depolarisation spreads over a greater area of exposed membrane instead of jumping cleanly node to node, and the impulse may fail to reach the target cell entirely, not merely arrive late.

Section 6 · spec content point

⏱️ The refractory period

A brief, enforced pause after every action potential — not a flaw in the system, but the reason impulses stay separate and only ever travel one way.

📖

What the specification says: "The nature and importance of the refractory period in producing discrete impulses and in limiting the frequency of impulse transmission."

1
2
3

Right after a firework goes off, it needs a moment to reload before it can fire again — no matter how hard you try to set it off early, nothing happens. That's the refractory period. It's actually useful: it stops the "flame" from accidentally jumping backwards along the fuse, and it stops fireworks going off in one continuous blur — it keeps each bang separate and limits how fast they can go off in a row.

Straight after an action potential fires, that stretch of membrane has an absolute refractory period, where the sodium channels are locked shut and simply cannot reopen, however strong a new stimulus is. As the membrane then hyperpolarises, there's a relative refractory period, where a new action potential is still possible, but only from an unusually strong stimulus, because the membrane is further from threshold than normal. Together these do two jobs: they force the impulse to travel in one direction only, and they set a hard limit on how close together, and how frequent, impulses can be.

Nature of the refractory period

  • Absolute refractory period: immediately after an action potential, voltage-gated sodium channels are closed/inactivated and cannot reopen, whatever the stimulus strength.
  • Relative refractory period: during the later hyperpolarisation/undershoot, the membrane is further from threshold, so only an unusually strong stimulus can trigger a new action potential.
  • Throughout, the membrane cannot be re-depolarised normally.

Its importance — two separate functions

  1. Ensures impulses travel in one direction only (unidirectional) — the section of membrane that has just fired cannot immediately re-fire, so the impulse cannot travel backwards into it.
  2. Produces discrete (separate, non-merging) impulses and limits the maximum frequency of impulse transmission, by fixing a minimum time between successive action potentials.
▶ 🚩 Trap — stating only one function

Both functions need to be stated for full credit — "prevents too many impulses" alone is not the same as separately stating discreteness and unidirectionality as two distinct points.

Calculating a maximum impulse frequency

📐

Method: maximum frequency (impulses s⁻¹, i.e. Hz) = 1 ÷ refractory period (in seconds).

✅

Worked example: a neurone has a refractory period of 2 ms. Convert to seconds: 2 ÷ 1000 = 0.002 s. Maximum frequency = 1 ÷ 0.002 = 500 impulses per second (500 Hz).

▶ 🚩 Trap — unit conversion and rounding

The recurring traps in this calculation are the ms → s unit conversion (dividing by 1000 incorrectly, or forgetting it altogether) and standard-form rounding — give a final answer to an appropriate number of significant figures, not an unrounded calculator display. Also worth remembering: once the maximum frequency is reached or exceeded, there's no further increase in the information a neurone can transmit — every stronger stimulus beyond that point looks the same to the next cell in the pathway.

Section 7 · spec content point

🌡️ Factors affecting the speed of conductance

Three separate factors, each with its own mechanism — describing only the effect ("faster" or "slower") without the reason behind it leaves marks on the table.

📖

What the specification says: "Factors affecting the speed of conductance: myelination and saltatory conduction; axon diameter; temperature."

1
2
3

Three things make a nerve "wire" carry its signal faster: wrapping it in insulation (so it can jump between bare patches instead of firing all the way along), making the wire fatter (less charge leaks out of a fat wire), and warming it up a bit (warm parts jiggle and react faster) — but only up to a point, because too much heat wrecks the proteins doing the work.

Myelination speeds things up because it forces the impulse to jump between nodes of Ranvier instead of firing along the whole membrane — saltatory conduction. A wider axon is faster too, because it has proportionally less surface area for its volume, so less ion leakage and less resistance to ions flowing along inside it. Warmer temperatures speed things up because ions and channel proteins have more kinetic energy, meaning faster diffusion and faster channel opening — but only up to about 40°C, above which the channel and pump proteins start to denature and speed drops sharply.

FactorMechanism
MyelinationSaltatory conduction — electrical insulation confines depolarisation to the nodes of Ranvier, so the impulse jumps rather than being regenerated at every point.
Axon diameterA wider axon has a smaller surface area to volume ratio, so proportionally less ion leakage out across the membrane, and less resistance to the flow of ions along the inside of the axon — both make conduction faster.
TemperatureHigher temperature gives ions/channel proteins more kinetic energy, so faster diffusion and faster channel gating (and more ATP available for the pump) — up to about 40°C, above which channel and pump proteins denature and speed falls sharply.

Evaluating experimental or disease data on myelin

A common application here presents data comparing a demyelinated/disease group with a healthy control group, and asks whether the difference is meaningful:

  • Judge whether a difference between groups is significant using error bars — overlapping bars suggest the difference may not be significant; non-overlapping bars suggest it may be.
  • Identify possible confounding variables or a small sample size that limits the conclusion that can be drawn — the condition could involve other factors besides reduced myelin, or the difference between forms of the condition could itself be large.

Writing a statistically rigorous conclusion

✅

Correct form: "the probability of this difference occurring by chance is less than 5%, so the difference is significant." Both the reference to probability and the specific claim that the difference (not just "the results") is significant are needed.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "the results are significant"

"The results are significant" alone, with no reference to probability/chance, is not accepted as a full statistical conclusion — and it's the difference between groups that is significant, not "the results" as a whole.

This spec point is testable at high difficulty less because the biology is conceptually hard, and more because so much of it rides on exact terminology. These are the slips that come up again and again.

▶ Where this point sits in difficulty terms

This is entirely second-year-level content: a labelled structure, a three-part resting-potential mechanism, two channel-based sequences for depolarisation/repolarisation, saltatory vs continuous conduction, a two-function refractory period, and three speed factors — a genuinely high memorisation load. On top of that, unfamiliar drug/toxin and demyelinating-disease application questions both require reasoning the mechanism onto new information rather than reciting it, and terminology precision (the compulsory "ions" wording, "messages/signals" vs "action potential/impulse", "electrical" vs "thermal" insulation) is checked closely throughout. Depolarisation and repolarisation tend to be described well on their own, but many answers under-detail how the resting potential is established and maintained, and fewer answers than expected explain clearly why saltatory conduction is actually faster, with explicit reference to the nodes of Ranvier.

Section 9 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

This content works well as a supporting paragraph inside a longer essay-style answer on more than one theme — the trick is closing each paragraph with a sentence that ties the mechanism back to whatever the title is actually about, rather than repeating a generic summary.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around "movement in living organisms"

The resting potential is established by differential membrane permeability together with active transport of Na⁺ out and K⁺ in by the sodium–potassium pump. Depolarisation follows when voltage-gated sodium channels open and Na⁺ diffuses in — ion movement through channel proteins, never pumps — making the inside of the axon less negative; repolarisation then follows as potassium channels open and K⁺ diffuses out. In a myelinated axon, saltatory conduction means depolarisation only occurs at the nodes of Ranvier, so the action potential appears to "jump" and travels faster as a result. Link to title: the impulse itself is fundamentally a wave of ion movement along the axon, and it is this movement that ultimately triggers muscle contraction — connecting molecular-scale movement directly to whole-organism movement.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around "diffusion in organisms"

The nerve impulse itself is generated by the diffusion of ions through channel proteins — sodium ions diffusing in during depolarisation, potassium ions diffusing out during repolarisation, both down their electrochemical gradients and both entirely independent of active transport. This shows diffusion operating even in fast electrical signalling, not just in slower processes like gas exchange: the speed of an axon's response depends on how quickly ions can diffuse through open channels, and factors like temperature and myelination change that speed by changing how, and where, that diffusion happens.

Whichever essay theme this content is used for, treat it as the "mechanism" paragraph: state the process precisely — naming the specific channels, ions and structures involved — then add one sentence linking it explicitly back to that theme, in the essay's own vocabulary.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Neurone structure

  • Know these by name + function: cell body, dendrites, axon, Schwann cells, myelin sheath, nodes of Ranvier.
  • Myelin sheath = electrical insulation, never thermal.
  • Nodes of Ranvier = the only place voltage-gated Na⁺ channels are concentrated / depolarisation can occur in a myelinated axon.

2 · The resting potential

  • K⁺ higher inside, Na⁺ higher outside — concentration gradients.
  • Membrane more permeable to K⁺ than Na⁺ (leak channels) → K⁺ diffuses out faster than Na⁺ in.
  • Na⁺/K⁺ pump: 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in, using ATP, against gradients.
  • Result: ≈ −70 mV, inside negative. Must say "ions" explicitly, or capped at 1 mark.

3 · Depolarisation & the action potential

  • Threshold ≈ −55 mV → voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open → Na⁺ in (facilitated diffusion) → peak ≈ +40 mV.
  • Na⁺ channels close, K⁺ channels open → K⁺ out → repolarisation.
  • K⁺ channels slow to close → hyperpolarisation (undershoot) → resting restored.
  • Never the pump for either step — channels only.

4 · All-or-nothing principle

  • Below threshold: no action potential at all. At/above threshold: always identical size.
  • Intensity = coded by frequency of impulses and/or number of neurones firing.
  • Drug locks Na⁺ channels open → can't repolarise → no further AP. Resistance = changed channel-protein shape, drug can't bind.

5 · Saltatory vs continuous conduction

  • Myelinated: depolarisation only at nodes of Ranvier → impulse "jumps" (saltatory conduction) → faster.
  • Non-myelinated: depolarises along whole length, point by point → slower.
  • Demyelination → saltatory conduction reduced/lost → depolarisation spreads over more membrane → slowed, may fail to arrive.
  • Always "impulse"/"action potential" — never "message"/"signal".

6 · The refractory period

  • Absolute: Na⁺ channels inactivated, can't reopen whatever the stimulus.
  • Relative: during hyperpolarisation, only a strong stimulus can trigger a new AP.
  • Two functions: unidirectional travel + discrete, frequency-limited impulses — state both.
  • Max frequency (Hz) = 1 ÷ refractory period (s). Convert ms→s first; round sensibly.

7 · Factors affecting speed

  • Myelination → saltatory conduction (see 5).
  • Wider axon → smaller SA:V ratio → less ion leakage, less resistance → faster.
  • Higher temperature → more kinetic energy → faster diffusion/gating — up to ≈40°C, then proteins denature and speed falls.
  • Stats conclusion: "probability of this difference by chance is <5%, so the difference is significant" — not just "the results are significant."

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Neurone structure

❓ Label the parts of a myelinated motor neurone shown in an unfamiliar diagram, giving the function of each structure you name.
Cell body — contains the nucleus and organelles, synthesises proteins and keeps the cell alive. Dendrites — carry impulses towards the cell body. Axon — carries the action potential away from the cell body towards the effector. Schwann cells — wrap around the axon in segments, forming the myelin sheath. Myelin sheath — electrically insulates the axon membrane. Nodes of Ranvier — gaps where the axon membrane is exposed and voltage-gated Na⁺ channels are concentrated; the only place depolarisation can occur in a myelinated axon.
❓ A student describes the myelin sheath as providing "thermal insulation, keeping the axon warm." Correct this statement.
The myelin sheath provides electrical, not thermal, insulation — it prevents ion movement across the axon membrane except at the nodes of Ranvier; it has no role in regulating the axon's temperature.

The resting potential

❓ Explain how a resting potential of about −70 mV is established and maintained in a neurone.
Potassium ion concentration is higher inside the axon and sodium ion concentration is higher outside it; the membrane is more permeable to potassium ions than to sodium ions, via potassium leak channels, so potassium ions diffuse out faster than sodium ions diffuse in; the sodium–potassium pump actively transports 3 sodium ions out for every 2 potassium ions in, using ATP, against their concentration gradients. Together, the permeability difference and the pump leave the inside of the axon negative relative to the outside.
❓ A student writes: "the sodium–potassium pump maintains the resting potential." Explain why this answer alone would not gain full credit.
It fails to reference the differential permeability of the membrane to sodium and potassium ions (via leak channels) as a separate mechanism, and merges the pump and the permeability difference into one vague statement rather than crediting both as distinct points; it should also state explicitly that 3 sodium ions are pumped out for every 2 potassium ions in.
❓ Give the direction of net ion movement for both sodium and potassium ions as a neurone's resting potential is being established.
Potassium ions move out of the axon (down their concentration gradient, via leak channels, faster than sodium moves in); sodium ions move out of the axon against their concentration gradient by active transport via the sodium–potassium pump — leaving a higher concentration of sodium ions outside and potassium ions inside at rest.

Depolarisation & the action potential

❓ Explain what happens at the axon membrane during depolarisation and repolarisation.
Depolarisation: a stimulus reaching threshold (≈−55 mV) causes voltage-gated sodium channels to open; sodium ions diffuse in down their electrochemical gradient by facilitated diffusion, making the membrane less negative then positive, with more channels opening as a result (positive feedback) up to about +40 mV. Repolarisation: at the peak, voltage-gated sodium channels close and voltage-gated potassium channels open; potassium ions diffuse out down their electrochemical gradient, bringing the membrane potential back towards resting.
❓ Explain why hyperpolarisation occurs after an action potential.
Voltage-gated potassium channels are slightly slow to close, so slightly too much potassium diffuses out of the axon before they shut; this takes the membrane potential briefly below the normal resting potential (an undershoot) before the sodium–potassium pump and potassium leak channels restore it.
❓ A student writes: "the sodium–potassium pump causes depolarisation." Explain why this is incorrect.
Depolarisation is caused by voltage-gated sodium channels opening and sodium ions diffusing in by facilitated diffusion — a passive process through channel proteins; the sodium–potassium pump uses active transport and plays no part in causing depolarisation or repolarisation, only in restoring the resting potential afterwards.

All-or-nothing principle

❓ State the all-or-nothing principle and explain how the nervous system codes for stimulus intensity.
A stimulus below threshold produces no action potential at all; a stimulus at or above threshold always produces an action potential of the same size and shape. Stimulus intensity is coded by the frequency of action potentials produced and/or the number of neurones firing, not by the size of any individual action potential.
❓ A newly discovered toxin is found to keep voltage-gated sodium channels permanently open once triggered. Predict and explain the effect on the axon.
Sodium ions continue to diffuse into the axon, so the membrane cannot repolarise back towards its resting potential; because the sodium channels cannot reset from their inactivated state without repolarisation, no further action potential can be generated in that region of the axon.
❓ A second population of the same organism is found to be resistant to this toxin. Suggest an explanation.
A mutation has changed the tertiary structure/shape of the sodium ion channel protein, so it is no longer complementary to the toxin; the toxin can no longer bind to the channel, so it cannot hold it open.

Saltatory vs continuous conduction

❓ Contrast the passage of an action potential along a myelinated axon with a non-myelinated axon.
In a myelinated axon, the myelin sheath electrically insulates the membrane, so depolarisation can only occur at the nodes of Ranvier; the action potential appears to jump from node to node — saltatory conduction. In a non-myelinated axon there is no insulation, so depolarisation occurs along the whole length of the membrane, at every adjacent point in turn; this is slower because far more of the membrane must be depolarised in sequence.
❓ A patient with a demyelinating disease is found to have slower nerve conduction than normal. Explain why.
With the myelin sheath damaged, saltatory conduction is reduced or lost; depolarisation spreads over a greater area of exposed axon membrane instead of jumping cleanly between nodes; conduction is slowed, and impulses may fail to reach the target cell (e.g. the neuromuscular junction) at all.

The refractory period

❓ State the nature of the refractory period and explain its importance.
Immediately after an action potential, voltage-gated sodium channels are closed/inactivated (absolute refractory period) and cannot reopen whatever the stimulus strength; during the later hyperpolarisation, only an unusually strong stimulus can trigger a new action potential (relative refractory period). It ensures impulses travel in one direction only, since the region that has just fired cannot immediately re-fire, and it produces discrete impulses while limiting the maximum frequency of transmission.
❓ A neurone has a refractory period of 4 ms. Calculate its maximum impulse frequency, giving appropriate units.
4 ms = 4 ÷ 1000 = 0.004 s. Maximum frequency = 1 ÷ 0.004 = 250 impulses per second (250 Hz).

Factors affecting speed

❓ Explain why a wider axon conducts impulses faster than a narrower one.
A wider axon has a smaller surface area to volume ratio, so proportionally less ion leakage occurs across the membrane and there is less resistance to the flow of ions along the inside of the axon; both of these allow depolarisation to spread, and threshold to be reached, more quickly along the axon.
❓ Explain why conduction speed increases with temperature up to about 40°C, but then falls sharply.
Higher temperature gives ions and channel proteins more kinetic energy, so ions diffuse faster and channels open and close faster, and more ATP is available for the sodium–potassium pump; above about 40°C, channel and pump proteins begin to denature, so conduction speed falls sharply.
❓ Data comparing a demyelinated group with a healthy control group shows non-overlapping error bars. Write a fully rigorous conclusion.
The probability of this difference between the demyelinated and control groups occurring by chance is less than 5%, so the difference is statistically significant; however, the possibility of confounding variables or a small sample size should be considered before drawing a firm conclusion.
