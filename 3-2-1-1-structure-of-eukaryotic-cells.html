Structure of Eukaryotic Cells

The ten named organelles every eukaryotic-cell question is built from — what each one actually looks like, the precise A-level function wording AQA credits (not the GCSE version), how a protein's whole journey through the cell links them together, and how the same organelles explain why specialised cells look the way they do.

⚠️

The single rule that costs the most marks on this whole spec point: AQA almost never credits an organelle name on its own. "Mitochondria produce energy" scores nothing, on any paper — the credited version is mitochondria are the site of aerobic respiration / ATP synthesis, via oxidative phosphorylation. Never reach for "powerhouse of the cell" — examiners name that exact phrase as one that earns zero marks, every time.

Section 1 · spec content point

🔬 Meet the ten organelles — the animal and plant cell map

There are exactly ten named structures on this spec point, no more — if it isn't on this list, AQA won't ask you to name its function here. Learn what each one looks like first; Section 2 then adds the precise function wording that actually earns the marks.

📖

What the specification says: "The structure of eukaryotic cells, restricted to the structure and function of: cell-surface membrane; nucleus (containing chromosomes, consisting of protein-bound, linear DNA, and one or more nucleoli); mitochondria; chloroplasts (in plants and algae); Golgi apparatus and Golgi vesicles; lysosomes (a membrane-bound organelle that releases hydrolytic enzymes); ribosomes; rough endoplasmic reticulum and smooth endoplasmic reticulum; cell wall (in plants, algae and fungi); cell vacuole (in plants)."

1
2
3

Think of a cell like a tiny walled city, and every organelle is a building with one job. There's a control centre in the middle that keeps the master plans (the nucleus). There are power stations dotted around making energy (mitochondria) — plant cities also have solar panels that catch sunlight (chloroplasts). There's a packaging depot that wraps up parcels before they leave (Golgi apparatus), tiny factories that build proteins (ribosomes), a recycling bin that breaks down rubbish (lysosome), and a boundary wall around the whole city (cell-surface membrane, plus a stronger outer wall in plants).

  • Control centre = nucleus
  • Power stations = mitochondria
  • Solar panels (plants only) = chloroplasts
  • Packaging depot = Golgi apparatus
  • Recycling bin = lysosome
  • Tiny factories = ribosomes

Every eukaryotic cell — animal or plant — is built from the same basic organelle set. The nucleus is the largest organelle, with a double envelope full of pores, holding the chromosomes and one or more nucleoli. Mitochondria have a folded inner membrane and a fluid centre. Ribosomes are tiny and either float free in the cytoplasm or sit on the rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER), which is a stack of membrane sheets continuous with the nucleus. The smooth ER (SER) looks similar but has no ribosomes attached. The Golgi apparatus is a curved stack of flattened sacs that buds off small vesicles, some of which become lysosomes. The cell-surface membrane wraps every cell. Plant (and algal) cells add three extra structures: chloroplasts, a rigid cell wall outside the membrane, and a large, permanent cell vacuole that usually takes up most of the cell's volume.

StructureFound in
Cell-surface membrane, nucleus, mitochondria, Golgi apparatus & vesicles, lysosomes, ribosomes, rough & smooth ERBoth animal and plant cells
Chloroplasts, cell wall, cell vacuolePlant / algal (cell wall also in fungi) only

Every generalised animal cell and every generalised plant cell shares the same organelle set and the same jobs — only three structures are restricted to plants (and, where stated, algae or fungi): the cell wall, chloroplasts, and the large permanent cell vacuole. In a mature plant cell the vacuole is drawn as the single largest compartment because that is structurally accurate — it typically occupies the greatest share of cell volume, pushing every other organelle out into a thin peripheral layer of cytoplasm.

A simplified generalised animal cell. Note the rough ER's membrane is continuous with the nuclear envelope — that continuity is exactly what makes the secretory pathway in Section 2 possible. Chloroplasts, the cell wall and the cell vacuole are added only in plant/algal cells.
📌

What you'll see drawn but never asked about: micrographs and textbook diagrams often show centrioles near the nucleus — they're genuinely part of a real animal cell, but they sit beyond this spec point and aren't separately assessed here. Recognise them; don't spend revision time on their function.

Naming organelles precisely — the traps

Identifying a named organelle from a micrograph or a one-line clue is a common question type, and mark schemes are strict on precision — a near-miss term that "sounds right" is refused even when the intended organelle is obvious.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "lysozyme" for lysosome

A lysozyme is an enzyme; a lysosome is the organelle. Where the question wants the organelle named, "lysozyme" is rejected.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "nuclear membrane" for cell-surface membrane

Rejected wherever cell-surface membrane is the required answer — the nucleus has its own double envelope, a completely different structure from the boundary of the whole cell.

▶ 🚩 Trap — explaining why a structure looks "missing" from an image

If a nucleus (or other organelle) appears to be missing from a micrograph, the credited explanations are structural or preparation-based: the image is a section, so the nucleus lies in a different plane and simply isn't included in that slice; or the sample wasn't stained for that structure. A resolution-based answer — "the microscope couldn't resolve it" / "it's too small to see" — is not credited here. Nuclei are easily within light-microscope resolution, so this reasoning is biologically wrong for this scenario, not just imprecise.

Section 2 · spec content point

⚙️ Structure and function — the credited wording & the secretory pathway

Every question on this spec point ultimately tests the shape and the exact function wording of these ten structures. Learn the function sentence for each organelle word-for-word — it's what actually earns the mark, not just naming the structure.

📖

What the specification says: "The structure of eukaryotic cells, restricted to the structure and function of: cell-surface membrane; nucleus (containing chromosomes, consisting of protein-bound, linear DNA, and one or more nucleoli); mitochondria; chloroplasts (in plants and algae); Golgi apparatus and Golgi vesicles; lysosomes (a membrane-bound organelle that releases hydrolytic enzymes); ribosomes; rough endoplasmic reticulum and smooth endoplasmic reticulum; cell wall (in plants, algae and fungi); cell vacuole (in plants)." (Same specification content point as Section 1 — this half covers the precise credited function wording and the secretory pathway.)

1
2
3

Follow one letter (a protein) through the factory. The plans come from the control centre (nucleus). A tiny machine (ribosome) builds the letter. It gets folded and put in an envelope (vesicle). A power station (mitochondrion) pays for the postage. A packaging depot (Golgi apparatus) wraps it up nicely. Then it gets posted out of the building (exocytosis, through the cell-surface membrane). Never say a power station "makes energy" — say it powers a specific job, like posting the letter.

Each organelle has one precise job, and the exam wants that job stated properly, not a rough GCSE version. The nucleus contains genetic information that codes for proteins; the nucleolus makes ribosomal RNA and assembles ribosomes. Mitochondria are the site of aerobic respiration — never say they "produce energy." Chloroplasts are the site of photosynthesis. Ribosomes make proteins (translation). The rough ER folds and processes proteins made by its ribosomes; the smooth ER makes lipids and steroids. The Golgi apparatus modifies and packages proteins and forms lysosomes, which then digest waste. Trace a secreted protein and every one of these jobs links together in order: nucleus → ribosomes on the rough ER → vesicle → (mitochondria supply the ATP) → Golgi apparatus → Golgi vesicle → released from the cell.

  1. DNA in the nucleus is transcribed / codes for the protein
  2. Ribosomes on the rough ER translate the mRNA, building the protein
  3. The protein is folded and moves into a vesicle
  4. Mitochondria supply the ATP this active transport needs
  5. The vesicle fuses with the Golgi apparatus, which modifies and packages the protein
  6. A Golgi vesicle carries the finished protein to the cell-surface membrane for release (exocytosis)

▶ 🚩 The costliest single wording error on this spec point

"Powerhouse of the cell", "storage of ATP", "production of energy" and "energy for respiration" are all rejected for the mitochondrion — write site of aerobic respiration / ATP synthesis (via oxidative phosphorylation) every time. The same GCSE-level trap catches the nucleus too: "holds genetic material to control cell activities" scores nothing at A-level — the credited version is contains genetic information that codes for polypeptides/proteins.

The secretory pathway — tracing a protein through the cell

This is the single best way AQA tests this spec point at length: trace the full route of a secreted protein through the cell, up to six marks, one mark per correctly named organelle + role, in the correct order.

Click a numbered dot, or use Next / Back above, to step through the six-mark secretory pathway — every organelle must appear in the correct order, each doing its own named job, never a generic "processed by the cell."
📎

Marking rule: this is a level-of-detail question — marks are awarded per correct organelle + role named in the correct sequence, up to the mark cap. Missing an organelle out of the chain (most often mitochondria/ATP) is the single most common way this question loses marks.

The reasoning AQA reuses everywhere

⭐

The three-step chain: AQA rarely credits naming an organelle in isolation; it wants a structure → function → consequence chain — e.g. "cells with a high metabolic demand contain many mitochondria → more ATP is made by aerobic respiration → enough energy is available for active transport / protein synthesis / muscle contraction." This exact pattern recurs with different organelles right through transport, immunity and beyond — practise it here, and it transfers directly. Secretory cells are recognised by an extensive RER and a large Golgi apparatus; absorptive cells by a high mitochondrial density.

Section 3 · spec content point

🧩 Specialisation — tissues, organs, systems & adapted cells

The same ten organelles explain why specialised cells are built the way they are. This section pairs the word-perfect definitions of tissue, organ and system with the fixed organelle-to-adaptation links AQA reuses every year.

📖

What the specification says: "In complex multicellular organisms, eukaryotic cells become specialised for specific functions. Specialised cells are organised into tissues, tissues into organs and organs into systems."

1
2
3

Cells are like workers who each learn one job really well instead of doing everything. A group of workers doing the same job together is a tissue — like a whole team of bricklayers. Different teams working together to finish one project make an organ — like all the different trades building one house. Several finished houses working together make a system — like a whole street of houses forming a neighbourhood. And a worker who's really good at one job usually has extra tools for it — e.g. a cell whose job is "send lots of energy-hungry signals" is built with extra power stations (mitochondria).

In complex organisms, cells specialise for one particular function rather than doing everything a cell could theoretically do. Similar specialised cells group together as a tissue; different tissues working together form an organ; and organs working together form a system. Whenever a cell needs to do one job especially well, it's usually built with an unusually large number of one particular organelle — a cell that does a lot of active transport has many mitochondria for ATP; a cell that absorbs a lot has extra folding (microvilli) to increase its surface area; a cell that makes a lot of protein has many ribosomes; and a cell that secretes a lot has an extra-large rough ER and Golgi apparatus.

Define tissue, organ and system precisely

TermMark-scheme wording — learn this exactly
TissueA group of similar/identical cells (or cells with a common origin) working together to perform a specific function
OrganA group of different tissues working together to perform a specific function
SystemA group of organs working together to perform a specific function
▶ 🚩 Trap — defining "tissue" by function alone

Defining tissue as "cells that do the same job" is not credited. The mark scheme requires the cells to be similar/identical, or of common origin, and working together — function alone omits the structural half of the definition.

Explain how a named cell is adapted for its function

Each standard adaptation has one fixed organelle-based explanation — learn these as pairs, and remember to finish every sentence at the consequence, not the organelle name.

Adaptation neededOrganelle-based explanation
Rapid active transportMany mitochondria → more ATP by aerobic respiration
Rapid absorption / exchangeMicrovilli → increased cell-surface membrane surface area
High rate of protein synthesisMany (free) ribosomes → more polypeptides made
Enzyme / hormone secretionExtensive RER + large Golgi apparatus + many vesicles → proteins folded, packaged and exported
▶ 🚩 Trap — reusing the same organelle twice in one answer

Reusing the same organelle for two different parts of a multi-part question scores only once. Where a question asks for two or more adaptations, use a different feature for each answer.

🔁

The reused reasoning chain, restated for specialised cells: the "many X → function → consequence" chain met in Section 2 is the single most reused reasoning pattern on the whole specification — it reappears, structurally unchanged, in explanations of transport, immunity, gas exchange and muscle contraction. Practise it here with organelles, and it transfers directly.

Section 4 · exam technique

🎯 Common mistakes & precise wording

This spec point is testable at surprisingly high difficulty not because the biology is conceptually hard — the organelle list is straightforward recall — but because so much of it rides on exact terminology. These are the slips that come up again and again.

Vague / GCSE-level versionWhat to say instead
"Mitochondria produce/make energy"Mitochondria are the site of aerobic respiration / ATP synthesis, via oxidative phosphorylation
"Nucleus holds genetic material to control cell activities"Contains genetic information that codes for polypeptides/proteins
"Lysozyme" for the organelleLysosome — a lysozyme is the enzyme it releases, not the organelle itself
"Nuclear membrane" for the cell boundaryCell-surface membrane — the nucleus has its own separate double envelope
"The microscope couldn't resolve it" (missing nucleus)The image is a section (nucleus in a different plane) or the sample wasn't stained
"Cells that do the same job" (defining tissue)Similar/identical cells, or cells of common origin, working together
Naming one organelle for two separate adaptation marksUse a different organelle/feature for each adaptation asked for

▶ The hardest application skill on this spec point

The hardest single skill here is not naming organelles — it's building the full three-step reasoning chain instead of stopping at the organelle name, and doing this differently for each part of a multi-part question. Long, detailed answers frequently still miss full marks by describing only one function when several were required, or several functions when the question asked for one specific one — read the command word and mark allocation before deciding how many points to make.

A genuinely paired comparison of mitochondria and chloroplasts is a recurring synoptic favourite: both are double membrane-bound, both contain their own circular DNA and 70S ribosomes, but cristae (mitochondria) are structurally and functionally distinct from thylakoids/grana (chloroplasts). A one-sided answer that only describes one organelle does not score the "difference" or "similarity" mark.

▶ Command words used on this spec point

Identify — name the exact technical term; near-miss terms (lysozyme, nuclear membrane) are refused. Describe — give the structure (e.g. "double membrane, inner folded into cristae") without yet explaining function. Explain — the dominant command; wants structure and function, and in application questions, the consequence too — naming alone scores zero. Suggest — apply the structure→function→consequence chain to an unfamiliar specialised cell. Compare — needs a genuinely paired statement covering both organelles, not one described in isolation.

Section 5 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

3.2.1.1 rarely sets an essay title on its own, but its organelle vocabulary is the toolkit almost every later essay draws on — the trick is closing each paragraph with a sentence that ties the mechanism back to whatever the title is actually about.

Typical essay titles this content supports: "The importance of organelles in cell function," "How the structure of cells is related to their function," and "Communication within and between cells" (organelle roles in secretion). The secretory pathway (RER → Golgi → vesicle → exocytosis) reappears whenever an essay discusses hormone or enzyme secretion; mitochondrial density reappears in essays on active transport, muscle contraction and energy budgets.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around secretion

Cells specialised for secretion, such as pancreatic acinar cells, contain an extensive rough endoplasmic reticulum studded with ribosomes, so large quantities of enzyme protein can be synthesised and folded. Many mitochondria supply the ATP this active synthesis and transport requires. A large Golgi apparatus then modifies and packages the protein into vesicles, which fuse with the cell-surface membrane and release their contents by exocytosis. Each organelle's abundance is therefore a direct structural adaptation to the cell's specific function.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around structure→function precision

The mitochondrion illustrates how precisely structure is matched to function: its inner membrane is folded into cristae, greatly increasing the surface area available for the enzymes of oxidative phosphorylation, so that ATP can be synthesised at a rate proportional to the cell's metabolic demand. This is a clear example of how surface area, not just the presence of an organelle, determines how much of a process a cell can actually carry out.

Whichever essay theme this content is used for, treat it as the "toolkit" paragraph: name the specific organelles involved, state their precise function, and finish every sentence at the consequence — a 25-mark essay is, at its core, dozens of these structure→function→consequence chains linked together.

Section 6

🧷 Practical links, memory hook & final checklist

Why this page is the hidden foundation of two required practicals

  • No numbered required practical is tied to 3.2.1.1 directly — but this page supplies the organelle-identification vocabulary that two later required practicals depend on.
  • The root-tip squash practical asks you to identify cells at different stages of mitosis under a light microscope — but before mitotic stages can be scored, you must already be able to recognise the nucleus and distinguish cell-surface membrane from cell wall in a plant root-tip cell, both taught here.
  • Cell fractionation and ultracentrifugation (isolating organelles for study) relies on knowing the relative density and size ranking of organelles: nuclei sediment first (largest/densest), then mitochondria and chloroplasts, then ER/Golgi fragments, with ribosomes sedimenting last — which only makes sense once the structures and their relative sizes are secure.

Where 3.2.1.1 reappears across the rest of the specification

  • All cells arise from other cells — mitosis in a nucleus already understood from here.
  • Transport across cell membranes — the cell-surface membrane's structure, developed in far more detail.
  • Cell recognition and the immune system — organelle-rich, secretory antibody-producing plasma cells.
  • Gas exchange and transport in plants — mitochondrial density in exchange-surface cells; chloroplast structure in leaf mesophyll.
  • Photosynthesis and respiration — chloroplast and mitochondrion structure directly underpinning both pathways.
  • Muscles — mitochondrial density in different muscle fibre types; practical-skills questions on cell fractionation across the whole paper.

Rule of thumb: any later question that needs a cell to move something, make something, or supply energy is, underneath, testing whether you can still name the right organelle from this page and finish the sentence at its consequence.

🧠

Memory hook. Ten structures, one sentence pattern each: name it → describe it → state its precise function → (in application) state the consequence. If a sentence about an organelle could be finished with "...and that's why the cell can survive" without saying anything more specific, it isn't finished — go back and name the actual biochemical process (aerobic respiration, translation, hydrolysis, photosynthesis) the organelle performs.

Self-check before moving on

  • I can name and describe the structure of all ten spec-listed structures, and state which three are plant/algal-only.
  • I can state the precise A-level function of the nucleus, nucleolus and mitochondrion without lapsing into GCSE wording or "powerhouse of the cell."
  • I can trace the full six-step secretory pathway for a protein: nucleus → RER/ribosomes → vesicle → mitochondria (ATP) → Golgi → Golgi vesicle → exocytosis.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Meet the ten organelles

  • Exactly ten named structures — cell-surface membrane, nucleus, mitochondria, chloroplasts, Golgi apparatus & vesicles, lysosomes, ribosomes, rough ER, smooth ER, cell wall, cell vacuole.
  • Only three are plant/algal(/fungal)-only: chloroplasts, cell wall, cell vacuole.
  • "Lysozyme" ≠ lysosome; "nuclear membrane" ≠ cell-surface membrane — both rejected if confused.
  • Missing nucleus in an image → it's a section, or wasn't stained. Never "resolution."

2 · Function & the secretory pathway

  • Mitochondria = site of aerobic respiration / ATP synthesis (oxidative phosphorylation) — never "produce energy" or "powerhouse."
  • Nucleus = contains genetic information that codes for proteins (not "controls cell activities").
  • Secretory pathway: nucleus (DNA transcribed) → RER/ribosomes (translated) → vesicle → mitochondria supply ATP → Golgi (modifies/packages) → Golgi vesicle → exocytosis at membrane.
  • Structure → function → consequence: never stop at the organelle name.

3 · Specialisation & adaptation

  • Tissue = similar/identical cells (or common origin) working together. Organ = tissues working together. System = organs working together.
  • Active transport → many mitochondria. Absorption → microvilli, more surface area. Protein synthesis → many ribosomes. Secretion → extensive RER + large Golgi + vesicles.
  • Never reuse the same organelle for two adaptations in one answer.
🧠

No calcium here — but the same habit applies: never let a "channel/organelle does X" sentence stop before the consequence. Ten structures, one sentence pattern each: name it → describe it → state its precise function → state the consequence.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Meet the ten organelles

❓ A micrograph shows an organelle with a stack of flattened, curved membrane sacs with small vesicles budding from the edges. Identify it, and state one structure visible only in a plant cell.
The organelle is the Golgi apparatus. A structure found only in a plant cell (not this one) would be the cell wall, the chloroplast, or the permanent cell vacuole — the Golgi apparatus itself is present in both animal and plant cells.
❓ A student labels a structure on a micrograph as the "nuclear membrane" where the cell-surface membrane was required. Explain why this is rejected, and give the correct term.
The nuclear membrane (nuclear envelope) is a separate double membrane surrounding the nucleus only, structurally distinct from the boundary of the whole cell; the correct term for the boundary of the whole cell is the cell-surface membrane.
❓ A student cannot see a nucleus in a light micrograph of a cell that should contain one. Suggest two possible explanations, other than resolution.
The image may be a section, so the nucleus lies in a different plane and is simply not included in that slice; or the sample was not stained (or the stain used does not show the nucleus). A resolution-based explanation is not credited, since nuclei are easily within light-microscope resolution.

Function & the secretory pathway

❓ A student writes "mitochondria produce energy for the cell." Rewrite this using precise, full-credit wording.
Mitochondria are the site of aerobic respiration; ATP is synthesised via oxidative phosphorylation on the folded inner membrane (cristae).
❓ Describe the route taken by a protein that is synthesised and then secreted from a cell, from the DNA that codes for it to its release.
DNA in the nucleus is transcribed, coding for the protein; ribosomes on the rough endoplasmic reticulum translate the mRNA, synthesising the polypeptide; the protein is folded and processed in the RER and moves into a vesicle; mitochondria supply the ATP needed for this active transport; the vesicle fuses with the Golgi apparatus, which modifies and packages the protein (e.g. by glycosylation); a Golgi vesicle then carries the finished protein to the cell-surface membrane, where it is released by exocytosis.
❓ Give one similarity and one difference between the rough and smooth endoplasmic reticulum.
Similarity: both are membrane systems of flattened sacs/tubules found in the cytoplasm (or: both are continuous with parts of the cell's internal membrane system). Difference: rough ER has ribosomes attached to its surface and folds/processes proteins, whereas smooth ER has no ribosomes attached and synthesises lipids and steroids instead.

Specialisation & adaptation

❓ Define the terms tissue, organ and system.
Tissue — a group of similar/identical cells, or cells with a common origin, working together to perform a specific function. Organ — a group of different tissues working together to perform a specific function. System — a group of organs working together to perform a specific function.
❓ A cell is described as absorbing nutrients rapidly across its surface. Suggest, and explain, two organelle-based adaptations you would expect this cell to show, using a different feature for each.
Microvilli — infoldings of the cell-surface membrane that increase surface area, allowing more channel/carrier proteins to be present for facilitated diffusion or active transport. Many mitochondria — providing the ATP needed to power active transport of nutrients against a concentration gradient. (Two distinct features are required — reusing the same organelle for both marks would score only once.)
❓ Explain why a cell specialised for enzyme secretion typically contains an unusually large amount of rough endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus.
An extensive rough ER, studded with ribosomes, allows large quantities of enzyme (protein) to be synthesised and folded; a large Golgi apparatus then modifies and packages this protein into vesicles for secretion by exocytosis — the abundance of each organelle is a direct structural adaptation to the cell's high secretory demand.
