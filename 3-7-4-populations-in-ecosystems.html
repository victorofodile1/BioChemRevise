Populations in Ecosystems

How ecologists actually measure and explain population change: the vocabulary that everything else nests inside, why populations level off at a carrying capacity, the full causal loop behind a predator–prey cycle, how quadrats, transects and mark–release–recapture turn a habitat into a number, and the staged process of succession from bare ground to a climax community — plus how conservation deliberately works against that process.

The single habit that protects the most marks on this spec point: never write the word "competition" on its own. Always say which type — intraspecific (same species) or interspecific (different species) — and which resource it's for. And never explain a predator–prey graph as just "there's a lag" — give the full causal loop: prey numbers change → food available to predators changes → predator numbers respond, with a time lag. Both of these are reported as the most commonly lost marks on this entire spec point.

Section 1 · spec content point

🧩 Populations, communities & ecosystems

Four definitions build directly on top of each other, in this order — get the order right and the rest of this spec point falls into place, because every later idea (carrying capacity, succession, conservation) is really just describing something changing within an ecosystem.

📖

What the specification says: "Populations of different species form a community. A community and the non-living components of its environment together form an ecosystem. Ecosystems can range in size from the very small to the very large."

1
2
3

Picture a block of flats. One flat has a family of mice living in it — that's a population: all the individuals of one species, living in the same place. Now picture the whole block: mice, spiders, the odd bird nesting in the roof — every different species' "flat" put together is a community. Add in the building itself — the bricks, the plumbing, the weather blowing through — and now you've got the whole ecosystem: every living thing, plus everything non-living around it, all interacting.

  • Population = one species' flat
  • Community = every species' flat, together
  • Ecosystem = the whole building, living and non-living parts combined

A population is all the organisms of one species living in a habitat at a given time. Where more than one population shares a habitat, together they form a community — every population of every different species living there. A community never exists in isolation from its surroundings: add in the non-living, abiotic parts of the environment — temperature, light, water, soil chemistry — and the community plus those abiotic components together make up an ecosystem. Ecosystems genuinely vary enormously in scale, from a single rotting log to an entire ocean, so "ecosystem" is a term about relationships and interactions, not a fixed size.

TermDefinitionWhy the order matters
PopulationAll the organisms of one species in a habitat, at a given timeThe smallest unit — everything else is built from populations
CommunityAll the populations of different species living together in a habitatSimply every population sharing that habitat, added together
EcosystemA community together with the non-living (abiotic) components of its environment, and the interactions between themLiving + non-living, plus the interactions — this is the level at which carrying capacity, succession and conservation all operate

Two supporting terms are worth defining precisely alongside these, since they get used throughout this spec point: an abiotic factor is a non-living influence on an organism (temperature, light intensity, water availability, pH, mineral ions); a biotic factor is a living influence (competition, predation, disease, food supply). Carrying capacity, covered in Section 3, is set by a mixture of both.

Ecosystems range enormously in size — from a puddle or a rotting log up to an entire rainforest or ocean — so scale is never part of the definition itself; what defines an ecosystem is the set of interactions between its living community and its abiotic surroundings, not how big it happens to be.

▶ Precise phrasing worth reusing

For a one-mark "define" question, the safest wording is close to the specification itself: "a population is all the organisms of one species in a habitat"; "a community is formed by the populations of different species living in that habitat"; "an ecosystem is a community together with the non-living components of its environment." Each definition should stand alone — don't fold one term's wording into another's.

Section 2 · spec content point

🎭 The ecological niche

This is the single most commonly mis-defined term on this spec point — and the mark scheme is unusually strict about the one wrong answer nearly everyone reaches for first.

📖

What the specification says: "Within a habitat, a species occupies a niche governed by adaptation to both abiotic and biotic conditions."

1
2
3

Think of an office building. The building itself — which floor, which room — is like a habitat: it's just the address, the place. But your niche is your actual job: what you do all day, what resources you need to do it, and how you fit in around everyone else's job. Two people can share an office (habitat), but if they're given the exact same job (niche) with the same one set of tools, sooner or later one of them gets pushed out.

  • Habitat = the address — where an organism lives
  • Niche = the job — the organism's role, and what it needs to do it

A niche is the role a species plays within its habitat — what it eats, what eats it, what resources it uses, and the range of abiotic conditions (temperature, light, pH, and so on) it can tolerate. Two different things are being described here: adaptation to biotic conditions (its position in feeding relationships and interactions with other organisms) and adaptation to abiotic conditions (the physical and chemical conditions it's suited to). Because a niche is defined this precisely, it is never correct to describe it as simply "where an organism lives" — that sentence describes the habitat, a different and much simpler idea.

A niche is the role a species occupies within its habitat, governed by its adaptations to both abiotic conditions (the physical/chemical environment it tolerates) and biotic conditions (its interactions with other organisms — what it feeds on, what feeds on it, and what it competes with). A niche includes a species' position within the food web of its community, not simply a location.

HabitatNiche
The place where an organism livesThe role a species plays there — governed by adaptation to abiotic and biotic conditions
A locationA function — feeding relationships, resource use, tolerance range

Because a niche is defined this specifically, two species can never occupy exactly the same niche in the same habitat indefinitely. If they try to, they are forced into direct competition for the same resources — and one out-competes and eliminates the other, at least locally. This is the definitional root of interspecific competition and the competitive exclusion principle, developed fully in Section 3.

▶ 🚩 Trap — the definition that is always rejected

"Where an organism lives" is explicitly rejected as a definition of niche — that sentence defines habitat. A niche has to be described as a role: what the organism does, what it needs, and how it's adapted to both the living and non-living conditions around it. If a question gives marks for defining niche, a location-only answer earns nothing.

Section 3 · spec content point

📈 Carrying capacity & competition

Why a population growing in a new habitat doesn't just keep growing forever — and the single most confused pair of terms on this whole spec point.

📖

What the specification says: "An ecosystem supports a certain size of population of a species, called the carrying capacity. This population size can vary as a result of: the effect of abiotic factors; interactions between organisms: interspecific and intraspecific competition and predation." (Same specification content point as Section 4 — this half covers carrying capacity, abiotic factors and the two competition types; Section 4 covers predation.)

1
2
3

Imagine a small room that fits exactly 20 chairs. People can keep walking in — but once every chair is taken, no one else can sit down until somebody leaves. A population is a bit like that room: it can grow quickly at first, but there's a maximum size the room (the habitat) can support, called the carrying capacity. What fills the "chairs" is a limited resource, like food or space.

Now imagine two different kinds of competing for a chair: two people from the same friend group racing for the last one (that's like intraspecific competition — same species), versus two people from different friend groups both wanting the same chair (that's like interspecific competition — different species).

  • Intraspecific = wIThIN one species
  • Interspecific = betWEEN different species

The carrying capacity (K) is the maximum population size a habitat can sustainably support. As a population grows towards K, individuals of the same species increasingly compete with one another for the same limited resources — food, water, light, space, mates, nesting sites. This is intraspecific competition, and because more individuals means more competition, its strength scales with how crowded the population already is: birth rate falls and/or death rate rises, and the population growth slows and eventually levels off around K.

Interspecific competition is a different thing entirely — competition between different species for the same resource or niche. Rather than regulating a species' own population size, it can reduce the carrying capacity, growth rate, or distribution of one or both competing species, and — because two species can't share an identical niche indefinitely — can end in one species locally eliminating the other.

Abiotic factors (temperature, light, water availability, and so on) act alongside these biotic interactions to set how large K actually is for a given species in a given place.

Carrying capacity and the sigmoid growth curve

The carrying capacity (K) is the maximum population size an ecosystem can sustainably support over a period of time. A population colonising a new habitat typically follows a sigmoid ("S-shaped") growth curve: a slow initial lag phase, an exponential phase while resources are abundant and birth rate exceeds death rate, and finally a stationary phase where birth rate ≈ death rate, as the population is regulated around K.

Slow initial growth (lag), then rapid growth while resources are abundant (exponential, birth rate > death rate), then a plateau as intraspecific competition increases with density and the population is regulated around the carrying capacity, K (stationary, birth rate ≈ death rate).

As the population approaches K, intraspecific competition for a limited resource (food, water, light, space, mineral ions, breeding/nesting sites) increases, so birth rate falls and/or death rate rises — this is density-dependent regulation: the strength of the effect scales directly with how crowded the population already is. Better-adapted individuals are more likely to survive this competition and go on to reproduce — the direct mechanistic link between intraspecific competition and natural selection.

📌

Applying this to unfamiliar data: this idea is recalled well "in principle" but applied poorly to real graphs. Never give a generic "resources become limited" answer — always name the actual limiting resource shown or implied by the data (a named food source, a specific nesting site, water availability).

Intraspecific vs interspecific competition

Point of comparisonIntraspecific competitionInterspecific competition
Species involvedThe same speciesTwo or more different species
What it regulatesThat species' own carrying capacity (K)The carrying capacity, growth rate or distribution of a different, competing species
MechanismBirth rate falls and/or death rate rises as density increasesOne species reduces the resource available to, or the success of, another
Extreme outcomePopulation size stabilises around KCompetitive exclusion — local elimination of the weaker competitor

Interspecific competition is competition between different species for the same limited resource or a shared niche; because two species cannot occupy the same niche indefinitely (Section 2), sustained interspecific competition can result in one species out-competing and eliminating the other, at least locally — the competitive exclusion principle.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "competition" used without qualification

Intraspecific and interspecific competition are reported as commonly confused nationally. Never write "competition" on its own — always state whether it's intraspecific (same species) or interspecific (different species), and always name the specific resource being competed for.

Section 4 · spec content point

🦊 Predator–prey population cycles

The third way a population's size can vary, alongside abiotic factors and competition. Examiners want the full causal loop here, not a description of a graph's shape.

📖

What the specification says: "An ecosystem supports a certain size of population of a species, called the carrying capacity. This population size can vary as a result of: the effect of abiotic factors; interactions between organisms: interspecific and intraspecific competition and predation." (Same specification content point as Section 3 — this half covers predation specifically.)

1
2
3

Imagine rabbits and foxes. When there are lots of rabbits, foxes have plenty to eat, so — a little while later — there are more foxes too. But more foxes means more hunting, so rabbit numbers start to drop. With fewer rabbits, foxes start running out of food, so — again a little while later — fox numbers drop too. With fewer foxes around, rabbits are safer, so their numbers climb back up, and the whole thing starts again.

The fox population always follows the rabbit population, a little behind — never ahead of it, and never moving exactly together with it.

A predator–prey cycle is a repeating, out-of-step ("lagged") oscillation between two population sizes, and the whole point is that it's driven by a causal chain, not just correlation. As prey numbers rise, more food becomes available to predators, so — after a delay, because it takes time for extra food to turn into extra breeding success — the predator population rises too. More predators means more predation pressure, so prey numbers start to fall. With less prey available, food again becomes scarce for predators, so — after another delay — the predator population falls as well. With fewer predators now, prey are under less pressure and their numbers rise again, and the cycle repeats.

As prey numbers rise, there is more food for predators, so the predator population rises too — but only with a time lag, because it takes time for extra food availability to translate into extra breeding success. As predator numbers rise, predation pressure increases, so prey numbers fall. This in turn, again with a lag, means less food is available to predators, so the predator population falls. With predator numbers now low, predation pressure eases and prey numbers rise again — a repeating, lagged oscillation in which the predator population always lags behind the prey population.

One full predator–prey cycle, as a causal chain — never just "there's a lag between the curves."
▶ 🚩 Trap — naming the lag without the causal chain

An answer that only names "a time lag between the curves," without the causal food/predation chain above, is incomplete. Also avoid claiming that predation alone controls prey numbers — food, space and other resources limit prey too, so predation is one regulating factor among several, not the sole cause.

Section 5 · spec content point

📏 Sampling population size

Turning "how many organisms are in this habitat?" into an actual number — three different methods, chosen to fit how the organism moves and how it's counted.

📖

What the specification says: "The size of a population can be estimated using: randomly placed quadrats, or quadrats along a belt transect, for slow-moving or non-motile organisms; the mark-release-recapture method for motile organisms. The assumptions made when using the mark-release-recapture method."

1
2
3

You can't count every single organism in a whole field, so ecologists sample instead. For plants or slow creatures, throw down a small square frame (a quadrat) in lots of random spots, count what's inside, then scale that up to the whole field's size. If you want to see how things change across a field — say, from a shady wood into an open field — line the squares up in a row instead (a transect).

For animals that move around, like voles, you can't use a square frame — so instead you catch some, mark them harmlessly, let them go, then catch a second batch later and see how many of your marked ones turn up again. The fewer marked ones you find, the bigger the population must be.

Random quadrat sampling is used for slow-moving or non-motile organisms (plants, snails). Quadrats are placed at coordinates generated from random numbers, to avoid the person doing the sampling introducing bias. The mean count per quadrat is then scaled up, using the ratio between the quadrat's area and the total habitat area, to estimate the whole population. A belt transect instead places quadrats at fixed, regular intervals along a line — this is deliberately systematic, not random, because the aim is to reveal how distribution changes across an environmental gradient (such as from woodland into open field), alongside a measured abiotic factor.

For motile animals, mark–release–recapture is used instead: catch and mark a sample, release them and allow time for them to mix back into the population, then catch a second sample and count how many are marked. The population size is then calculated from the proportion of marked individuals recaptured — this relies on several assumptions holding true, including that the population doesn't change (no births, deaths, immigration or emigration) between visits.

Random quadrat sampling and the scale-up calculation

A sufficient number of quadrats (typically 20 or more) are placed using coordinates generated from a numbered grid and a random number table or generator, to avoid investigator bias and make the sample representative of the whole habitat. The number of individuals — or, for plants that are hard to count individually, the percentage cover using a gridded quadrat — is recorded in each, and a mean count per quadrat calculated. This mean is then explicitly scaled up: the phrase "scale up" alone is not enough on its own — the actual multiplication must be shown.

Left: random placement avoids investigator bias. Right: systematic placement along a transect deliberately reveals how distribution changes across an environmental gradient.
🧮

Worked example — quadrat scale-up. Mean count = 4 individuals per 0.25 m² quadrat; total habitat area = 100 m². Step 1: number of quadrat-areas in the habitat = 100 ÷ 0.25 = 400. Step 2: estimated total population = 4 × 400 = 1600 individuals. It's the multiplication itself, not the phrase "scale up," that earns the mark.

Belt transects

A transect places quadrats at regular, fixed intervals along a line crossing an environmental gradient, with the abiotic factor recorded alongside distribution or abundance so a correlation between the two can be tested. This is systematic, not random, sampling — chosen deliberately to reveal a trend, gradient or zonation, in direct contrast to random quadrat placement, which exists purely to avoid investigator bias.

Mark–release–recapture and the Lincoln index

For motile organisms, capture a sample, count and mark them (in a way that doesn't harm them, doesn't wash off, and doesn't make them more visible to predators or otherwise change their behaviour), then release them and allow sufficient time for them to redistribute and mix randomly back into the population. A second sample is then caught using the same method, and the number of marked individuals within it is counted. The population size is estimated using the Lincoln index:

N = (n₁ × n₂) ÷ n₃

where n₁ = number caught and marked at the first visit, n₂ = total number caught at the second visit, and n₃ = the number of marked individuals recaptured at the second visit.

🧮

Worked example — the Lincoln index. 30 field voles are caught, marked and released (n₁ = 30). A second sample of 45 voles is later caught, of which 6 are marked (n₂ = 45, n₃ = 6). N = (30 × 45) ÷ 6 = 1350 ÷ 6 = 225 voles.

This calculation relies on a set of assumptions: the population is closed (no significant births, deaths, immigration or emigration between the two visits); marking has no effect on survival or recapture probability; and marked individuals have enough time to redistribute and mix randomly back into the population before the second sample is taken.

▶ 🚩 Trap — the most commonly omitted assumption

The omission reported most often is forgetting to state that sufficient time must be left for marked organisms to disperse and mix back in before the second visit. Assumptions should also be stated in full — a bare "population doesn't change" is incomplete without naming births, deaths and migration explicitly. If a mark rubs off between visits, fewer marked individuals are recaptured than there should be, which makes n₃ artificially low and so the population estimate too high — worth being ready to reason through in that direction too.

Section 6 · spec content point

🔄 Ecosystems as dynamic systems

A short but important idea that reframes everything already covered, and sets up succession: an ecosystem is never a fixed, unchanging picture.

📖

What the specification says: "Ecosystems are dynamic systems."

1
2
3

An ecosystem is never like a photograph, frozen still — it's more like a video that's always playing. Population sizes wobble up and down around the carrying capacity, predator and prey numbers rise and fall in their repeating cycle, and — over a much longer timescale — whole communities of species can be gradually replaced by different ones. "Dynamic" just means: always moving, never standing completely still.

Being "dynamic" doesn't mean chaotic or unstable — it means an ecosystem constantly adjusts around a moving balance rather than sitting at one fixed value. Over short timescales, this shows up as population sizes fluctuating around the carrying capacity, driven by competition and predator–prey cycles. Over much longer timescales, it shows up as whole communities changing through succession, as the species present at any one time change the conditions for the species that come after them.

An ecosystem is a dynamic system — never in a fixed, static state, but constantly adjusting around a moving equilibrium rather than settling at one permanent value. This idea operates on two timescales that this spec point covers in turn:

  • Short-term dynamics — population sizes fluctuate around the carrying capacity as intraspecific and interspecific competition, predation, and changing abiotic conditions act on them (Sections 3–4).
  • Long-term dynamics — over a much longer timescale, entire communities change through succession, as the species present at each stage alter the abiotic environment, making conditions progressively more suitable for different species and less suitable for themselves (Sections 7–9).

Even a "stable" climax community, reached at the end of succession, is still a dynamic equilibrium rather than a frozen end-point — species persist there because they are in balance with their abiotic surroundings, not because all change has stopped completely.

▶ Why this idea matters beyond its own mark

This single sentence in the specification is really the umbrella statement for the rest of 3.7.4: it's the reason carrying capacity is described as something a population fluctuates around rather than settles at, and it's the reason succession is framed as an ongoing process with a "climax" rather than a true, unchanging endpoint. Treat it as context for the sections either side of it, not as an isolated fact to learn on its own.

Section 7 · spec content point

🌱 Primary succession: from bare ground to climax community

The staged causal sequence examiners want — not just the two end labels of "pioneer species" and "climax community," but the whole chain that gets from one to the other.

📖

What the specification says: "Primary succession, from colonisation by pioneer species to climax community."

1
2
3

Picture a patch of completely bare rock, freshly exposed — nothing can grow there yet except the toughest, hardiest species around, like lichens and mosses. These are the pioneer species. As they live and die, they slowly build up a thin layer of soil. Once there's a bit of soil, slightly less tough plants — grasses — can move in and take over. As those grow, die and add more soil, shrubs and small trees can move in next. Eventually, after a long time, a full woodland grows — a settled, stable community called the climax community.

Primary succession starts from a completely bare, hostile substrate with no soil at all — bare rock, sand, or volcanic ash. Pioneer species are the first colonisers, and they have to be tough enough to survive the harsh starting conditions: little or no soil, high exposure, low nutrients. Over time, a predictable sequence of communities replaces one another — each stage making the habitat progressively less hostile, until a stable climax community is eventually reached, in equilibrium with the local conditions.

Pioneer species colonise a bare, hostile environment (such as bare rock) — they must tolerate extreme starting conditions: little or no soil, high exposure, and low nutrient availability. As the habitat progresses through a sequence of communities — often called seres — biodiversity increases and soil depth increases at each stage, until eventually a stable climax community is reached: a community in dynamic equilibrium with the local abiotic conditions, with no further net change in species composition.

Each stage changes the abiotic environment (more organic matter, deeper soil) so species better adapted to the new conditions out-compete and replace the last — biodiversity and soil depth increase at every stage until a stable climax community is reached.
🔎

Secondary succession, briefly: succession that starts where soil is already present — for example after a fire, or when farmland is abandoned — is called secondary succession. Because soil already exists, it proceeds faster and begins from a later stage than primary succession does.

Section 8 · spec content point

🔁 How each stage changes the environment for the next

This is the actual mechanism behind succession — why species get replaced at each stage, and the precision trap examiners flag most often around it.

📖

What the specification says: "At each stage in succession, certain species may be recognised which change the environment so that it becomes more suitable for other species with different adaptations. The new species may change the environment in such a way that it becomes less suitable for the previous species."

1
2
3

It's tempting to think the pioneer species "get used to" the new conditions and slowly turn into the next species — but that's not what happens. Instead, imagine a group of early settlers who build roads and houses in a wild area. They didn't personally turn into city-dwellers — they built the conditions that let a completely different, new group of people move in and take over instead, while the original settlers often struggle once things get more crowded and developed. Succession works the same way: the pioneers change the environment, and then get replaced by different species better suited to the changed conditions — the individuals themselves never change.

At each stage, the species present change their own abiotic surroundings — usually by dying and decomposing, which adds organic matter and builds up soil. This makes conditions less hostile and more suitable for a new set of species with different adaptations, which then out-compete and replace the previous occupants. Crucially, the same environmental change that helps the new species can make things worse for the old ones — for example, taller plants shading out the smaller, light-demanding species that came before them. It's the community that changes at each stage, through replacement — not the individual organisms adapting or transforming.

As species live, die and decompose at any stage in succession, they change the abiotic environment — most often by adding organic matter and helping soil form, which increases the habitat's capacity to hold water and mineral ions. This typically makes the environment less hostile and more suitable for other species with different adaptations, allowing those new species to colonise and out-compete the previous occupants — so the dominant species changes at each stage. The relationship can run the other way too: the same environmental change caused by the new species can make conditions less suitable for the species that were there before — for instance, taller, later-successional plants shading out smaller, light-demanding pioneers.

▶ 🚩 Trap — species are replaced, they don't personally adapt

A very common wrong answer describes succession as "the plants adapt to the conditions." This is rejected: individual organisms do not change during succession — species are replaced by different species that already carry different adaptations. Always phrase it as one species out-competing and replacing another, never as an existing population gradually adapting in place.

▶ 🚩 Trap — precision in a named pioneer example

In the commonly used beach-grass pioneer example, describing the grass as something that "blocks all the light" from reaching the ground is rejected — the credited phrasing is that it reduces or absorbs more of the light. This is a precision-about-degree issue: avoid absolute claims ("blocks all," "completely prevents") when a partial, graded effect is what's actually being described.

Section 9 · spec content point

🌍 Abiotic change & biodiversity through succession

The consequence of Section 8's mechanism, stated as its own idea: succession doesn't just replace species, it also changes how many different species can coexist.

📖

What the specification says: "Changes that organisms produce in their abiotic environment can result in a less hostile environment and change biodiversity."

1
2
3

Think of a brand-new, empty housing estate versus an old, established town. The empty estate can only support a few types of shops — maybe just a corner shop. But once roads, water and electricity are properly built up (like soil forming), a much wider range of shops and services can open. Succession works the same way — as the "infrastructure" (soil, water retention, shelter) builds up, more and more different species can be supported at once, so biodiversity climbs at each stage.

The environmental changes organisms cause during succession — organic matter and soil building up, more water and mineral ions being retained, more shelter and shade becoming available — generally make the habitat less hostile over time. A less hostile habitat can support a wider range of adaptations, so more species are able to survive there at once, and biodiversity increases at each successive stage. If succession is interrupted by human activity before it reaches its climax, this process is halted at an earlier, lower-biodiversity stage — called a plagioclimax, developed fully in Section 10.

Each stage in succession changes the abiotic environment in ways that generally make it less hostile — organic matter and soil accumulate, water and mineral-ion retention improve, and shelter and microclimate become more varied. A less hostile, more varied abiotic environment can support species with a wider range of adaptations, so biodiversity increases at each successive stage of succession, alongside the increase in soil depth shown in Figure (Section 7).

This increase in biodiversity is itself a creditable point that's frequently missed — answers on succession are often purely descriptive of the species sequence, without mentioning that biodiversity changes as a direct consequence of the abiotic changes involved.

🔎

Looking ahead to conservation: if succession is deflected or halted before it reaches a true climax — usually by human activity such as grazing, mowing, burning or felling — the community is held at an earlier stage, called a plagioclimax, rather than reaching the climax community that would otherwise develop. Section 10 covers why this is often done deliberately, as a conservation tool.

▶ Don't confuse succession with speciation or natural selection

Succession is well known "in outline" — pioneer species through to climax community — but weaker answers are purely descriptive, unlinked to data, and sometimes confuse succession with natural selection or speciation entirely. These are genuinely different processes: succession is a community-level change in which species present change over time through replacement; speciation is the evolutionary origin of an entirely new species. Keep the vocabulary of each separate.

Section 10 · spec content point

🛡️ Conservation of habitats & managed succession

Conservation deliberately works against the natural direction of succession — and evaluating evidence for it follows a fixed, learnable structure.

📖

What the specification says: "Conservation of habitats frequently involves management of succession."

1
2
3

Left completely alone, a field will eventually turn into woodland through succession — but woodland can only support a narrower range of species than a field full of grasses, flowers and open ground. So conservationists sometimes deliberately "freeze" succession at an earlier stage on purpose — by grazing animals on it, mowing it, or occasionally burning it — so it never reaches woodland, and a wider mix of species gets to stay. It's a bit like deliberately keeping a garden a bit wild and untidy, on purpose, because a perfectly tidy lawn or a fully grown forest each support far fewer different plants and insects than something in between.

Left to run its full course, succession ends in a climax community dominated by a small number of highly competitive species — which actually reduces overall biodiversity compared with an earlier stage. Managed succession deliberately halts this process — through grazing, mowing, coppicing or controlled burning — holding the habitat at an earlier stage (a plagioclimax) that supports a wider range of niches and species than the climax community would.

Conservation is justified for several distinct reasons — economic, ecological, genetic and ethical/aesthetic — and evaluating evidence about it follows a fixed structure: state what the data actually show, give the argument each side would make from it, then critique the evidence's limitations.

Managed succession as a conservation tool

Deliberately halting succession before a climax community develops — by grazing, mowing, coppicing or controlled burning — maintains a plagioclimax, which supports a wider range of niches and species than the climax community it prevents from forming. This is the main worked application of "management of succession" in conservation contexts: a single dominant climax species, left unchecked, would otherwise out-compete and reduce the diversity present at an earlier stage.

Evaluating conflicting conservation evidence

A well-structured evaluation follows a fixed pattern: state what the data show, with specific figures; give the argument each side of the conservation debate would make from that data; then critique the evidence's limitations (sample size, time period, whether other variables were controlled, whether the study covers the whole habitat). A one-sided answer, or one that quotes figures without naming a specific limitation, will always be capped — at least one concrete weakness in the evidence should always be named.

Case study: integrated pest control

Rather than relying on chemical pesticides or biological control agents alone, an integrated approach combines both, drawing on the different strengths of each. Biological agents settle into a stable predator–prey-type relationship with the pest (Section 4) — this is exactly why they keep a pest population at a persistently low level over the long term, rather than eliminating it outright, and because they're specific to the target pest, they avoid harming non-target species and the pest doesn't develop resistance to them. Chemical pesticides act quickly and can be applied to a specific area, killing a greater variety or proportion of pests immediately — useful where a fast initial reduction is needed. Combined, the two approaches offset each other's weaknesses: a chemical brings the population down rapidly, while a biological agent maintains it at a low level afterwards, generally allowing less chemical to be used overall and reducing the risk of chemical residue and bioaccumulation.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "immunity" is the wrong word

Pests do not develop "immunity" to a biological control agent — the correct term is resistance. "Immunity" describes a different biological concept entirely and is specifically rejected here.

🧪 Required practical 12

Turning the sampling methods from Section 5 into a full investigation, including which statistical test to reach for and why.

📖

What the specification says: "Required practical 12: Investigation into the effect of a named environmental factor on the distribution of a given species."

1
2
3

Imagine wanting to know whether a plant grows better in sunny or shady spots. You'd measure the light in both places, count the plants using quadrats in both places, and then check whether the numbers actually line up with the light levels using a proper statistical test — rather than just eyeballing it and guessing. And even if they do line up, that's not automatically proof that light caused it — something else might be going on behind the scenes.

RP12 investigates whether a named abiotic factor affects where a species is found. The design: choose and measure a named factor (light intensity, soil moisture, pH), then sample at least two areas that differ in that factor using 20 or more randomly placed quadrats in each, recording species presence, frequency or percentage cover alongside the measured factor. Which statistical test to use depends on what's being compared: a t-test for comparing two means, a correlation coefficient for testing an association between the factor and abundance, or chi-squared for comparing observed against expected frequencies. Crucially, even a genuine statistical correlation doesn't prove the factor actually causes the pattern seen.

Design

Choose and measure a named abiotic factor (for example light intensity, soil moisture, or pH), and place 20 or more randomly positioned quadrats in each of at least two areas being compared, recording species presence, frequency or percentage cover alongside the measured factor. Controlled variables matter here too — sampling at the same time of day, in similar weather, using the same quadrat size, and (for percentage cover) the same observer throughout.

A correctly worded null hypothesis for the specific variables in the study must also be stated — for example, "there is no significant difference/association between [named factor] and [named species' distribution/abundance]."

📌

Explicitly credited evaluation point: correlation does not prove causation. A measured association between the abiotic factor and species distribution does not, by itself, prove that factor causes the distribution pattern seen — other unmeasured, co-varying factors could be responsible instead.

This links directly to statistical test-selection logic used elsewhere in the course: if the calculated probability (p) is less than 0.05, the probability that the observed difference or association is due to chance alone is less than 5%, so the difference is considered statistically significant and the null hypothesis is rejected.

Section 13 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

This spec point supplies some of the clearest worked examples for essay questions about interaction, competition, or relationships between organisms — use it as the essay's ecological-scale evidence, not its whole argument.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around "the importance of competition in biology"

Competition operates at every biological scale. At the ecological scale, intraspecific competition — within one species, for a limited resource such as food, water, light, space, mineral ions or breeding sites — increases as a population approaches its carrying capacity, regulating population size as birth rate falls and death rate rises. Interspecific competition, between different species, arises because two species cannot occupy the same niche indefinitely; sustained competition results in one species out-competing and often eliminating the other locally, the competitive exclusion principle. This shows competition acting as a genuine regulating force on population size and community composition, not simply a background pressure.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around "relationships between organisms of different species"

Predator–prey population cycles show a lagged, dynamic relationship between two species' population sizes: as prey numbers rise, more food becomes available to predators, so — after a delay — predator numbers rise too; increased predation pressure then reduces prey numbers, which in turn reduces food availability for predators, so their numbers fall as well, allowing prey to recover. This demonstrates that a relationship between two species can regulate both populations simultaneously, without either population growing or declining without bound.

Whichever essay theme this content supports, treat it as ecological-scale supporting evidence: name the specific mechanism precisely (intraspecific/interspecific competition, the predator–prey causal chain, or succession's stage-by-stage replacement), then close with one sentence linking it explicitly back to the essay's actual title, rather than leaving it as a stand-alone description.

  • Population → community (different species) → ecosystem (+ non-living/abiotic components), in that order.
  • Ecosystems range from tiny to huge — scale is never part of the definition.

  • Niche = the role a species occupies, governed by adaptation to abiotic and biotic conditions.
  • Never "where an organism lives" — that's habitat, and it's explicitly rejected.

  • K = max population size a habitat sustainably supports; sigmoid curve = lag → exponential → stationary at K.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Populations, communities & ecosystems

❓ Define the terms population, community and ecosystem.
Population — all the organisms of one species in a habitat at a given time. Community — all the populations of different species living together in a habitat. Ecosystem — a community together with the non-living, abiotic components of its environment, and the interactions between them.
❓ A student says "an ecosystem is just a really big habitat." Explain why this is incorrect.
An ecosystem is defined by its living community plus the non-living, abiotic components of its environment and the interactions between them, not by its physical size — ecosystems range from very small to very large, so scale alone does not define one, and a habitat on its own contains no reference to abiotic components or a community of populations.

The ecological niche

❓ Define the term niche, and explain why "where an organism lives" is not an acceptable definition.
A niche is the role a species occupies within its habitat, governed by adaptation to both abiotic and biotic conditions. "Where an organism lives" describes the habitat, not the niche — a niche is a functional role, including a species' position within the food web and its abiotic tolerance range, not a location.
❓ Explain why two species cannot occupy the same niche in the same habitat indefinitely.
If two species occupy the same niche, they require the same resources, so they are forced into direct competition for them; because they cannot both have unlimited access to the same limited resource, one species out-competes and eliminates the other, at least locally — the competitive exclusion principle.

Carrying capacity & competition

❓ Explain why a population's growth slows as it approaches its carrying capacity.
As the population increases in density, intraspecific competition for a limited resource (such as food, water, light, space, mineral ions or breeding sites) increases; this causes birth rate to fall and/or death rate to rise, so population growth slows and the population size stabilises around the carrying capacity.
❓ Distinguish between intraspecific and interspecific competition.
Intraspecific competition is competition between individuals of the same species for a shared resource, and regulates that species' own carrying capacity. Interspecific competition is competition between different species for the same resource or niche, and can reduce the carrying capacity, growth rate or distribution of one or both species, potentially leading to competitive exclusion.
❓ A student writes "the fish population levelled off because resources became limited." Improve this answer.
Name the specific limiting resource shown or implied by the data (e.g. a named food source, dissolved oxygen, or nesting/spawning sites), and state that intraspecific competition for that resource increased as density rose, causing birth rate to fall and/or death rate to rise — a generic "resources became limited" answer without a named resource is incomplete.

Predator–prey population cycles

❓ A graph shows lynx and hare population sizes cycling, with the lynx peak consistently following the hare peak. Explain this pattern.
As hare (prey) numbers rise, more food is available to lynx (predators), so — after a time lag, because it takes time for extra food to translate into breeding success — the lynx population rises too. Increased lynx numbers raise predation pressure, so hare numbers fall; with less prey available, lynx numbers then fall too, again with a lag, allowing hare numbers to recover and the cycle to repeat.
❓ Explain why the predator population never rises at exactly the same time as the prey population.
There is a time lag between a rise in prey numbers (and the resulting increase in food availability) and the resulting increase in predator numbers, because it takes time for the extra food to be converted into increased predator breeding success — the predator population therefore always lags behind, rather than tracking the prey population exactly.
❓ Evaluate the claim "predation is the only factor that controls prey population size."
This claim is incomplete: while predation pressure does reduce prey numbers, food availability, space, disease and other abiotic factors also limit the prey population independently of predation, so predation should be described as one of several regulating factors rather than the sole control.

Sampling population size

❓ Describe how you would estimate the population size of a slow-moving, non-motile plant species in a field using random quadrats, including how you would scale up your result.
Lay out two tape measures at right angles to form a grid across the field, and use a random number generator or table to obtain coordinate pairs; place a quadrat (typically 20 or more) at each pair of coordinates and count the number of individuals (or estimate percentage cover) in each. Calculate the mean count per quadrat, then scale up by multiplying the mean by the total area of the field divided by the area of one quadrat.
❓ A student states "I scaled up my quadrat data to estimate the total population." Why would this alone not gain full credit, and what should be added?
Stating "scale up" without showing the actual multiplication does not earn credit — the working must be shown: mean count per quadrat multiplied by (total habitat area ÷ area of one quadrat), giving the estimated total population as a numerical answer.
❓ 40 woodlice are caught, marked and released. Two days later, a second sample of 60 woodlice is caught, of which 8 are marked. Calculate the estimated population size, and state two assumptions made.
N = (n₁ × n₂) ÷ n₃ = (40 × 60) ÷ 8 = 2400 ÷ 8 = 300 woodlice. Assumptions: the population is closed, with no significant births, deaths, immigration or emigration between the two samples; and marking does not affect the survival or recapture probability of the marked individuals (it does not increase predation risk, wash off, or change behaviour). Sufficient time must also have been allowed for marked individuals to redistribute randomly through the population before the second sample was taken.

Ecosystems as dynamic systems

❓ Explain what is meant by describing an ecosystem as a "dynamic system."
A dynamic system is one that is constantly changing rather than fixed at one permanent state; population sizes fluctuate around the carrying capacity due to competition and predation over short timescales, while over longer timescales whole communities change through succession as species alter the abiotic conditions for those that follow — even a climax community represents a dynamic equilibrium, not a state with no further change at all.

Primary succession

❓ Describe primary succession from bare rock to a climax community.
Pioneer species, adapted to tolerate harsh conditions such as little or no soil and high exposure, colonise the bare rock first. As they live, die and decompose, organic matter accumulates and soil begins to form, making the environment less hostile. This allows new species, better suited to the changed conditions, to colonise and out-compete the previous species, so the dominant species changes at each stage; biodiversity and soil depth increase throughout. Eventually a stable climax community is reached, in dynamic equilibrium with the local abiotic conditions.
❓ Distinguish between primary and secondary succession.
Primary succession begins on a bare substrate with no soil present, such as bare rock, sand or volcanic ash, and pioneer species must tolerate very harsh initial conditions. Secondary succession begins where soil is already present, for example after a fire or on abandoned farmland, so it proceeds faster and starts from a later successional stage.

How species change the environment

❓ A student answers "at each stage of succession, the plants adapt to survive the new conditions." Explain why this is incorrect and give the correct explanation.
Individual organisms do not adapt or change during succession — the species present are replaced by different species that already possess different adaptations suited to the changed abiotic conditions. The correct explanation is that species at one stage change the abiotic environment (e.g. by adding organic matter as they decompose), making it more suitable for a different species, which then out-competes and replaces the original species.
❓ In a named example, a pioneer grass species is described as growing over bare sand. Explain, using precise wording, how this pioneer changes conditions for the species that follow, and identify one imprecise phrase to avoid.
The pioneer grass reduces (or absorbs more of) the light reaching the sand surface and its roots help stabilise and trap organic matter, allowing a thin layer of soil to begin forming; this makes conditions less hostile and more suitable for the next species in the succession. Avoid the phrase "blocks all the light," which is rejected — it should be described as a partial reduction, not an absolute one.

Abiotic change & biodiversity

❓ Explain why biodiversity typically increases during succession.
As species change the abiotic environment at each stage — increasing organic matter, soil depth, and water/mineral-ion retention — the habitat becomes progressively less hostile; a less hostile environment can support species with a wider range of adaptations, so more species are able to coexist, and biodiversity increases at each successive stage.

Conservation & managed succession

❓ Explain how managed succession is used to conserve biodiversity in a habitat.
Left unmanaged, succession would proceed to a climax community dominated by a small number of highly competitive species, reducing biodiversity. Management — such as grazing, mowing, coppicing or controlled burning — deliberately halts succession at an earlier stage, maintaining a plagioclimax; this earlier stage supports a wider range of niches and therefore more species than the climax community would.
❓ State and briefly explain the four categories of reason given for conserving a habitat.
Economic — species provide medicines, food and materials, and habitats support tourism and fisheries. Ecological — greater biodiversity gives more stable ecosystems and maintains services such as pollination and nutrient cycling. Genetic — conservation preserves a large gene pool, retaining alleles potentially useful for future breeding or adaptation. Ethical/aesthetic — species have an inherent right to exist, and landscapes have value in themselves.
❓ Explain the advantages of using an integrated system of biological and chemical pest control, rather than either method alone.
A biological control agent is specific to the target pest, so it does not harm non-target species; it settles into a stable predator–prey-type relationship with the pest, keeping its population at a persistently low level over the long term without the pest developing resistance, and often needs only one application since it can reproduce. A chemical pesticide acts quickly and can be applied to a specific area, killing a greater variety or proportion of pests immediately. Combined, the fast initial reduction from the chemical is followed by long-term, low-level control from the biological agent, generally allowing less chemical to be used and reducing the risk of chemical residues.

Required practical 12

❓ Design an investigation into the effect of light intensity on the distribution of a named moss species across a woodland floor.
Measure light intensity using a light meter at a series of sampling points across the woodland floor, selecting at least two areas that differ clearly in light intensity. In each area, place 20 or more randomly positioned quadrats and record moss percentage cover using a gridded quadrat alongside the corresponding light intensity reading. Keep other variables controlled (same time of day, similar weather, same quadrat size and observer), then test the relationship between light intensity and percentage cover using a correlation coefficient such as Spearman's rank.
❓ A student calculates a significant correlation between soil moisture and plant abundance, and concludes "soil moisture causes the increase in plant abundance." Evaluate this conclusion.
A statistically significant correlation shows an association between the two variables but does not, by itself, prove that soil moisture causes the change in plant abundance; other unmeasured, co-varying factors (such as light intensity, soil pH or nutrient availability) could also differ between the sampling areas and be responsible for the pattern observed — correlation does not prove causation.
