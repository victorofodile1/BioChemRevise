The single habit that protects the most marks on this spec point: when an uptake graph rises then flattens, the plateau is never caused by "losing the concentration gradient" — the gradient is still there. What runs out is the number of channel or carrier proteins, which become saturated. Section 1 · spec content point

🧱 Why every cell membrane shares one basic structure

Before any transport mechanism makes sense, you need to be precise about what a "membrane" actually is — because the exam draws a sharp line between the general term and the specific one.

📖

What the specification says: "The basic structure of all cell membranes, including cell-surface membranes and the membranes around the cell organelles of eukaryotes, is the same."

1
2
3

Imagine a house made of the same kind of wall material everywhere — the outside walls of the house and the walls of a locked room inside it are built the exact same way, just used in different places. A cell is like that: the "skin" around the whole cell and the "skin" around little compartments inside it (like the powerhouse or the food-making compartment) are made of the exact same kind of material, a bendy double layer with proteins dotted through it.

  • Plasma membrane = the general name for "a wall made of this material," wherever it is in the cell
  • Cell-surface membrane = specifically the outer skin around the whole cell
  • Organelle = a compartment inside the cell with its own job, e.g. the powerhouse (mitochondrion)

Plasma membrane is the umbrella term for any membrane in or around a cell. Cell-surface membrane is the specific name for the one membrane that forms the outer boundary of the whole cell, separating the cytoplasm from the environment outside. Every internal organelle — mitochondria, chloroplasts, the endoplasmic reticulum, lysosomes — is bounded by its own membrane built from the same basic phospholipid-and-protein design, even though each one is doing a completely different job. Mitochondria and chloroplasts are unusual in having two of these membranes, one inside the other.

The exam distinguishes two terms that are easy to blur. Plasma membrane is the general term for any membrane found around, or inside, a cell. Cell-surface membrane refers specifically to the plasma membrane that surrounds the entire cell — the boundary between the cytoplasm and the external environment. Mitochondria and chloroplasts are each bounded by two plasma membranes, not one.

If you're shown a diagram of a bilayer and asked to identify whether it's the cell-surface membrane or an organelle membrane, the deciding evidence is whether a cell wall sits outside it, or cellulose is visible on the outside — only the cell-surface membrane can have a wall built directly against it.

Why cells need internal membranes at all

  • They control the entry and exit of materials into and out of discrete organelles, e.g. mitochondria, chloroplasts.
  • They separate organelles from the cytoplasm, so specific metabolic reactions can proceed within them without outside interference.
  • They provide an internal transport system, e.g. the endoplasmic reticulum.
  • They isolate enzymes that would otherwise damage the cell, e.g. inside lysosomes.
  • They provide surfaces for reactions to occur on, e.g. ribosomes attached to rough ER.

A synoptic osmosis link worth knowing

An isolated organelle (e.g. a chloroplast suspension) has to be kept in a solution with a matching water potential to the organelle itself. If the two water potentials matched exactly, no net osmosis would occur across the organelle's membrane, so the organelle neither takes in nor loses water and does not burst, lyse, shrivel, or shrink.

Distribution of membranesEukaryotic cellProkaryotic cell
Internal membrane-bound organellesPresent — nucleus, mitochondria, ER, Golgi, etc.Absent — no membrane-bound organelles, no nuclear envelope
Membranes presentCell-surface membrane plus many internal membranesCell-surface membrane only
▶ 🚩 Trap — organelle wording, not cell wording

When a question is specifically about an isolated organelle rather than a whole cell, "the cell bursts / shrivels" is not credited — it's an organelle, not a cell. "Becomes turgid / flaccid" is also wrong here, because those terms only ever apply to walled plant cells. Stick to organelle-appropriate language: bursts / lyses / shrivels / shrinks.

Section 2 · spec content point

🧬 The fluid-mosaic model

Five components, each with its own arrangement and its own job. This diagram gets drawn wrongly by a lot of students nationally — learn it cold, including exactly how a protein sits relative to the phospholipids around it.

📖

What the specification says: "The arrangement and any movement of phospholipids, proteins, glycoproteins and glycolipids in the fluid-mosaic model of membrane structure. Cholesterol may also be present in cell membranes where it restricts the movement of other molecules making up the membrane."

1
2
3

Picture a swimming pool covered by lots of floating rafts. The "water" side of everything faces outward (top and bottom of the membrane), and the "avoid-water" side hides in the middle, sandwiched between two layers. Some rafts have tunnels through them (for things to swim through), some rafts are like ferries that grab a passenger and carry it across, and some little marbles (cholesterol) are wedged between the rafts to stop them jiggling around too freely. Little flags (sugar chains) poke up on the outside only, so the cell can be recognised by its neighbours.

  • Phospholipid = the raft material itself, arranged in two layers
  • Channel protein = a tunnel raft
  • Carrier protein = a ferry raft
  • Cholesterol = the marbles wedged between rafts
  • Glycoprotein / glycolipid = the flags on the outside only

The membrane is a phospholipid bilayer: two layers of phospholipid, hydrophilic (water-loving) heads facing outward to the water on both sides, hydrophobic (water-fearing) tails facing inward and meeting in the middle. Scattered through this bilayer are proteins of different shapes and sizes — some sit in only one surface, others span all the way across. A spanning protein is either a channel (a water-filled pore, for ions) or a carrier (binds a molecule, changes shape, releases it on the other side — no pore). Cholesterol sits within the tails and restricts how freely everything else can move. On the outer surface only, some lipids and proteins carry short carbohydrate chains — glycolipids and glycoproteins — which act as recognition markers. "Fluid" describes the way phospholipids and unanchored proteins can drift sideways within their own layer; "mosaic" describes the scattered, irregular pattern the different protein types make when you look down on the membrane.

A simplified fluid-mosaic membrane — bilayer, channel protein, carrier protein, cholesterol, and outer-surface-only glycolipid/glycoprotein chains.

Why the phospholipids arrange as a bilayer

Water is present on both the inside and the outside of the cell, so the hydrophobic fatty-acid tails point away from — are repelled by — the water on each side, while the hydrophilic phosphate heads point towards, and are attracted to, the water. That's the whole reason the bilayer forms itself the way it does.

Why the membrane is a barrier to most molecules

  • Not lipid-soluble, so they can't cross the hydrophobic tails directly.
  • Too large for the channels present.
  • Electrically charged or polar — repelled by the non-polar tails.
  • The wrong charge, or wrong shape, for the specific protein channel present.
📎

Applied cholesterol contexts worth knowing: red blood cells are unsupported by any surrounding tissue, so cholesterol helps maintain their shape more than it does for a cell like an ileum epithelial cell, which is supported by neighbouring cells. A pathogen struggles to force its way into a membrane where cholesterol is restricting movement, because it can't easily build a channel through it. A prokaryote has no cholesterol at all, yet keeps a constant shape anyway — because it has a rigid cell wall made of peptidoglycan (murein) instead.

▶ 🚩 Trap — drawing a protein into the membrane

If you're asked to draw phospholipids around a given protein, every head must extend into the hydrophilic region of the protein and every tail into the hydrophobic region, on both faces of the bilayer — phospholipids drawn actually within the protein are rejected. This is a nationally poorly-executed question, so it's worth practising from memory using the diagram above.

A second trap: never write that "hydrophilic heads protect the hydrophobic tails" — that's not the credited mechanism. The heads and tails arrange themselves in response to water, not to shield one another.

▶ Comparing the fluid-mosaic model with an older membrane model

Similarity: both models have a phospholipid bilayer, with hydrophobic tails facing each other and hydrophilic heads facing outwards, and both include protein.

Differences (any two): the older model has no channel or carrier proteins — or shows protein as a continuous layer outside the phospholipids rather than scattered ("dotted") through them; it has no cholesterol; no glycoprotein; no glycolipid.

A "give a similarity and differences" question like this is capped at partial marks if the similarity point is missed entirely — you can't score full marks on the differences alone.

Section 3 · spec content point (part 1 of 5 — movement across membranes)

➡️ Simple diffusion

The simplest of the five processes, and the baseline everything else gets compared against. This section, and the next four, all cover the same specification content point — each takes one of the five named mechanisms.

📖

What the specification says: "Movement across membranes occurs by: simple diffusion (involving limitations imposed by the nature of the phospholipid bilayer); facilitated diffusion (involving the roles of carrier proteins and channel proteins); osmosis (explained in terms of water potential); active transport (involving the role of carrier proteins and the importance of the hydrolysis of ATP); co-transport (illustrated by the absorption of sodium ions and glucose by cells lining the mammalian ileum)." (One content point, covered across Sections 3–7 — this section covers simple diffusion.)

1
2
3

Imagine spraying perfume in the corner of a room. At first it's strong in one spot, but the smell gradually spreads out on its own until it's evenly mixed through the whole room — nobody has to push it, the tiny particles just naturally wander from where there's lots of them to where there's fewer. That's diffusion. A warmer room makes it spread faster, because the particles are jiggling around more.

  • Concentration gradient = the difference between "lots here" and "little there"
  • Passive = happens by itself, no extra push needed

Diffusion is the net movement of molecules or ions from where they're more concentrated to where they're less concentrated, until they're evenly spread out. It's passive — it runs on the particles' own kinetic energy, not on ATP. Small, non-polar, lipid-soluble molecules like oxygen and carbon dioxide can slip straight through the phospholipid tails without needing any protein at all. The bigger the concentration difference, the faster the rate; a warmer temperature also speeds it up, because particles have more kinetic energy and move faster and more randomly.

Diffusion is the net movement of molecules or ions from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, until they are evenly distributed. It's passive: it uses the kinetic energy of the particles themselves, not ATP. Only small, non-polar, lipid-soluble substances (O₂, CO₂) can cross the phospholipid bilayer directly this way — everything else is limited by the nature of the bilayer itself and needs a protein (Section 4).

Fick's law and the straight-line graph

Rate of diffusion is proportional to (surface area × difference in concentration) ÷ thickness of the exchange surface. With surface area and thickness held constant, rate is directly proportional to the concentration difference — which is why a simple-diffusion uptake graph is a straight line that keeps rising, rather than levelling off. Diffusion is proportional specifically to the difference in concentration, not to "concentration" on its own — and it only occurs between two different concentrations of the same substance.

Increasing temperature increases the rate of diffusion, because particles gain more kinetic energy, so they move faster and more randomly, and the net movement down the gradient speeds up.

FeatureSimple diffusion
DirectionDown the concentration gradient
Protein needed?None
ATP used?No
What movesSmall, non-polar, lipid-soluble molecules (O₂, CO₂)
▶ Diffusion vs osmosis — two shared features

Diffusion and osmosis share two properties worth being able to state together: (1) both involve movement down a gradient, from high to low; and (2) both are passive — neither uses energy from respiration, ATP, or metabolism. Never write "movement from gradient to gradient" (meaningless phrasing) and never write "do not use energy" unqualified — you must specify that it's ATP/respiration/metabolic energy that isn't used, since diffusion still uses the particles' own kinetic energy.

Section 4 · spec content point (part 2 of 5 — movement across membranes)

🚇 Facilitated diffusion

Still passive, still down the concentration gradient — but now a protein is required, and that protein is what creates the plateau on the uptake graph that trips so many students up.

📖

What the specification says: "Movement across membranes occurs by: simple diffusion (involving limitations imposed by the nature of the phospholipid bilayer); facilitated diffusion (involving the roles of carrier proteins and channel proteins); osmosis (explained in terms of water potential); active transport (involving the role of carrier proteins and the importance of the hydrolysis of ATP); co-transport (illustrated by the absorption of sodium ions and glucose by cells lining the mammalian ileum)." (Same content point as Section 3 — this section covers facilitated diffusion.)

1
2
3

Some things are too big, or the wrong shape, to sneak between the phospholipids, so they need a helper. A tunnel (channel protein) lets a matching-shaped particle swim straight through a hole. A ferry (carrier protein) picks a particle up on one side, changes shape like a rotating door, and drops it off on the other side. Neither helper needs any extra energy to work — the particle is still just moving from crowded to less crowded. But if every tunnel and ferry is busy at once, extra particles just have to wait — that's why the "how much gets through" graph flattens out even though there's still plenty of particles waiting to get in.

Channel proteins form water-filled pores through the membrane for specific water-soluble ions; each one opens only in the presence of the ion it's shaped for. Carrier proteins bind a specific molecule (like glucose), change shape, and release it on the other side — no pore involved. Both still move substances down their concentration gradient, and neither uses ATP. On an uptake graph, the rate rises in proportion to the external concentration at first, then flattens into a plateau once every available channel or carrier protein is in use — the concentration gradient is still there, it's the number of proteins that's become the limiting factor.

Channel proteinCarrier protein
StructureWater-filled hydrophilic pore spanning the membraneSpans the membrane, no permanent pore
MechanismOpens specifically in the presence of the ion it's shaped forBinds a specific molecule, changes shape, releases it on the other side
What it movesSpecific water-soluble ionsLarger polar molecules, e.g. glucose, amino acids
Binding site nameBinding site — never "active site" (active sites belong to enzymes, and this wording is rejected here)

Both processes still move substances down a concentration gradient, and neither uses ATP — the only thing facilitated diffusion adds over simple diffusion is the requirement for a specific protein.

Explaining the rising-then-plateau shape of an uptake curve

  1. Movement is occurring through carrier (or channel) proteins — i.e. by facilitated diffusion.
  2. On the rising section, the rate of uptake is proportional to the external concentration.
  3. At the plateau, all the channel/carrier proteins present are in use — saturated — so concentration is no longer the limiting factor.
🚩

Never explain the plateau as "loss of the concentration gradient" — the gradient hasn't gone anywhere. What's actually happened is that every available transport protein is occupied at once.

▶ Why can't a large molecule use facilitated diffusion?

It's too big, or the wrong shape, to fit or bind to the carrier or channel protein present — so no facilitated-diffusion route exists for it at all, regardless of how steep its concentration gradient is.

▶ 🚩 Trap — why two different molecules can sometimes share one channel

Two different molecules can pass through the same channel protein because they are both small, or similar in size, and have a similar shape — complementary to the channel. This is purely a size-and-shape question. Explaining channel-sharing using polar/non-polar or charge-based reasoning is the exact trap here — that language belongs to explaining why a substance can't cross the bilayer directly, not to explaining why two substances can share one channel.

Section 5 · spec content point (part 3 of 5 — movement across membranes)

💧 Osmosis and water potential

A special case of diffusion that's only ever about water — and the one topic on this spec point where a single wrong word ("concentration" instead of "potential") loses the mark outright.

📖

What the specification says: "Movement across membranes occurs by: simple diffusion (involving limitations imposed by the nature of the phospholipid bilayer); facilitated diffusion (involving the roles of carrier proteins and channel proteins); osmosis (explained in terms of water potential); active transport (involving the role of carrier proteins and the importance of the hydrolysis of ATP); co-transport (illustrated by the absorption of sodium ions and glucose by cells lining the mammalian ileum)." (Same content point as Sections 3–4 — this section covers osmosis.)

1
2
3

Think of "water potential" as a score for how much "free" water is in a solution — pure water scores the maximum, and the more stuff (sugar, salt) you dissolve into it, the lower the score gets. Water always wanders from a high score to a low score, through a membrane with tiny holes only water can fit through. So if you put a cell into really salty water, the water inside the cell has a higher score than the salty water outside, and water leaves the cell to try to even the scores out — the cell shrinks.

  • Water potential (Ψ) = the "free water" score of a solution
  • Selectively permeable = a membrane that lets water through but not the dissolved stuff

Osmosis is the movement of water from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential, through a selectively (partially) permeable membrane. Water potential is measured in kPa, symbol Ψ; pure water has the maximum possible value, Ψ = 0, and every solution has a negative value — the more concentrated the solution, the more negative its Ψ. If Ψ is equal on both sides, there's dynamic equilibrium and no net movement. To answer any "explain using water potential" question, use three steps: say which side has the lower (more negative) Ψ and why; say that water moves by osmosis from higher to lower Ψ; then state the consequence in the question's own context.

Osmosis is the passage of water from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential, through a selectively permeable membrane. Water potential (Ψ) is measured in kPa or MPa. Pure water at 25 °C and 100 kPa has Ψ = 0 — the maximum possible value. Dissolved solute always lowers Ψ, so every solution has a negative water potential, and the more concentrated the solution, the more negative Ψ becomes. Equal Ψ on both sides of a membrane means dynamic equilibrium — no net movement.

Water (small dots) crosses both ways through the membrane's pores, but net movement is towards the more concentrated side. The larger solute molecules (bigger circles) cannot cross — that's what makes the membrane "partially permeable" rather than freely permeable.

The universal three-step method

  1. Name which side has the lower / more negative Ψ, and why (more dissolved solute there).
  2. State that water moves from the higher to the lower Ψ, by osmosis.
  3. State the consequence, in the question's own words.

The plant cell survives where the animal cell doesn't because the cellulose wall is tough and inelastic — pressure builds up inside it and resists further water entry. The animal cell-surface membrane, at roughly 7 nm thick, simply can't stretch to cope, so it breaks.

▶ 🚩 The single most costly wording error on this spec point

"Water concentration" is never credited anywhere on this specification — always say water potential. This applies even in unfamiliar synoptic contexts, e.g. gill gas-exchange questions, where the correct language is actually about partial pressure of oxygen and diffusion, not water potential at all — don't substitute the wrong vocabulary just because both involve a gradient.

Section 6 · spec content point (part 4 of 5 — movement across membranes)

🔋 Active transport

The first process on this page that goes against the gradient — and the first that needs ATP to pay for it.

📖

What the specification says: "Movement across membranes occurs by: simple diffusion (involving limitations imposed by the nature of the phospholipid bilayer); facilitated diffusion (involving the roles of carrier proteins and channel proteins); osmosis (explained in terms of water potential); active transport (involving the role of carrier proteins and the importance of the hydrolysis of ATP); co-transport (illustrated by the absorption of sodium ions and glucose by cells lining the mammalian ileum)." (Same content point as Sections 3–5 — this section covers active transport.)

1
2
3

Diffusion is like rolling a ball downhill — it happens by itself. Active transport is like carrying that ball back up the hill — it takes real effort, because you're moving something from where there's less of it to where there's already more. That effort has to be paid for, and the "payment" is a tiny fuel packet called ATP. Only ferry-style proteins (carriers) can do this job — there's no tunnel version of active transport.

Active transport moves molecules or ions from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration — against the gradient — and it always needs both a carrier protein and ATP. The carrier binds the molecule on one side, ATP is hydrolysed to ADP and phosphate, and the energy released changes the protein's shape so it opens to the other side and releases the molecule. The protein then reverts to its original shape, ready to bind again. Unlike facilitated diffusion, active transport never uses channel proteins — only carriers.

Active transport is the movement of molecules or ions from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration — against the concentration gradient — using ATP and carrier proteins.

The mechanism, step by step

  1. A carrier protein spans the membrane and binds the molecule/ion on one side, at a binding site.
  2. ATP binds to the protein and is hydrolysed to ADP + inorganic phosphate (Pᵢ); the energy released changes the protein's shape, opening it to the other side.
  3. The molecule/ion is released on the other side.
  4. Pᵢ is released, and the protein reverts to its original shape; the Pᵢ recombines with ADP to reform ATP during respiration.
Point of contrastFacilitated diffusionActive transport
Protein usedChannel or carrierCarrier only
EnergyNo ATP — passiveUses ATP
DirectionDown a concentration gradientCan occur against a concentration gradient

Because "contrast" as a command word requires both sides of every difference to be stated explicitly, naming only the active-transport half of each row above would lose marks — always give both.

What a membrane-bound ATP hydrolase does

It hydrolyses ATP to ADP + Pᵢ, releasing energy that allows ions to be moved against a concentration gradient. Never write that ATP hydrolysis "produces" or "creates" energy — energy is released from ATP, not created from nothing.

Evidence that a substance is being actively transported

  • Temperature evidence: uptake is faster at a higher temperature, because faster respiration produces more ATP.
  • Saturation evidence: uptake plateaus once carrier proteins are all in use — concentration is no longer the limiting factor.
  • Respiratory-inhibitor evidence: with the inhibitor present, uptake is slower and plateaus at equilibrium — diffusion only, no active transport. Without it, uptake keeps rising past that point, because active transport continues working against the gradient and no equilibrium is ever reached.

Cells that carry out a lot of active transport, such as ileum epithelial cells, need many mitochondria — mitochondria are the site of aerobic respiration, which produces the ATP that active transport hydrolyses. Dead cells cannot take up substances by active transport at all: they can't respire, so no ATP is produced, and — depending on the scenario — the specific carrier protein with the right binding site may not even be present.

Section 7 · spec content point (part 5 of 5 — movement across membranes)

🧫 Co-transport: Na⁺/glucose absorption in the ileum

Examiners consistently flag this as the hardest single application skill on the AS specification: three transport processes, three different proteins, and two different membranes, all in the correct order.

📖

What the specification says: "Movement across membranes occurs by: simple diffusion (involving limitations imposed by the nature of the phospholipid bilayer); facilitated diffusion (involving the roles of carrier proteins and channel proteins); osmosis (explained in terms of water potential); active transport (involving the role of carrier proteins and the importance of the hydrolysis of ATP); co-transport (illustrated by the absorption of sodium ions and glucose by cells lining the mammalian ileum)." (Same content point as Sections 3–6 — this section covers co-transport.)

1
2
3

Imagine two people crossing a river holding hands — one of them (sodium) really wants to cross and is pulling hard, and that pull drags the other person (glucose) across too, even though glucose alone wouldn't have crossed. But somebody first has to keep making sure there are lots of people wanting to cross that river in the first place — that's a separate pump, working hard (using fuel) on the far bank, to keep sending sodium back so it builds up and wants to cross again.

  • Sodium–potassium pump = the "far bank" worker using fuel (ATP) to keep sodium wanting to cross
  • Co-transporter = the pair holding hands, crossing together
  • GLUT carrier = a separate ferry that lets glucose finish its journey into the blood

Co-transport is described as indirect active transport, because the actual ATP-spending step happens somewhere else in the chain, not at the co-transporter itself. First, the sodium–potassium pump actively pumps Na⁺ out of the epithelial cell into the blood, using ATP — this keeps the Na⁺ concentration inside the cell much lower than in the gut lumen. That steep gradient is then used: Na⁺ diffuses back into the cell through a co-transporter protein, and as it does, the co-transporter simultaneously drags glucose (or an amino acid) into the cell with it, against glucose's own gradient — no ATP is spent at this specific step. Finally, glucose leaves the cell into the blood through a separate carrier protein, by facilitated diffusion, down its own gradient.

Label each step active or passive before writing any prose

StepActive or passive?

⭐

It is the sodium ion gradient, not ATP directly, that powers glucose uptake — this is why co-transport is called indirect active transport. No ATP is hydrolysed at the co-transporter itself; the ATP was spent earlier, maintaining the Na⁺ gradient that the co-transporter then exploits.

▶ Why a respiratory inhibitor stops amino-acid (or glucose) absorption

Less or no ATP is produced, so there is less or no active transport of Na⁺ out of the epithelial cell. Because Na⁺ is not moved out, no concentration gradient exists for Na⁺ to diffuse back in — and without that gradient, the co-transporter has nothing to power glucose/amino acid entry with. Stating "no active transport of sodium ions" alone only earns partial credit — you must also link it explicitly to the loss of the Na⁺ gradient that absorption depends on.

▶ 🚩 Trap — collapsing three proteins into one

Never collapse the three proteins into a single generic "carrier protein." The sodium–potassium pump, the Na⁺/glucose co-transporter, and the GLUT facilitated-diffusion carrier are three distinct proteins doing three distinct jobs, on two different membranes. A closely related trap: active transport of one ion via a named carrier must still be explained through an ATP-driven conformational change in that specific protein — it can't be explained away using the electrochemical gradient of a second, co-moving ion, unless that really is how the question describes the mechanism.

How to tackle any co-transport question: label each of the three steps active or passive before writing a word of prose, then narrate in that order, naming the membrane (basal vs apical) and the specific protein at each step.

Section 8 · spec content point

📈 Adaptations for rapid transport

Only two levers exist for speeding up transport across a membrane — surface area, and protein number — and the ileum epithelial cell model answer uses both.

📖

What the specification says: "Cells may be adapted for rapid transport across their internal or external membranes by an increase in surface area of, or by an increase in the number of protein channels and carrier molecules in, their membranes."

1
2
3

Think of a shop wanting to serve customers faster. There are only two real options: open more checkout lanes (more transport proteins) or make the shop front bigger so more customers can even get near a checkout at once (more surface area). The ileum's cells lining your gut do both — they're wrinkled into thousands of tiny folds to make their surface huge, and they're packed with checkout-lane proteins for glucose and sodium.

A cell can only speed up transport across its membrane in two ways: by increasing its surface area (giving more membrane for the same rate-per-area to act across), or by increasing the number of channel or carrier proteins in that membrane (letting more molecules cross per second at the same concentration). The ileum epithelial cell is the model example — it folds its apical membrane into microvilli to maximise surface area, and is densely packed with the specific proteins needed for facilitated diffusion, active transport, and co-transport, plus enough mitochondria to keep supplying ATP for all of it.

The model answer: how an ileum epithelial cell is adapted for absorption

  • Microvilli — greatly increase surface area (the "brush border"; large surface-area-to-volume ratio).
  • Many mitochondria — produce the ATP needed for active transport (needs the idea of "lots," not just one).
  • Carrier proteins for active transport.
  • Channel and carrier proteins for facilitated diffusion.
  • Co-transporters for the co-transport (symport) of Na⁺ and glucose/amino acids.
  • Membrane-bound enzymes that digest disaccharides, producing glucose ready for absorption.
🚩

Never write "villi on epithelial cells" — villi are folds of the intestinal wall itself, while microvilli are folds of an individual cell's own membrane; confusing the two is a common and costly error. Also never write "energy produced" alone — it must specify ATP released via respiration.

How protein number affects permeability

  • More/increased channel protein (e.g. aquaporin) present → increased permeability to that substance.
  • Converse: fewer channel proteins → less movement of that substance → the concentration on either side changes accordingly.
  • Damaged or destroyed microvilli → reduced surface area (and/or fewer channel/carrier proteins) → less facilitated diffusion / active transport.
  • Active transport out of a cell keeps the internal concentration low, which steepens (or maintains) the gradient into the cell, so diffusion in stays fast.

Worked surface-area calculation

Surface area of one cylindrical microvillus = 2πrl + πr². With r = 0.05 μm and l = 1 μm, that's ≈ 0.32 μm² per microvillus. Multiplying by 1000 microvilli gives ≈ 321 μm² of surface — compared with a flat cell surface of about 7.85 μm², a ratio of roughly 40–41 : 1. A related idea: when transport rate is expressed per unit area, cubes of different total sizes give effectively the same rate — it's surface area alone, not any other factor, that explains the difference in total rate between them.

🧪 RP3 — water potential of plant tissue

Roughly half of all marks on this spec point sit inside RP3 and RP4, and most of those are technique marks, not biology recall — dilution arithmetic, blotting, percentage change, repeats, and interpolation are all worth drilling as carefully as the transport mechanisms themselves.

📖

What the specification says: "Required practical 3: Production of a dilution series of a solute to produce a calibration curve with which to identify the water potential of plant tissue."

1
2
3

You take identical little cylinders of potato or apple and drop each one into a slightly different strength of sugar solution. Some cylinders gain weight (they sucked up water), some lose weight (they gave water away). Wherever a cylinder's weight doesn't change at all, that solution must be a perfect match for how "watery" the plant's own cells are — and that tells you the plant's water potential.

  • Dilution series = a set of solutions made progressively weaker from one starting stock
  • Calibration curve = a graph you can use to look up an unknown value

Equal-sized cylinders of plant tissue are cut and placed one per tube into a serial dilution series made from a single stock solution. After weighing, soaking for a set time, blotting, and reweighing, the percentage change in mass is calculated for each concentration (not the raw change, because starting masses differ). Plotting concentration against percentage change in mass gives a line that crosses zero at the one concentration with the same water potential as the tissue — reading that concentration off a separate resource converts it into an actual water potential value.

Method outline

  • Cut equal-sized tissue cylinders/discs from a stock solution's serial dilution series — e.g. making 30 cm³ of 0.15 mol dm⁻³ from a 1.0 mol dm⁻³ stock means 4.5 cm³ of stock plus 25.5 cm³ of distilled water. The total volume must equal the stated volume — not just be a multiple or factor of it.
  • Weigh each cylinder, place one per tube, and cover the tubes (this stops evaporation changing the solution's water potential) for a set, equal time.
  • Remove and blot dry before reweighing — surface water would affect the reading, and the amount of surface water varies between cylinders.
  • Calculate percentage change in mass, not raw change — this allows fair comparison, since starting masses differ between cylinders.
  • Take repeats: this lets anomalies be identified or their effect reduced, and makes the mean/line of best fit more reliable (never say "more accurate").

Finding water potential from the results

  1. Plot concentration (the independent variable, x-axis) against percentage change in mass (y-axis); draw a line of best fit.
  2. Read off (interpolate) the concentration where the line crosses zero percentage change.
  3. Use a second resource (a table or graph) to convert that concentration into an actual water potential value.

A gain in mass means the solution's water potential is higher (less negative) than the tissue's, so the tissue gains water by osmosis — the reverse logic applies for a loss in mass.

▶ Practical design details worth remembering

Cut cylinders lengthways down a root so each one passes through the same proportion of tissue types. The initial length of each cylinder doesn't need to be identical, because results are expressed as a ratio/proportion. The graph can be improved with standard deviation or error bars (again, never "more accurate"). A higher temperature speeds up osmosis (more kinetic energy), giving a larger, more easily measurable mass difference in the time available. There are two valid design routes: mass/length change (record before/after, over a fixed time, removing surface water, and interpreting the direction of the change) or microscopy (observing turgor or plasmolysis directly, or scoring the percentage of cells plasmolysed) — don't mix marking points from the two different routes in one answer.

Section 10 · spec content point · required practical

🧫 RP4 — permeability of cell-surface membranes

The classic beetroot-pigment practical, usually run against temperature, ethanol concentration, or pH as the named variable — and a strong source of "explain why" and "evaluate the method" questions.

📖

What the specification says: "Required practical 4: Investigation into the effect of a named variable on the permeability of cell-surface membranes."

1
2
3

Beetroot cells are full of a purple pigment normally locked safely inside. If you damage the membrane keeping that pigment in — with heat, alcohol, or acid — the pigment leaks out into the surrounding water, turning it purple. The more damage, the more colour leaks out, and you can measure exactly how purple the water has become with a light-measuring machine (a colorimeter) instead of just eyeballing it.

Equal-sized beetroot cylinders are washed (to remove pigment released just from the cutting itself), then each placed into the same volume of solution while a named variable — temperature, ethanol concentration, or pH — is applied for a fixed time. Afterwards, a colorimeter measures how much pigment has leaked into each solution as a number (absorbance or % transmission), rather than relying on eye judgement, which is subjective. More pigment release means more membrane damage: proteins denature, or the phospholipid bilayer's fluidity/damage increases, letting the pigment escape.

Method outline

  • Cut equal-sized beetroot cylinders/discs with a cork borer, and wash them — cutting ruptures surface cells and releases pigment that would otherwise contaminate the result.
  • Place one piece per tube, with the same volume of solution in every tube — too much or too little water changes the pigment's concentration, making results incomparable.
  • Apply the named variable for a set, equal time; remove the tissue.
  • Measure the released pigment with a colorimeter (absorbance or % transmission) rather than estimating colour by eye, which is subjective.

Explaining pigment release

  • Damage to the cell-surface membrane; membrane proteins denature; increased fluidity/damage to the phospholipid bilayer.
  • Ethanol dissolves the phospholipid bilayer.
  • Acid alters membrane protein structure.
  • High temperature denatures membrane proteins and increases bilayer fluidity/permeability.
🚩

Attributing increased dye release to "water potential" changes, rather than to membrane damage, protein denaturation, or increased bilayer fluidity, is a common wrong answer here — water potential isn't the mechanism this practical is testing.

▶ Method and evaluation details worth remembering

A thermometer alone does not "monitor" temperature — that requires repeated readings over time, with a corrective measure taken if it has fluctuated. Draw the results curve either as a point-to-point line or a smooth S-shaped best-fit line, and never extrapolate beyond the plotted range. Resolution can be improved with an instrument with finer graduations (give a specific numerical value, e.g. <2 cm³). Variables often left uncontrolled include cylinder length/diameter/surface area/mass, and time spent in solution. Plasmolysis is itself evidence that a membrane is selectively/partially permeable — permeable to water but not to the solute — which is why the gap between a shrunken protoplast and the (fully permeable) cell wall contains external solution.

Section 12 · exam technique

✍️ Using 3.2.3 in a longer written answer

This is one of the most reusable spec points in the whole course for a 25-mark essay: membrane structure opens the paragraph, the five transport processes give a natural sub-structure, and the ileum's adaptations plus RP3/RP4 supply concrete, markable detail rather than vague description.

▶ Essay titles this content underpins

"The structure and function of cell membranes"; "The importance of transport across membranes to living organisms"; "Communication and homeostasis" (the osmoregulation strand). Whenever a paragraph elsewhere needs a substance to cross a membrane — glucose into a cell, water into a root hair, a hormone binding a receptor, oxygen into an alveolar capillary — name the specific process (facilitated diffusion via a named carrier, osmosis down a Ψ gradient, active transport using ATP) rather than leaving the movement implicit.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around structure-to-function

The fluid-mosaic model explains how a single membrane can be selectively permeable: small, non-polar molecules cross the phospholipid bilayer directly by simple diffusion, while everything else depends on the specific channel or carrier proteins present. This shows the general biological principle that structure determines function even at the molecular level — a membrane's transport capability is entirely set by which proteins are embedded in it, which is exactly why cells adapted for rapid transport, such as ileum epithelial cells, increase both their surface area and their protein density rather than relying on diffusion alone.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around co-transport as a synoptic link

Co-transport in the ileum shows that active transport doesn't always mean ATP is spent at the exact point a substance crosses a membrane: the sodium–potassium pump spends ATP maintaining a Na⁺ gradient, and it is that stored gradient, not further ATP, that drags glucose into the cell against its own gradient. The same indirect-active-transport logic reappears in the kidney nephron during selective reabsorption, showing how one transport mechanism, evolved once, gets reused across entirely different organs.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Why membranes all match

  • Plasma membrane = general term; cell-surface membrane = specifically the one around the whole cell.
  • Mitochondria and chloroplasts each have two membranes.
  • Evidence a bilayer is the cell-surface membrane: a cell wall/cellulose sits outside it.
  • Organelle suspensions need matching Ψ so osmosis doesn't burst/shrivel the organelle — never "cell bursts" or "turgid/flaccid" for an organelle.

2 · The fluid-mosaic model

  • Bilayer: hydrophilic heads out (to water), hydrophobic tails in.
  • Protein = channel (pore) or carrier (binds, changes shape) — "fluid" = they can move; "mosaic" = scattered pattern.
  • Cholesterol restricts movement/fluidity — never just "strength."
  • Glycoprotein/glycolipid = recognition sites, outer surface only.

3 · Simple diffusion

  • Net movement, high → low concentration, passive (kinetic energy, no ATP).
  • Only small, non-polar, lipid-soluble molecules (O₂, CO₂) — straight-line uptake graph.
  • Rate ∝ concentration difference (Fick's law); rises with temperature.

4 · Facilitated diffusion

  • Channel (pore, ion-specific) or carrier (binds, changes shape) — both passive, down gradient, no ATP.
  • Binding site, never "active site."
  • Plateau = proteins saturated, never "loss of gradient."
  • Shared channel = size/shape match, not charge.

5 · Osmosis & water potential

  • Water moves high Ψ → low Ψ through a partially permeable membrane; pure water Ψ = 0.
  • Three-step answer: which side is lower Ψ and why → water moves by osmosis → state the consequence.
  • Animal cell in dilute solution bursts (no wall); plant cell goes turgid (wall resists).
  • Never "water concentration" — always "water potential."

6 · Active transport

  • Low → high concentration, against the gradient, carrier only, uses ATP.
  • Carrier binds → ATP hydrolysed to ADP + Pᵢ → shape change → release → protein resets.
  • Evidence: faster with heat/more ATP; plateaus when saturated; inhibitor blocks it (diffusion only remains).

7 · Co-transport in the ileum

  • Na⁺/K⁺ pump actively pumps Na⁺ out (basal membrane) → low Na⁺ inside cell.
  • Na⁺ diffuses back in via co-transporter (apical membrane), dragging glucose in against its gradient.
  • Glucose leaves into blood via GLUT carrier — facilitated diffusion (basal membrane).
  • Indirect active transport: sodium's gradient powers it, not ATP directly at that step.

8 · Adaptations for rapid transport

  • Only two levers: more surface area, or more channel/carrier proteins.
  • Ileum cell: microvilli, many mitochondria, AT/FD/co-transport proteins, membrane-bound digestive enzymes.
  • Microvilli ≠ villi — don't confuse cell folds with gut-wall folds.

9 · RP3 — water potential of plant tissue

  • Serial dilution → equal cylinders → weigh, blot, reweigh → % change in mass (not raw change).
  • Plot concentration vs % change; interpolate where it crosses zero; convert using a second resource.
  • Cover tubes (stop evaporation); repeats improve reliability, never "accuracy."

10 · RP4 — membrane permeability

  • Wash beetroot cylinders first (removes cutting-damage pigment); same volume in every tube.
  • Colorimeter measures pigment release — not eye judgement.
  • Release = membrane damage/protein denaturation/increased bilayer fluidity, never "water potential."

Proteins, not gradients, run out. Every plateau and every permeability difference comes down to protein number. Sodium pays, glucose rides.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

1 · Why membranes all match

❓ A student is shown two phospholipid bilayer diagrams and told one is the cell-surface membrane. State the evidence that lets them decide.
A cell wall, or cellulose, is present on the outside of that membrane — only the cell-surface membrane can have a wall built directly against it.
❓ Explain why an isolated chloroplast suspension must be kept at the same water potential as the chloroplasts themselves.
If the water potentials are matched, no net osmosis occurs across the chloroplast membrane, so the chloroplast does not burst, lyse, shrivel, or shrink; if the water potentials were unequal, osmosis would occur.
❓ Give two ways the distribution of membranes differs between a eukaryotic and a prokaryotic cell.
A eukaryotic cell has membrane-bound organelles and internal membranes (e.g. nucleus, mitochondria, ER, Golgi); a prokaryotic cell has no membrane-bound organelles and no nuclear envelope, with membrane present only as the cell-surface membrane.

2 · The fluid-mosaic model

❓ Explain the arrangement of phospholipids in a cell-surface membrane.
They form a bilayer, because water is present on both the inside and outside of the cell; the hydrophobic fatty-acid tails point away from / are repelled by water, while the hydrophilic phosphate heads point towards / are attracted to water.
❓ Explain the function of cholesterol in a cell membrane.
Cholesterol restricts the movement of molecules/phospholipids/fatty-acid tails making up the membrane, reducing lateral movement; it makes the membrane less fluid at high temperature and prevents leakage of water and dissolved ions.
❓ State one similarity and two differences between an older membrane model and the fluid-mosaic model.
Similarity: both have a phospholipid bilayer with hydrophobic tails facing each other and hydrophilic heads facing outwards, and both contain protein. Differences (any two): the older model has no channel/carrier proteins (or shows protein as a continuous outer layer rather than scattered through the bilayer); no cholesterol; no glycoprotein; no glycolipid.

3 · Simple diffusion

❓ Use Fick's law to explain why a simple-diffusion uptake graph is a straight line rather than a curve.
Rate of diffusion is proportional to (surface area × difference in concentration) ÷ thickness of the exchange surface; with surface area and thickness held constant, rate is directly proportional to the concentration difference, giving a straight-line relationship that does not level off.
❓ Give two similarities between diffusion and osmosis.
Both involve movement down a gradient, from a region of high concentration/water potential to low; both are passive processes that do not use energy from respiration, ATP, or metabolism.
❓ Explain the effect of increasing temperature on the rate of simple diffusion.
Rate increases, because particles gain more kinetic energy and move faster and more randomly, so the net movement down the concentration gradient occurs more quickly.

4 · Facilitated diffusion

❓ A graph of glucose uptake by facilitated diffusion rises steeply then plateaus. Explain this shape.
Movement occurs through carrier proteins (facilitated diffusion); on the rising section, rate of uptake is proportional to the external concentration; at the plateau, all the carrier proteins present are in use/saturated, so concentration is no longer the limiting factor.
❓ Explain why a large molecule cannot be absorbed by facilitated diffusion.
The molecule is too big, or the wrong shape, to fit or bind to the carrier or channel protein present, so it cannot pass through the membrane by this route.
❓ Suggest why two different ions can sometimes pass through the same channel protein.
They are both small, or similar in size, and have a similar shape that is complementary to the channel — not because of any similarity in charge or polarity.

5 · Osmosis & water potential

❓ A patient is prescribed lactulose. Use your knowledge of water potential to explain how it relieves constipation.
Lactulose lowers the water potential of the faeces/intestinal contents; water is therefore retained in, or enters, the gut contents by osmosis, softening the faeces.
❓ Explain what happens to a plant cell placed in a solution with a lower (more negative) water potential than the cell.
Water leaves the cell by osmosis, down the water potential gradient; the protoplast pulls away from the cellulose cell wall, and the cell becomes plasmolysed.
❓ A student is given mass data showing a plant tissue sample gained mass in a solution. Justify a conclusion about the water potentials involved.
Mass increased, so water has moved into the tissue by osmosis; this means the solution's water potential was higher (less negative) than the tissue's water potential.

6 · Active transport

❓ Describe the mechanism of active transport of an ion into a cell.
A carrier protein spans the membrane and binds the ion on one side, at a binding site; ATP binds and is hydrolysed to ADP and inorganic phosphate, and the energy released changes the protein's shape so it opens to the other side; the ion is released; phosphate is released and the protein reverts to its original shape, ready to bind again.
❓ Contrast facilitated diffusion with active transport.
Facilitated diffusion uses channel or carrier proteins, whereas active transport uses carrier proteins only; facilitated diffusion does not use ATP and is passive, whereas active transport uses ATP; facilitated diffusion occurs down a concentration gradient, whereas active transport can occur against one.
❓ Data show that ion uptake by a root-hair cell is much slower when a respiratory inhibitor is added. Explain this observation.
The inhibitor reduces or stops aerobic respiration, so less or no ATP is produced; with less ATP available for hydrolysis, less active transport of the ion into the cell can occur, so uptake is slower and eventually plateaus once diffusion alone reaches equilibrium.

7 · Co-transport in the ileum

❓ Describe the mechanism by which glucose is absorbed into an ileum epithelial cell alongside sodium ions.
Sodium ions are actively transported out of the epithelial cell into the blood by the sodium–potassium pump on the basal membrane, using ATP; this maintains a much lower Na⁺ concentration inside the cell than in the gut lumen; Na⁺ then diffuses back into the cell down this gradient, across the apical membrane, through a co-transporter protein, which simultaneously carries glucose into the cell against its own concentration gradient; glucose then passes out of the cell into the blood by facilitated diffusion, through a separate carrier protein on the basal membrane, down its own gradient.
❓ Explain why co-transport is described as "indirect" active transport.
No ATP is hydrolysed at the co-transporter protein itself; instead, it is the sodium ion concentration gradient — generated earlier by the ATP-driven sodium–potassium pump — that powers glucose movement into the cell against its own gradient.
❓ A respiratory inhibitor is added to ileum tissue. Explain why amino acid absorption stops.
Little or no ATP is produced, so the sodium–potassium pump cannot actively transport sodium ions out of the epithelial cell; without sodium ions being removed, no concentration gradient exists for sodium to diffuse back into the cell, so the co-transporter has no gradient available to bring the amino acid in with it.

8 · Adaptations for rapid transport

❓ Explain how a cell lining the ileum is adapted for the rapid absorption of digestion products.
Microvilli greatly increase surface area; many mitochondria produce ATP for active transport; carrier proteins enable active transport; channel and carrier proteins enable facilitated diffusion; co-transporter proteins allow the co-transport of sodium ions with glucose or amino acids; membrane-bound enzymes digest disaccharides, producing monosaccharides ready for absorption.
❓ Explain why an increase in the number of aquaporin channel proteins in a membrane increases the rate of water movement across it.
More channel protein is present, so more water-filled pores are available for water to move through at the same time, increasing the permeability of the membrane to water.
❓ Microvilli on ileum epithelial cells are damaged by a bacterial infection. Explain the effect on absorption.
Damage to the microvilli reduces the surface area of the apical membrane, and may reduce the number of channel/carrier proteins present, so less facilitated diffusion and active transport of digestion products can occur.

9 · RP3 — water potential of plant tissue

❓ Describe how you would prepare 20 cm³ of a 0.2 mol dm⁻³ solution from a 1.0 mol dm⁻³ stock solution.
Add 4 cm³ of the stock solution to 16 cm³ of distilled water, using a graduated pipette or syringe, to give a total volume of 20 cm³.
❓ Explain why percentage change in mass, rather than the raw change in mass, is calculated for each tissue cylinder.
Calculating percentage change allows a fair comparison between cylinders, because the cylinders had different starting masses.
❓ Describe how the results of this investigation are used to determine the water potential of the plant tissue.
Plot concentration of the bathing solution (independent variable) against percentage change in mass, and draw a line of best fit; find the concentration at which the line crosses zero percentage change by interpolation; use a second resource, such as a calibration graph or table, to convert that concentration into a water potential value.

10 · RP4 — membrane permeability

❓ Explain why beetroot cylinders are washed before being placed into the test solutions.
Cutting the cylinders ruptures cells at the cut surface and releases pigment; washing removes this pigment so it does not contaminate the results of the actual investigation.
❓ A student estimates pigment release by comparing tube colours by eye. Suggest an improvement to this method.
Use a colorimeter to measure absorbance (or percentage transmission) of each solution, since this gives a quantitative reading and removes the subjectivity of judging colour by eye.
❓ Explain why increasing temperature increases the release of pigment from beetroot cells.
High temperature denatures membrane proteins and increases the fluidity of, or causes damage to, the phospholipid bilayer, increasing the permeability of the cell-surface membrane and allowing more pigment to leak out.
