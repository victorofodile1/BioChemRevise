Recombinant DNA Technology

How a gene is cut out, copied, and inserted into another organism so it works there too — the small enzyme toolkit (restriction endonuclease, DNA ligase, reverse transcriptase, Taq polymerase), the polymerase chain reaction, and the full in vivo cloning pipeline from plasmid to marker gene.

⚠️

The single rule that costs the most marks on this spec point: the enzyme names and their exact jobs are marked with zero tolerance for swapping them. Restriction endonuclease cuts DNA — never "cuts the gene" alone, it cuts the plasmid or the DNA either side of the gene. DNA ligase joins fragments by forming phosphodiester bonds. Reverse transcriptase makes cDNA from mRNA. Taq polymerase — never just "DNA polymerase" — extends new strands in PCR because it is heat-tolerant. Students very often substitute DNA helicase, ordinary DNA polymerase or reverse transcriptase for restriction endonuclease, so before every answer, check which of the four enzymes the step actually needs.

Section 1 · spec content point

🧬 Why a gene from one species can work in another

Before any cutting, copying or inserting happens, there's one idea underneath the whole technology — and it's worth exactly as many marks as any mechanism you'll meet later in this spec point.

📖

What the specification says: "Recombinant DNA technology involves the transfer of fragments of DNA from one organism, or species, to another. Since the genetic code is universal, as are transcription and translation mechanisms, the transferred DNA can be translated within cells of the recipient (transgenic) organism."

1
2
3

Every living thing on Earth writes its instructions in the exact same code, and every living cell reads that code in the exact same way — like every country in the world agreeing to write recipes using identical words and identical kitchen steps. That means you can literally rip a page out of one organism's recipe book, glue it into a completely different organism's book, and that organism can still follow the recipe and cook the dish. An organism carrying a page from a different species' recipe book has a special name: transgenic.

  • Genetic code = the "alphabet" every organism's DNA is written in
  • Transgenic organism = one carrying DNA transferred in from another species
  • Transcription & translation = the two "reading" steps every cell uses to turn DNA into protein

Recombinant DNA technology means physically transferring a DNA fragment from one organism, or one species, into another. Two separate facts make it possible for the recipient cell to actually use that DNA once it's inside: the genetic code is universal — the same three-base codon always codes for the same amino acid, in every organism — and the machinery of transcription and translation is universal too, so the recipient's own RNA polymerase, ribosomes and tRNA can read the new DNA exactly as if it were their own. An organism that now contains DNA transferred in from another species is called transgenic — a precise term, not just a casual way of saying "genetically modified."

Two ideas are required together — not either one alone

  • The genetic code is universal: the same base triplet (codon) codes for the same amino acid in essentially every organism on Earth.
  • The mechanisms of transcription and translation are also universal: the recipient cell's own RNA polymerase, ribosomes and tRNAs work the same way regardless of which organism the DNA originally came from.
  • Because both of these are true together, the recipient (host) cell's normal machinery can transcribe and translate the transferred DNA to produce a functional, correctly-folded protein — exactly as it would with its own genes.
  • An organism that contains DNA transferred in from another species is described precisely as transgenic — this exact term is expected, not a paraphrase such as "genetically modified" used on its own.
📌

Why both ideas are needed: a universal code on its own only guarantees the base sequence would specify the right amino acids — it says nothing about whether the recipient cell can actually carry out transcription and translation on that DNA at all. You need both: the code must match, and the reading machinery must be shared.

▶ 🚩 The half-answer trap

Giving only "the genetic code is universal" scores half of the reasoning available. Full credit needs the universality of transcription and translation stated as well — write both sentences, every time this question comes up, however it's worded.

Section 2 · spec content point

✂️ Three ways to obtain a DNA fragment

Before a gene can be transferred anywhere, you first need a clean copy of just that gene, isolated from everything else. AQA gives three genuinely different routes to the same starting material — know one clear advantage of each.

📖

What the specification says: "Fragments of DNA can be produced by several methods, including: conversion of mRNA to complementary DNA (cDNA), using reverse transcriptase; using restriction enzymes to cut a fragment containing the desired gene from DNA; creating the gene in a 'gene machine'."

1
2
3

Imagine you need one specific paragraph of text. You could: photocopy it starting from a "clean" already-edited version with all the typos and extra notes already removed (reverse transcriptase making cDNA); use a pair of scissors to cut that exact paragraph straight out of the original messy document (a restriction enzyme cutting DNA); or just type the whole paragraph out fresh yourself, word for word (a gene machine building it from scratch).

  • cDNA = a clean DNA copy made from a message (mRNA), with no "junk" left in it
  • Restriction endonuclease = a "molecular scissors" enzyme
  • Sticky ends = short overhanging ends left after a staggered cut, which can grab onto matching ends
  • Gene machine = a computer-controlled way of building DNA from scratch

There are three genuinely different starting points. Reverse transcriptase takes an mRNA molecule — copied from a cell that makes lots of the protein you want — and builds a complementary DNA strand from it, called cDNA; because mRNA has already had its introns spliced out, this cDNA is automatically intron-free. Restriction endonucleases instead go straight to the organism's DNA and cut out the region containing the gene, recognising one exact short sequence and cutting a staggered line across both strands, which leaves single-stranded "sticky end" overhangs. A gene machine skips biological material altogether — the base sequence is worked out and the gene is built artificially, nucleotide by nucleotide, in short overlapping pieces that are then joined together.

Why cDNA, not the genomic gene, is used to make a human protein in bacteria

Eukaryotic mRNA has already been spliced to remove introns, so cDNA reverse-transcribed from it is intron-free — essential, because a prokaryote cannot splice pre-mRNA and so could not correctly transcribe and translate a gene copied straight from human genomic DNA.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "cuts the gene"

Never say a restriction enzyme "cuts the gene" on its own — describe it as cutting the DNA either side of the gene (or, once the fragment is already excised, producing sticky ends on it). This exact phrasing distinction is explicitly rejected on mark schemes, and it matters even more once you reach inserting the gene into a vector in Section 4.

Section 3 · spec content point

🔁 Amplifying DNA in vitro — the principles of PCR

Once you have one copy of a DNA fragment, you usually need billions of copies before you can do anything useful with it. PCR is the outside-the-cell route to get there fast.

📖

What the specification says: "Fragments of DNA can be amplified by in vitro and in vivo techniques. The principles of the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) as an in vitro method to amplify DNA fragments."

1
2
3

PCR is like a photocopier for DNA that runs on heat instead of light. First it gets really hot, so the two strands of DNA peel apart, like unzipping a zip. Then it cools down a bit, so short "starter" tags can stick onto each strand, marking where copying should begin. Then it warms up again, just enough for a special heat-proof copying enzyme to build a brand new matching strand next to each old one. Repeat that whole cycle over and over, and the pile of DNA doubles every single time — so it grows enormously fast.

  • Denaturation = the "unzipping" heating step
  • Annealing = the "starter tags stick on" cooling step
  • Extension = the "build a new strand" warming step
  • Exponential = doubling each round, so the total explodes upward very quickly

PCR (the polymerase chain reaction) copies DNA outside a living cell, using a machine that cycles through three temperatures. First, heating to around 95°C breaks the hydrogen bonds holding the two DNA strands together, separating them. Second, cooling to around 50–65°C lets short DNA primers bind to the single strands by complementary base pairing, marking the start point for copying. Third, warming to around 72°C lets Taq polymerase — a heat-stable enzyme — build a new strand from each primer using free nucleotides. This three-step cycle is repeated 25–35 times, and because the amount of DNA doubles every single cycle, the increase is exponential rather than steady.

The three-step PCR cycle

  1. Denaturation (≈95°C) — the DNA is heated, breaking the hydrogen bonds between the two strands and separating the DNA into two single strands.
  2. Annealing (≈50–65°C, often ≈55°C) — the mixture is cooled, so short DNA primers, complementary to the sequences flanking the target region, bind to each single strand by complementary base pairing; the primers provide the free 3′ end that a polymerase needs to start building from.
  3. Extension (≈72°C) — Taq polymerase, a heat-stable DNA polymerase, joins free DNA nucleotides to each template strand by complementary base pairing, forming new phosphodiester bonds and extending a new strand from each primer.

This cycle is repeated (typically 25–35 times); the quantity of target DNA doubles with every cycle, so amplification is exponential (2ⁿ), not linear — after 30 cycles, roughly 10⁹ copies exist from a single starting fragment.

Denaturation breaks hydrogen bonds between strands only — never the covalent phosphodiester bonds within a strand. Repeated cycling doubles the DNA quantity each time, giving exponential, not linear, growth.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "DNA polymerase" alone, and vague primer wording

Do not write "DNA polymerase" alone for the extension step — the heat-stable enzyme used is specifically Taq polymerase. An ordinary DNA polymerase would be denatured by the repeated heating to 95°C used every cycle, so a naturally heat-tolerant enzyme (originally from a bacterium living in hot springs) is required instead. Separately, describing primer specificity as "complementary to different named diseases" instead of "complementary to a specific target base sequence" loses the mark — omitting either "base" or "sequence" is enough to lose it.

Section 4 · spec content point

🔗 Building a recombinant plasmid

The in vivo route works differently to PCR: instead of copying the fragment directly, the fragment is first stitched into a vector, which a living host cell will then copy for you as it divides.

📖

What the specification says: "The culture of transformed host cells as an in vivo method to amplify DNA fragments. The addition of promoter and terminator regions to the fragments of DNA. The use of restriction endonucleases and ligases to insert fragments of DNA into vectors."

1
2
3

Picture a jigsaw piece (the gene) and a puzzle board with a gap in it (the plasmid). If you cut both of them out using the exact same shaped cutter, the edges will match perfectly and slot together. Once they're pushed together, a dab of glue (DNA ligase) seals the join permanently. But the piece also needs an "on" switch at the start (a promoter) and a "stop here" sign at the end (a terminator), or the cell won't know to actually read it.

  • Vector = the carrier that takes the gene into a new cell (here, a plasmid — a small circle of DNA)
  • Ligase = the "glue" enzyme that seals DNA pieces together
  • Promoter = the "start reading here" switch
  • Terminator = the "stop reading here" sign

A recombinant plasmid is built by cutting both the desired gene fragment and a plasmid (a small circular loop of bacterial DNA, used as a vector) with the same restriction endonuclease. Because it's the same enzyme, both pieces end up with identical, complementary sticky ends, so they can line up and base-pair with each other. DNA ligase then seals the join, creating new phosphodiester bonds and producing one continuous circular recombinant plasmid — DNA from two different sources joined into one molecule. A promoter region is also added before the gene, since this is where RNA polymerase and transcription factors bind and without it the gene can't be transcribed at all; a terminator region is added after it, telling RNA polymerase where to stop, so only the gene itself gets transcribed rather than reading on into the rest of the plasmid.

Cutting the gene and the plasmid with the same enzyme

  • A restriction endonuclease cuts the plasmid open; the same enzyme was used to cut the gene fragment out of its donor DNA, so the sticky ends produced on each are complementary to one another.
  • Using a different enzyme would recognise a different sequence and leave sticky ends that cannot base-pair — the gene simply could not be inserted.
  • The complementary sticky ends anneal (base-pair); DNA ligase then joins them, catalysing the formation of new phosphodiester bonds along the sugar-phosphate backbone and sealing the plasmid into one continuous circle — a recombinant plasmid.
The gene fragment and the plasmid are cut with the same restriction endonuclease, leaving identical complementary sticky ends. The ends anneal by base pairing, and DNA ligase seals the backbone — joining DNA from two different sources into one recombinant plasmid.

The role of the promoter and terminator regions

RegionRole
PromoterThe region where RNA polymerase and transcription factors bind — without it, the gene is not transcribed at all.
TerminatorSignals RNA polymerase to detach, so that transcription stops after the desired gene — meaning surrounding plasmid DNA is not transcribed along with it.
▶ 🚩 The costliest phrasing trap on this spec point

"Restriction enzymes cut the gene" is explicitly rejected phrasing on mark schemes — they cut the plasmid, or the DNA either side of the gene, never the gene itself. Practise writing every restriction-enzyme sentence with the plasmid, or the flanking DNA, as the grammatical object of "cut" — never the gene.

Section 5 · spec content point

🦠 Transformation and marker genes

A recombinant plasmid is useless sitting in a test tube — it has to get inside a living host cell, and because that step is very inefficient, scientists need a reliable way to find the rare cells where it actually worked.

📖

What the specification says: "Transformation of host cells using these vectors. The use of marker genes to detect genetically modified (GM) cells or organisms. (Students will not be required to recall specific marker genes in a written paper.)"

1
2
3

To get the ring of DNA (the plasmid) inside a bacterium, scientists give the cells a chemical and a quick shock, which makes their outer wall temporarily "leaky" so the plasmid can slip in — like briefly unlocking a door. Only a tiny fraction of cells actually let a plasmid in, though. So to find the lucky few, everyone gets grown on a food source laced with a poison. Only the cells that got the plasmid also got a hidden "antidote" tag riding along with it, so only they survive — everyone else dies. Whoever's still alive at the end must have taken up the plasmid.

  • Transformation = a cell taking up new DNA from its surroundings
  • Marker gene = the "antidote tag" riding on the plasmid, used to spot the successes
  • Antibiotic resistance = one common type of marker gene

Recombinant plasmids are mixed with host cells (often bacteria) in a solution containing calcium ions, then given a heat shock or an electric current — this increases the permeability of the cell-surface membrane, allowing plasmids to enter. This process, called transformation, is very inefficient: typically only around 1–5% of cells actually take up a plasmid. To find those rare successes among the many failures, plasmids also carry a marker gene, commonly one that confers resistance to a particular antibiotic. Growing all the cells on a medium containing that antibiotic means only cells carrying the plasmid survive and grow; every other cell dies. Survival is therefore evidence that transformation worked — the marker gene itself doesn't do anything to the gene of interest, it's purely a way to tell the two groups of cells apart.

Transformation

  • Recombinant plasmids are mixed with host cells (usually bacteria); calcium ions plus a heat shock (or, alternatively, an electric current) increase the permeability of the host cell-surface membrane, allowing the plasmid to enter.
  • Transformation efficiency is low — typically only around 1–5% of cells take up a plasmid — which is exactly why a marker gene is needed to find the successes among the failures.

How a marker gene identifies transformed cells

  • A marker gene carried on the plasmid (commonly conferring antibiotic resistance, though a fluorescent gene or an enzyme colour-change gene are equally acceptable named examples) shows that a cell has taken up the recombinant plasmid.
  • Host cells are grown on a medium containing that antibiotic: only cells carrying the plasmid (and so the marker gene) survive and grow; non-transformed cells die.
  • Survival is evidence of successful transformation — the marker gene itself does nothing to the gene of interest; it is a detection and selection tool only.
  • The specification explicitly does not require recall of any specific named marker gene — only the underlying principle needs to be described.
Transformation is inefficient, so only a small fraction of host cells take up the recombinant plasmid. Growing every cell on a medium containing the marker gene's antibiotic kills non-transformed cells — survival identifies the successes.
📌

The marker gene is a selection tool, not a switch. It does nothing to the gene of interest itself — its only job is to let scientists tell transformed and non-transformed cells apart. Cells that survive on the antibiotic medium must carry the plasmid, and therefore, on it, the gene of interest too.

🔎

Worth knowing: a transgenic organism can still fail to pass the inserted gene on to its offspring — this can happen when gene insertion occurs after cell division has already begun, so the cells that go on to produce gametes never receive a copy of the inserted gene.

Section 6 · exam technique

🎯 Common mistakes & precise wording

This spec point is hard less because the biology is conceptually difficult, and more because five enzymes each have one precise job that's very easy to swap for a similar-sounding wrong one. These are the slips that come up again and again.

Vague or wrong versionWhat to write instead
"Restriction enzymes cut the gene"They cut the plasmid, or the DNA either side of the gene — never the gene itself
"DNA polymerase" for the PCR extension stepName Taq polymerase specifically — it is heat-stable, unlike ordinary DNA polymerase
"Ligase joins DNA together" (as a complete answer)Name the bond formed — DNA ligase forms phosphodiester bonds between the vector and the fragment
Reverse transcriptase described as making "RNA from DNA"It's the reverse: it makes DNA (cDNA) from an mRNA template
Substituting DNA helicase, DNA polymerase or reverse transcriptase for restriction endonucleaseOnly a restriction endonuclease cuts DNA at a specific recognition site to produce sticky ends
Primer specificity as "complementary to different named diseases""Complementary to a specific target base sequence" — keep both "base" and "sequence"

▶ Straight from examiner reports

Restriction endonuclease action is generally well known — cutting DNA at a specific recognition site, producing sticky ends — but naming the correct enzyme is a common error, with DNA helicase, DNA polymerase and reverse transcriptase all frequently substituted in its place. DNA ligase's role is often described too vaguely ("joins DNA together," without naming the phosphodiester bond or the vector/plasmid context). Reverse transcriptase is frequently confused with other enzymes — some answers describe it as making "RNA from DNA," the reverse of its true action, or muddle it with DNA polymerase, RNA polymerase, restriction endonucleases or DNA helicase.

▶ Where this point sits in difficulty terms

This is A-level-only content, dense with named enzymes and two full multi-step pipelines sitting side by side — PCR's three-temperature cycle, and the restriction–ligase–transformation–marker-gene sequence — each vulnerable to enzyme-name substitution errors. Memorisation load is high (five separate enzymes/tools, each with one precise job), application depth is medium (drug and data-interpretation style questions do appear), and terminology/wording precision risk is high throughout. The single hardest transferable skill is resisting the pull to write a plausible-sounding but wrong enzyme name: before every answer, check whether the step is cutting DNA at a specific site (restriction endonuclease), joining fragments together (DNA ligase), copying mRNA into DNA (reverse transcriptase), or extending a new strand from a primer during PCR (Taq polymerase).

⚖️ Evaluating GM technology & gene therapy

Once the mechanism is secure, AQA expects it applied to real-world judgement: interpreting unfamiliar data, weighing benefits against concerns sector by sector, and relating recombinant DNA technology to gene therapy specifically.

⚠️

The one-sided-answer trap: AQA's own instruction is to balance the humanitarian case for the technology against organised opposition from environmentalists and anti-globalisation activists. A one-sided answer, however long, is capped — always structure the answer with points on both sides, finishing with a justified conclusion.

Relating recombinant DNA technology to gene therapy

A functioning allele is inserted into a patient's cells to supplement or replace a faulty one, using the same enzyme toolkit — restriction enzymes, ligase and a vector, often a modified virus or a liposome.

TypeWhat's alteredKey consequence
Somatic gene therapyBody cells onlyNot inherited; must be repeated, since the correction dies with the treated cells
Germ-line gene therapyGametes or an embryoInherited by future generations; currently illegal in humans in many countries

Conditions caused by a single recessive allele are the best target for gene therapy, because a single working copy inserted is often sufficient to restore function.

▶ Evaluating a gene therapy trial, or a GM organism trial

As with other extended-evaluation questions across this topic, there is no mark for a bare "yes" or "no" — marks come from the supporting biological argument, typically weighing quality of life without treatment against the risk of the condition or procedure recurring or causing harm. Judging whether a GM organism trial's results are convincing draws on both statistical criteria (non-overlapping standard deviations, a clearly named significant variable) and experimental-design criteria (sample size, duration, and the presence of a control).

▶ Example essay paragraph — DNA as an information-carrying molecule

"Because the genetic code and mechanisms of transcription and translation are universal, DNA transferred between species — producing a transgenic organism — can still be expressed. DNA fragments can be produced using reverse transcriptase (cDNA from mRNA), restriction endonucleases (producing sticky ends) or a gene machine, then amplified by PCR in vitro, or inserted into a vector and cloned in host cells in vivo. This is the direct practical application of DNA as an information-carrying molecule: genetic information can be physically cut, copied and transferred between organisms."

Essay technique: this paragraph does two jobs — it summarises the whole spec point in three sentences, then explicitly links back to the essay's title in the final sentence. A strong extension is to name real end-products once the mechanism is established: human insulin, factor VIII and vaccine antigens produced free from pathogen contamination, GM crops with pest, disease and drought resistance, and gene therapy inserting a functioning allele to treat genetic disease.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around enzyme specificity

Restriction endonucleases recognise one specific, short palindromic recognition sequence, and DNA ligase catalyses the formation of phosphodiester bonds regardless of which two DNA fragments it's joining. This shows how the same small set of highly specific enzymes can be applied to any pair of compatible DNA molecules — the specificity lies in the recognition sequence and the sticky ends it produces, not in which organisms the DNA originally came from.

Section 9

🧠 Memory hook, synoptic links & final checklist

🧠

Memory hook — cut, copy, join, grow, check, make. Restriction endonuclease cuts at a specific site; Taq polymerase (PCR) or reverse transcriptase copies DNA; DNA ligase joins sticky ends by forming phosphodiester bonds; transformation lets the host cell grow the recombinant plasmid; the marker gene lets you check which cells actually took it up; and, given a promoter, the transformed cell can make the protein — for example, human insulin.

Where this reappears across the rest of the specification

  • DNA replication — the natural process PCR deliberately imitates: helicase/hydrogen bonds versus heat/95°C; RNA primers versus added primers; DNA polymerase versus Taq polymerase.
  • DNA, genes and chromosomes — introns/exons are the reason cDNA, not the genomic gene, is used for a prokaryotic host.
  • Regulation of transcription and translation — promoters and transcription factors, the same vocabulary reused for the inserted gene's own promoter.
  • Later gene-technology spec points — DNA probes and genetic fingerprinting both reuse restriction endonucleases, PCR and gel electrophoresis as shared underlying tools.
  • Evolution — antibiotic resistance genes carried on plasmids are the same type of vector, transferring naturally rather than by engineering, through horizontal gene transfer.

Rule of thumb: almost every technique named elsewhere in gene technology — DNA probes, fingerprinting, gene sequencing — is built from the same small enzyme toolkit introduced here. Learn the enzymes once, precisely, and they carry you through the rest of the topic.

Self-check before moving on

  • I can explain why a gene from one species can work in another, stating both that the genetic code is universal and that transcription/translation mechanisms are universal, and I can use the term "transgenic" correctly.
  • I can describe all three ways of obtaining a DNA fragment (reverse transcriptase/cDNA, restriction endonuclease, gene machine), each with its distinguishing advantage.
  • I can explain the PCR cycle as three ordered, correctly-temperatured steps — denaturation (≈95°C), annealing (≈50–65°C), extension (≈72°C, Taq polymerase) — and state that DNA doubles every cycle, giving exponential amplification.
  • I can describe the full in vivo pipeline in order: same restriction enzyme cuts gene and plasmid → matching sticky ends anneal → DNA ligase seals phosphodiester bonds → transformation (inefficient) → marker gene selects survivors → expression, given a promoter and terminator.
  • I never write "restriction enzymes cut the gene" — they cut the plasmid, or the DNA either side of it.
  • I can name each enzyme's one precise job without swapping restriction endonuclease, DNA ligase, reverse transcriptase, Taq polymerase, DNA helicase or DNA polymerase for one another.
  • I can evaluate GM technology sector by sector (agriculture, industry, medicine) in a balanced way, and distinguish somatic from germ-line gene therapy.

5-Minute Cram

The five spec content points, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Why a gene works in another species

  • Two ideas needed together: genetic code universal + transcription/translation mechanisms universal — one alone is only half marks.
  • An organism carrying transferred DNA from another species = transgenic, not just "genetically modified."

2 · Three ways to obtain a DNA fragment

  • Reverse transcriptase: mRNA → cDNA, intron-free (essential for a prokaryote host, which can't splice pre-mRNA).
  • Restriction endonuclease: cuts at a palindromic site, staggered cut → sticky ends.
  • Gene machine: built base by base — fast, accurate, no donor DNA needed.
  • Never say a restriction enzyme "cuts the gene" — it cuts the DNA either side of it.

3 · Amplifying DNA in vitro — PCR

  • Denaturation ≈95°C (breaks H-bonds) → annealing ≈50–65°C (primers bind) → extension ≈72°C (Taq polymerase, heat-stable).
  • Repeat 25–35 times; DNA doubles every cycle = exponential (2ⁿ), not linear.
  • PCR = DNA copies only, fast, outside a cell; in vivo culture can also yield the protein but is slower.

4 · Building a recombinant plasmid

  • Same restriction enzyme cuts gene and plasmid → complementary sticky ends → DNA ligase seals with phosphodiester bonds.
  • Promoter = where RNA polymerase binds (no promoter, no transcription); terminator = stops RNA polymerase after the gene.

5 · Transformation & marker genes

  • Ca²⁺ + heat shock (or electric current) → membrane more permeable → plasmid enters. Only ~1–5% of cells transformed.
  • Marker gene (e.g. antibiotic resistance) lets you select survivors on a medium containing that antibiotic — it does nothing to the gene of interest itself.
  • You are not required to name a specific marker gene — only the principle.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Why a gene works in another species

❓ Explain why a gene taken from a jellyfish can still be expressed once transferred into a bacterium.
The genetic code is universal — the same base triplet codes for the same amino acid in every organism, including the bacterium. The mechanisms of transcription and translation are also universal, so the bacterium's own RNA polymerase, ribosomes and tRNA can transcribe and translate the transferred gene. Because both of these are true, the bacterium can produce the same polypeptide the jellyfish gene originally coded for.
❓ Define the term "transgenic organism."
An organism that contains DNA that has been transferred into it from a different organism or species.
❓ A student writes "the DNA works because all organisms are genetically modified the same way." Identify what is wrong with this statement and correct it.
The statement confuses cause and description. The correct reasoning is that the genetic code and the mechanisms of transcription and translation are universal across organisms, which is why transferred DNA can be expressed; the resulting organism is described as transgenic, not as "genetically modified the same way."

Three ways to obtain a DNA fragment

❓ Describe how a fragment of DNA containing a desired gene can be obtained using reverse transcriptase, and explain why this method is preferred over using the gene directly from a eukaryotic chromosome when the host is a bacterium.
Reverse transcriptase uses a single-stranded mRNA template to synthesise a complementary strand of DNA (cDNA) by complementary base pairing. Because mRNA has already had its introns removed by splicing, the cDNA produced is intron-free. This matters because a prokaryotic host cannot splice pre-mRNA, so a gene taken directly from a eukaryotic chromosome (still containing introns) would not be correctly transcribed and translated.
❓ Explain how a restriction endonuclease is able to produce complementary sticky ends on two different DNA molecules.
A restriction endonuclease recognises one specific, short palindromic recognition sequence and makes a staggered cut across the two strands at that site, leaving single-stranded overhangs (sticky ends). Because the recognition sequence is palindromic, cutting any DNA molecule with the same enzyme produces overhangs with the same sequence, so they are complementary and can base-pair with one another.
❓ State one advantage of using a gene machine to produce a fragment of DNA, compared with cutting the gene out of genomic DNA using a restriction endonuclease.
A gene machine does not require any donor DNA or organism, is rapid and accurate, and the gene can be synthesised with no introns and with promoter/terminator regions already built in — none of which is guaranteed when cutting a gene directly out of genomic DNA.

Amplifying DNA in vitro — PCR

❓ Describe the three steps of one PCR cycle, including approximate temperatures.
Denaturation, at approximately 95°C: the DNA is heated, breaking the hydrogen bonds between the two strands and separating them. Annealing, at approximately 50–65°C: the mixture is cooled so that primers, complementary to the sequences flanking the target region, bind to the single strands by complementary base pairing. Extension, at approximately 72°C: Taq polymerase, a heat-stable DNA polymerase, joins free nucleotides to each template strand, extending a new strand from each primer.
❓ A sample is amplified by PCR for 25 cycles, starting from a single DNA fragment. Explain why the increase in DNA quantity is described as exponential rather than linear.
The quantity of target DNA doubles with every cycle, since each strand from the previous cycle acts as a template for a new strand in the next cycle. This means the total number of copies after n cycles is 2ⁿ, which increases exponentially rather than by a constant amount each cycle (linearly).
❓ Explain why an ordinary DNA polymerase, rather than Taq polymerase, could not be used for PCR.
Each PCR cycle requires repeated heating to approximately 95°C during denaturation; an ordinary DNA polymerase would be denatured by this repeated heating and would stop working, whereas Taq polymerase is naturally heat-stable (originally isolated from a bacterium living in hot springs) and survives every cycle.

Building a recombinant plasmid

❓ Describe how a gene fragment is inserted into a plasmid vector to form a recombinant plasmid.
The same restriction endonuclease is used to cut both the plasmid and the DNA either side of the gene fragment, producing complementary sticky ends on each. The sticky ends anneal by complementary base pairing, and DNA ligase then catalyses the formation of phosphodiester bonds, sealing the plasmid backbone and producing a recombinant plasmid.
❓ Explain why the same restriction enzyme, rather than two different ones, must be used to cut the gene fragment and the plasmid.
A restriction enzyme recognises one specific recognition sequence; using the same enzyme on both the gene fragment and the plasmid ensures the sticky ends produced on each are complementary. A different enzyme would recognise a different sequence and produce sticky ends that could not base-pair with each other, so the gene could not be inserted.
❓ Explain the roles of the promoter and terminator regions added to a fragment of DNA before it is inserted into a vector.
The promoter is the region where RNA polymerase and transcription factors bind; without it, the gene would not be transcribed. The terminator signals RNA polymerase to detach from the DNA, so that transcription stops after the gene of interest rather than continuing into the surrounding plasmid DNA.

Transformation & marker genes

❓ Describe how host bacterial cells are made to take up a recombinant plasmid.
The recombinant plasmids are mixed with the host cells in a solution containing calcium ions, and the cells are then given a heat shock (or exposed to an electric current); this increases the permeability of the cell-surface membrane, allowing plasmids to enter the cells. This process is called transformation.
❓ Explain why a marker gene is included on a plasmid vector, and describe how it is used to identify transformed cells.
Because transformation is inefficient (typically only around 1–5% of cells take up a plasmid), a marker gene — commonly conferring antibiotic resistance — is needed to distinguish the rare transformed cells from the many non-transformed ones. Host cells are grown on a medium containing that antibiotic; only cells carrying the plasmid, and therefore the marker gene, survive and grow, while non-transformed cells die. Survival is evidence of successful transformation.
❓ A transgenic plant does not pass its inserted gene on to any of its offspring. Suggest an explanation.
Gene insertion may have occurred after cell division had already begun, so the cells that went on to produce the gametes never received a copy of the inserted gene — the gene is therefore not present in any of the offspring.
