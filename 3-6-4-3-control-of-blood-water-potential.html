Control of Blood Water Potential

How the nephron keeps the water potential of the blood inside a narrow range: bulk filtration under pressure at the glomerulus, selective reabsorption in the proximal convoluted tubule, a salt gradient built and maintained by the loop of Henle, and a final, moment-to-moment adjustment of water reabsorption at the distal convoluted tubule and collecting duct, switched by ADH.

The single habit that protects the most marks here: never let an answer stop at "more water is reabsorbed." Examiners want the mechanism — the collecting duct's permeability to water increases because ADH binds to receptors, causing aquaporin-containing vesicles to fuse with the membrane. And on the loop of Henle, direction always matters: water leaves the descending limb into the medulla, and is only reabsorbed back into the blood much later, at the collecting duct — never say water is "reabsorbed into" the loop of Henle itself.

Section 1 · spec content point

💧 Osmoregulation: what it is, and why it matters

Before any of the kidney's machinery, get the concept straight: everything in this spec point exists in service of one job — keeping the water potential of the blood inside a narrow band, whatever you eat, drink, or sweat out.

📖

What the specification says: "Osmoregulation as control of the water potential of the blood."

1
2
3

Imagine a jug of squash that always has to taste exactly the same, no matter what — not too watery, not too strong. If you pour in extra water it goes weak; if you forget to top it up it goes too strong. Your blood is a bit like that jug. Your body is constantly checking how "watery" or "concentrated" your blood is, and quietly adjusting things behind the scenes so it always stays at roughly the same strength — whether you've just drunk a huge glass of water or been sweating all afternoon in the sun.

  • Osmoregulation — keeping the "strength" (water content) of the blood steady
  • Water potential — a measure of how much water is in a solution, and how much it "wants" to move
  • Osmosis — water moving from a weaker solution to a stronger one

Osmoregulation is the control of the water potential of the blood — keeping it roughly constant even though water intake, sweating, exercise and how salty your diet is are all constantly changing it. This matters because of osmosis: body cells sit in fluid derived from the blood, so any change in blood water potential changes the water potential gradient across every cell membrane in the body. If blood becomes too dilute, water moves into cells and they swell; if it becomes too concentrated, water moves out of cells and they shrink. Either extreme disrupts how cells work, so the kidneys constantly correct the blood's water potential back towards normal, using negative feedback.

Osmoregulation is the control of the water potential of the blood — holding it within a narrow, tolerable range despite continual changes in water intake, sweating, exercise and the salt content of the diet.

Why this matters follows directly from osmosis: body cells are bathed in fluid derived from blood plasma, so any change in the blood's water potential changes the water potential gradient across every cell membrane in the body.

  • If blood water potential rises too far (blood becomes too dilute), water moves into cells by osmosis, down the water potential gradient — cells swell, and in extreme cases can burst (cytolysis).
  • If blood water potential falls too far (blood becomes too concentrated), water moves out of cells by osmosis — cells shrink, disrupting enzyme-controlled reactions and normal cell function.

The nephron corrects this in three stages, each covered by its own section later on this page: ultrafiltration at the glomerulus (bulk, non-selective filtration under pressure), selective reabsorption in the proximal convoluted tubule (reclaiming useful solutes and most of the water), and a hormonally-controlled fine adjustment of exactly how much of the remaining water is reabsorbed, at the distal convoluted tubule and collecting duct.

Osmoregulation is also a complete, examinable worked example of negative feedback (3.6.4.1): a receptor detects a deviation from the norm, a coordinator responds by adjusting hormone output, and an effector brings the value back towards normal — switching the response back off once it has.

📌

Always write "water potential", never a vague "concentration" — the direction of osmosis depends on the water potential gradient specifically, and mark schemes credit the precise term.

Section 2 · spec content point

🧠 The roles of the hypothalamus, posterior pituitary and ADH

Three structures, three separate jobs — detect, decide, dispatch. Keeping straight exactly which structure does what is worth more marks here than almost anything else.

📖

What the specification says: "The roles of the hypothalamus, posterior pituitary and antidiuretic hormone (ADH) in osmoregulation."

1
2
3

Think of it like a tiny alarm system. Special "water alarms" (osmoreceptors) sit in a control room in your brain (the hypothalamus) and shrink up like raisins whenever there isn't quite enough water around them. That shrinking sets off the alarm. The control room then tells a nearby delivery depot (the posterior pituitary) to send out a chemical messenger (ADH) into the blood, telling the kidneys to hold onto more water.

  • Osmoreceptors — the "water alarms" that shrink or swell
  • Hypothalamus — the control room that houses them and reacts
  • Posterior pituitary — the depot that actually sends the messenger out
  • ADH — the messenger chemical itself

Osmoreceptors are cells in the hypothalamus that detect changes in blood water potential by osmosis: a fall in blood water potential makes them lose water and shrink; a rise makes them gain water and swell. This shrinking or swelling is itself the detection step. Shrinking stimulates the hypothalamus, which causes the posterior pituitary gland to release more ADH into the blood. It's worth remembering that ADH is made in the hypothalamus but stored and released from the posterior pituitary — two different jobs, two different structures. Once released, ADH travels in the blood to the kidney, where it changes how much water gets reabsorbed (Section 6). The whole loop only counts as negative feedback once you trace it all the way round: more water reabsorbed raises blood water potential, which switches the osmoreceptors — and so ADH release — back off again.

Detection: the osmoreceptors

Osmoreceptors are specialised cells in the hypothalamus that respond directly to the water potential of the blood flowing past them, by osmosis:

  • A fall in blood water potential causes osmoreceptor cells to lose water by osmosis and shrink — this shrinking is how the change is detected.
  • A rise in blood water potential (e.g. after drinking a lot of water) causes osmoreceptor cells to gain water and swell — the mirror-image case.

Decide and dispatch: the hypothalamus and posterior pituitary

StructureRole
HypothalamusHouses the osmoreceptors; is stimulated when they shrink; manufactures ADH
Posterior pituitaryDoes not make ADH itself — stores and releases the ADH made in the hypothalamus, into the blood
ADH (antidiuretic hormone)Travels in the blood to the kidney, where it acts on the distal convoluted tubule and collecting duct (Section 6)
⚖️

Don't confuse the two structures. ADH is made in the hypothalamus, but stored and released from the posterior pituitary — they are not the same structure, and a "describe" question can credit this distinction explicitly.

The negative feedback loop, traced all the way round

  1. Blood water potential falls (e.g. through sweating, low water intake, a salty meal).
  2. Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus lose water by osmosis and shrink.
  3. This stimulates the hypothalamus, causing the posterior pituitary to release more ADH into the blood.
  4. (ADH's effect on the kidney tubule is covered fully in Section 6 — more water is reabsorbed.)
  5. Blood water potential rises back towards normal — osmoreceptors are no longer stimulated, so ADH release falls again.
▶ 🚩 Trap — stopping the loop too early

Stating "more water is reabsorbed" and stopping there does not, on its own, demonstrate negative feedback. The loop has to be traced the whole way back round: more water reabsorbed raises blood water potential, which switches the osmoreceptors off, so ADH release falls again. It's the full circuit closing — not the single response in the middle — that earns the negative-feedback mark.

The converse case works exactly the same way in reverse: a rise in blood water potential causes osmoreceptors to gain water and swell, so less ADH is released.

Section 3 · spec content point

🧫 Nephron structure & the formation of glomerular filtrate

Every nephron does the same job in the same order — learn the full sequence of named structures, then the pressure-driven mechanism that forces the first filtrate out of the blood.

📖

What the specification says: "The structure of the nephron and its role in the formation of glomerular filtrate."

1
2
3

The nephron is like a very long, twisty pipe with a coffee filter at the start. Blood is squeezed hard through the filter — water and tiny dissolved things squeeze through into the pipe, while big things like proteins and blood cells are too large and stay in the blood. The pipe itself has several differently-named sections, one after another, and each one does a different job as the liquid travels along it.

  • Glomerulus — the tight ball of capillaries doing the squeezing
  • Bowman's capsule — the cup that catches what gets squeezed out
  • Filtrate — the liquid that has been squeezed out, before anything useful is reclaimed

The nephron is one continuous tube: blood arrives at the glomerulus, a tight knot of capillaries sitting inside a cup-shaped Bowman's capsule, then the filtrate travels through the proximal convoluted tubule, down and back up the loop of Henle, through the distal convoluted tubule, and into the collecting duct, before draining into the renal pelvis and ureter. Filtration happens because the blood vessel bringing blood in (afferent arteriole) is wider than the one taking it away (efferent arteriole), so pressure builds up and forces water and small dissolved molecules — glucose, amino acids, urea, ions — out of the blood and into the capsule. A membrane called the basement membrane acts as the actual filter, holding back anything too large, like proteins and blood cells.

The sequence of structures — learn the full names

Glomerulus → Bowman's capsule → proximal convoluted tubule → loop of Henle (descending → ascending) → distal convoluted tubule → collecting duct → renal pelvis/ureter.

▶ 🚩 Trap — abbreviations instead of full names

Writing only "PCT" or "DCT" with no full name written out anywhere in the answer risks losing the mark on a "describe the structures" question — write the full term out at least once.

A simplified nephron: cortex at the top, medulla shaded below. Only the loop of Henle and collecting duct dip into the medulla — the reason they're the structures that concentrate urine (Sections 5 & 6).

Ultrafiltration — how the first filtrate is formed

  1. The afferent arteriole is wider than the efferent arteriole, so blood is squeezed into the glomerular capillaries faster than it can leave — building high hydrostatic pressure.
  2. This pressure forces water and small dissolved molecules — glucose, amino acids, urea, mineral ions — out of the capillaries.
  3. These pass through the capillary pores, the basement membrane, and the gaps between podocytes (the cells forming Bowman's capsule's inner lining), into the capsule space.
  4. The basement membrane is the actual size-selective filter: plasma proteins and blood cells are too large to pass through and stay in the blood.
▶ 🚩 Trap — crediting podocytes as "the filter"

Podocytes wrap around the capillaries and leave gaps for filtrate to pass through, but they are not themselves the size-selective barrier — that job belongs to the basement membrane. Naming podocytes as "the filter" is a common but costly imprecision.

When filtration goes wrong: protein in the urine

Protein sometimes appears in urine because either the basement membrane is damaged or more permeable than normal, letting larger molecules through that would usually be retained, or there are simply more protein channels/pores present, letting proteins into the filtrate.

Filtration (clearance) rate

Filtration or clearance rate follows the standard rate formula — the volume of plasma cleared of a substance per unit time. Named factors that can affect a person's rate include body/muscle mass, ethnicity, exercise, and kidney disease.

▶ 🚩 Trap — answering "health" instead of a named factor

A vague answer of "health" is not accepted for factors affecting filtration rate — always name the specific factor instead (body/muscle mass, ethnicity, exercise, or kidney disease).

Section 4 · spec content point

🔄 Reabsorption of glucose and water in the proximal convoluted tubule

A sodium-driven co-transport sequence you've already met once before — this is the same mechanism reused from glucose absorption in the ileum.

📖

What the specification says: "Reabsorption of glucose and water by the proximal convoluted tubule."

1
2
3

Picture a delivery depot: workers (protein pumps) throw sodium out of the truck bay using energy. That empties the bay, so sodium from outside rushes back in through a special door — and grabs a hitchhiker (glucose) on the way through. The glucose then wanders off into the blood, and water follows along right behind it, because that's where things are now more concentrated.

Sodium ions are actively pumped out of the tubule's epithelial cells into the blood, using ATP. This creates a concentration gradient that pulls sodium back into the cell from the filtrate, through a co-transport protein — and glucose (or amino acids) gets dragged in alongside it, against its own concentration gradient. Glucose then leaves the cell into the blood by facilitated diffusion, and water follows by osmosis because removing all these solutes lowers the water potential of the cell and blood. Nearly all the glucose and amino acids, and about 85% of the water, are reabsorbed at this one stage.

  1. Na⁺ is actively transported out of the epithelial cell into the blood, using ATP — creating a sodium ion concentration gradient between the filtrate and the cell.
  2. Na⁺ then diffuses back in from the filtrate through a co-transport protein, carrying glucose (or amino acids) with it, against glucose's own concentration gradient.
  3. Glucose then moves into the blood by facilitated diffusion.
  4. Removing these solutes lowers the water potential of the cell and blood, so water follows by osmosis.
  5. Essentially all of the glucose and amino acids, and around 85% of the water, are reabsorbed at this stage.

PCT epithelium — adaptations for reabsorption

  • Microvilli — increase surface area for reabsorption
  • Many mitochondria — provide ATP for active transport and co-transport
  • Many carrier and co-transport proteins in the cell membrane
  • Close association with a surrounding capillary network — carries reabsorbed substances away quickly, maintaining the concentration gradient
🔁

Directly synoptic. This is the same active-transport-then-co-transport mechanism as glucose/Na⁺ absorption in the ileum (3.2.3 / 3.3.3) — one of the most explicitly reused mechanisms in the whole specification. If you can describe it there, you can describe it here.

Why a diabetic's urine may contain glucose

Blood — and so filtrate — glucose concentration is too high for all of it to be reabsorbed in the PCT: the carrier/co-transport proteins become saturated, working at their maximum rate. The capacity for glucose reabsorption at the PCT is exceeded, so the excess glucose remains in the filtrate and is excreted.

🔁

Synoptic link. This is the same carrier-saturation logic reused from blood glucose control (3.6.4.2) — "too much substrate for the number of carriers available" explains glucose in the urine of both an untreated diabetic and an overloaded PCT.

Section 5 · spec content point

🌀 The loop of Henle: maintaining the sodium gradient in the medulla

Two limbs, opposite permeabilities, running in opposite directions — that mismatch is the entire trick behind the countercurrent multiplier.

📖

What the specification says: "Maintaining a gradient of sodium ions in the medulla by the loop of Henle."

1
2
3

Picture two escalators running side by side, one going down and one going up. The down escalator leaks water out along the way. The up escalator leaks salt out along the way instead. Because they're right next to each other and running in opposite directions, all that leaked salt builds up more and more the deeper you go — like a salt battery charging up — and that makes the down escalator's water leak out even faster near the bottom, since there's more salt outside pulling it.

The two limbs of the loop of Henle have opposite, one-way permeabilities. The descending limb is permeable to water but not salt, so water leaves it by osmosis into the increasingly salty medulla, concentrating the filtrate as it travels down. The ascending limb is permeable to salt but not water, so sodium and chloride ions are actively pumped out into the medulla, but no water can follow. Because fluid flows in opposite directions in the two limbs — "countercurrent" — the salt these pumps push out keeps building up rather than washing away, creating one steep gradient that's saltiest right at the tip of the loop.

Each small, repeated pump of salt out of the ascending limb makes the medulla saltier at that level, which pulls more water out of the descending limb right beside it — small effects that "multiply" into one steep gradient, saltiest at the tip.

How the gradient is created and maintained

  1. In the ascending limb (impermeable to water), Na⁺/Cl⁻ ions are actively transported out into the surrounding medulla tissue.
  2. This makes the medulla increasingly salty — its water potential falls — especially deep near the bottom of the loop.
  3. In the descending limb (permeable to water, not salt), water leaves the filtrate by osmosis into this salty medulla, concentrating the filtrate further as it travels down.
  4. Because fluid flows in opposite directions in the two limbs, this gradient never equalises — the small, repeated effect at every level "multiplies" into one steep gradient along the whole length of the loop: the countercurrent multiplier.
▶ 🚩 Direction is always marked

Never write "water reabsorbed into the loop of Henle" — direction matters. Water leaves the descending limb into the medulla; it is only reabsorbed back into the blood much later, at the collecting duct (Section 6). Getting the two limbs' permeabilities swapped is the single most commonly made error on this whole diagram — descending = water, not salt; ascending = salt, not water.

The effect of loop length — and the desert-mammal application

A thicker medulla means a longer loop of Henle. A longer loop moves more sodium into the medulla, so a steep water-potential gradient is maintained over a greater length of the nephron — meaning more water can later be reabsorbed from the collecting duct as it passes through the medulla.

🌵

The classic desert-mammal application. A desert mammal has a longer loop of Henle and a thicker medulla, producing a steeper sodium/water-potential gradient — and therefore a smaller volume of more concentrated urine, an adaptation to conserve water in a dry environment.

Section 6 · spec content point

🚰 Reabsorption of water by the DCT & collecting duct

This is the one stage under moment-to-moment hormonal control — the same salt gradient built by the loop of Henle only pulls water out of the collecting duct if ADH has opened enough aquaporins to let it through.

📖

What the specification says: "Reabsorption of water by the distal convoluted tubule and collecting ducts."

1
2
3

Think of the wall of the collecting duct like a wall with taps in it that can be opened or shut. ADH is the signal that turns extra taps (aquaporins) on, letting more water flow straight back through the wall and into the blood. More ADH means more taps open means more water saved, and a smaller, stronger cup of urine left over. No ADH means the taps stay shut, water just carries on out, and you end up with a big, watery wee.

ADH travels in the blood and binds to receptors on cells of the distal convoluted tubule and collecting duct. This causes vesicles carrying aquaporins — water-channel proteins — to fuse with the cell membrane, so more aquaporins sit in the membrane and it becomes more permeable to water. Water then moves out of the tubule by osmosis, down the water potential gradient into the salty medulla and back into the blood — the more ADH, the more water is reabsorbed, so urine becomes smaller in volume and more concentrated. With too little ADH, fewer aquaporins are inserted, less water is reabsorbed, and a large volume of dilute, pale urine is produced instead.

The converse case: after drinking a lot of water

The mirror-image response works the same way in reverse: osmoreceptors gain water and swell; less ADH is released; the collecting duct becomes less permeable to water (fewer aquaporins); less water is reabsorbed, producing a large volume of dilute (pale) urine.

▶ 🚩 Always name "water" — and always name the structure

Always specify water, never "fluid" or "solution", when describing this osmotic movement — and always name which structure (collecting duct, medulla, blood) water is moving into or out of. A large number of students lose marks by describing "movement of fluid" or by getting the direction of the water potential gradient the wrong way round between the tubule and the surrounding tissue.

Symptoms of too little ADH

Less water reabsorbed by the collecting duct means dehydration/thirst, an increased volume of urine, and dilute/pale urine — the direct converse of the normal ADH response.

▶ Where the difficulty in this spec point actually comes from

The underlying biology — pressure filtration, co-transport, osmosis down a gradient, a hormone changing membrane permeability — is all reused from elsewhere in the specification. What makes this spec point hard is the sheer number of named structures and the precision needed around direction: which way water or ions are moving, and between which two named structures, at every single stage. Long-answer questions here are won or lost on completeness and direction, not on understanding a new concept.

Section 8 · exam technique

💊 Applying the mechanism: diuretic drugs & unfamiliar scenarios

Reciting the ADH sequence in order is well rehearsed by most students — reasoning through an unfamiliar scenario, like a drug interfering somewhere else in the nephron, is the harder application skill this spec point is actually tested on.

A diuretic drug (for example, one used to treat high blood pressure) typically works by blocking ion (Na⁺/Cl⁻) reabsorption in the nephron, most often at the loop of Henle. This reduces the salt gradient built in the medulla, so the water potential gradient between the collecting duct and the medulla becomes smaller. Less water is reabsorbed by osmosis as a result, producing a larger volume of urine — without ADH itself changing at all.

🧭

How to tackle any scenario like this: (1) identify exactly which named structure the drug or change acts on; (2) state the consequence for the water potential gradient between the collecting duct and the medulla; (3) only then state the consequence for water reabsorption and urine volume. Each link in the chain has to be made explicit — never jump straight from "the drug does X" to "so urine volume changes", skipping the gradient step in between.

▶ 🧪 Try one yourself — click to reveal the reasoning

Scenario: a new drug is found to block sodium/chloride ion reabsorption specifically in the ascending limb of the loop of Henle. Predict and explain its effect on urine volume.

Reasoning: with less Na⁺/Cl⁻ actively transported out of the ascending limb, the sodium ion gradient normally built up in the medulla is reduced; this means the water potential gradient between the collecting duct and the surrounding medulla is smaller than normal; so less water is reabsorbed from the collecting duct by osmosis, producing a larger volume of more dilute urine — even though the drug never touches ADH or the collecting duct directly.

Interpreting trial data on a diuretic drug

When a question shows trial data comparing a drug against a placebo or control, look at whether the standard deviation / error bars of the two groups overlap. Substantial overlap means the difference in means might just be due to chance; clearly separated ranges support a genuine difference caused by the drug. Always state the comparison using the actual figures given in the question, not a generic description of "a difference".

🔎

The second-hardest part of this application skill is simply getting the direction of movement right at every stage — between the nephron tubule and the surrounding medulla tissue, and never describing water as being "reabsorbed into" the loop of Henle. For each named region in an unfamiliar scenario, state exactly which substance moves, by which mechanism, and in which direction.

Section 9 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

The nephron works especially well inside a synoptic essay, because it's the one organ where pressure, active transport, co-transport and osmosis all cause movement within a single structure.

▶ Example paragraph — a "causes of movement" essay theme

High hydrostatic pressure in the glomerulus, caused by the afferent arteriole being wider than the efferent arteriole, forces water and small solutes out of the blood and into the nephron — a pressure-driven movement rather than an active one. In the proximal convoluted tubule, sodium ions are first actively pumped out of the epithelial cell, then diffuse back in through a co-transport protein that carries glucose along with them — the same active-transport-then-co-transport pairing that drives glucose absorption in the ileum. Further along the nephron, ADH increases the permeability of the collecting duct to water by triggering the insertion of aquaporins, so water is reabsorbed by osmosis down a water potential gradient that the loop of Henle has built and maintained in the medulla. One organ, four separate physical causes of movement — pressure, active transport, co-transport and osmosis — each doing a distinct job in the same continuous pathway.

Essay technique: name each physical cause of movement precisely — pressure, active transport, co-transport, osmosis — before linking it back to whatever the title is actually asking about. Treat a nephron paragraph as a strong closing paragraph in any synoptic essay on transport or movement of substances.

🧠 Memory hook & final checklist

🧠

Memory hook — wide in, narrow out; water down, salt up. The afferent arteriole is wider than the efferent, so blood is squeezed under pressure through the glomerulus — ultrafiltration. In the loop of Henle, the descending limb loses water (down into a saltier medulla); the ascending limb loses salt (up towards the cortex) — always opposite. And for ADH: more ADH means more aquaporins means more water reabsorbed means a smaller, more concentrated urine — and ADH is made in the hypothalamus but released from the posterior pituitary.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Osmoregulation: the concept

  • Osmoregulation = control of the water potential of the blood.
  • Too dilute → cells gain water, swell/burst. Too concentrated → cells lose water, shrink.
  • Achieved in 3 stages: ultrafiltration → selective reabsorption (PCT) → hormonal fine-tuning (DCT/collecting duct).
  • It's a full worked example of negative feedback.

2 · Hypothalamus, pituitary & ADH

  • Osmoreceptors (hypothalamus) shrink when blood water potential falls — that's the detection step.
  • ADH is made in the hypothalamus, but stored & released from the posterior pituitary.
  • Trace the loop fully round: more ADH → more water reabsorbed → Ψ rises → ADH release falls again.

3 · Nephron structure & filtration

  • Sequence: glomerulus → Bowman's capsule → PCT → loop of Henle → DCT → collecting duct → renal pelvis. Write full names at least once.
  • Afferent wider than efferent → high hydrostatic pressure → ultrafiltration.
  • Basement membrane is the actual filter — not the podocytes.
  • Filtration-rate factors: body/muscle mass, ethnicity, exercise, kidney disease — never just "health".

4 · PCT reabsorption

  • Na⁺ actively pumped out → gradient drives Na⁺ + glucose back in via co-transport protein → glucose out by facilitated diffusion → water follows by osmosis.
  • Same mechanism as the ileum (3.2.3/3.3.3). ~85% of water and essentially all glucose/amino acids reabsorbed here.
  • Diabetic glycosuria = carrier proteins saturated, not "no glucose reabsorbed".

5 · Loop of Henle

  • Descending = permeable to water, not salt. Ascending = permeable to salt, not water. Always opposite.
  • Countercurrent flow means the gradient never equalises — builds up, saltiest at the tip.
  • Never say "water reabsorbed into the loop of Henle".
  • Desert mammal = longer loop + thicker medulla → steeper gradient → smaller, more concentrated urine.

6 · DCT, collecting duct & ADH

  • ADH binds receptors → vesicles with aquaporins fuse with membrane → more permeable to water → more water reabsorbed by osmosis.
  • More ADH = smaller volume, more concentrated urine. Less ADH = larger volume, dilute urine.
  • Always say "water" (never fluid/solution), and always name the structure.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Osmoregulation: the concept

❓ Define osmoregulation, and explain why the water potential of the blood must be kept within a narrow range.
Osmoregulation is the control of the water potential of the blood. If blood water potential rises too far, water moves into cells by osmosis and they swell (in extreme cases, burst); if it falls too far, water moves out of cells by osmosis and they shrink, disrupting normal cell function.
❓ Explain how osmoregulation is an example of negative feedback.
A receptor (osmoreceptors) detects a deviation in blood water potential from the norm; a coordinator (hypothalamus/posterior pituitary) responds by adjusting ADH release; an effector (the collecting duct) changes water reabsorption so that blood water potential returns towards normal, which in turn switches the original response back off.

Hypothalamus, pituitary & ADH

❓ State where ADH is made, and where it is stored and released from.
ADH is made (synthesised) in the hypothalamus, but is stored in, and released from, the posterior pituitary gland — these are two different structures with two different roles.
❓ Explain how a fall in blood water potential is detected.
Osmoreceptor cells in the hypothalamus lose water by osmosis, down a water potential gradient, and shrink; this shrinking stimulates the hypothalamus, which in turn causes the posterior pituitary to release more ADH into the blood.
❓ A student writes "more ADH is released, so more water is reabsorbed" as a complete explanation of negative feedback. Explain what is missing.
The loop must be traced all the way back round: the extra water reabsorbed raises blood water potential back towards normal, so osmoreceptors are no longer stimulated and ADH release falls again — it is this return to the original stimulus level that makes it negative feedback, not the response in isolation.

Nephron structure & filtration

❓ Describe the sequence of structures a molecule of urea passes through, from the glomerulus to the ureter.
Glomerulus → Bowman's capsule → proximal convoluted tubule → loop of Henle (descending limb, then ascending limb) → distal convoluted tubule → collecting duct → renal pelvis → ureter.
❓ Explain the mechanism of ultrafiltration.
The afferent arteriole is wider than the efferent arteriole, creating high hydrostatic pressure in the glomerular capillaries; this forces water and small molecules (glucose, amino acids, urea, mineral ions) through the capillary pores, the basement membrane, and gaps between podocytes, into the Bowman's capsule space; the basement membrane acts as the size filter, so plasma proteins and blood cells are too large to pass and remain in the blood.
❓ Suggest and explain two possible reasons why a urine sample might test positive for protein.
Either the basement membrane is damaged or more permeable than normal, allowing larger molecules through that would normally be retained; or there are more protein channels/pores present in the membrane, allowing proteins into the filtrate.

PCT reabsorption

❓ Describe how glucose and water are reabsorbed in the proximal convoluted tubule.
Sodium ions are actively transported out of the epithelial cell into the blood, using ATP, creating a concentration gradient; sodium ions diffuse back into the cell from the filtrate through a co-transport protein, carrying glucose with them against its own concentration gradient; glucose then moves into the blood by facilitated diffusion; the resulting fall in water potential of the cell and blood causes water to follow by osmosis.
❓ Explain why an untreated diabetic's urine may contain glucose.
Blood, and therefore filtrate, glucose concentration is too high for it all to be reabsorbed; the carrier/co-transport proteins in the proximal convoluted tubule become saturated, working at their maximum rate; the excess glucose that cannot be reabsorbed remains in the filtrate and is excreted in the urine.

Loop of Henle

❓ Explain how the loop of Henle maintains a sodium ion gradient in the medulla.
In the ascending limb, which is impermeable to water, sodium and chloride ions are actively transported out into the surrounding medulla tissue, lowering its water potential; in the descending limb, which is permeable to water but not salt, water leaves the filtrate by osmosis into this increasingly salty medulla; because filtrate flows in opposite directions in the two limbs, the gradient does not equalise, and becomes progressively steeper towards the tip of the loop.
❓ A desert-adapted mammal has an unusually thick renal medulla. Explain the advantage of this.
A thicker medulla means a longer loop of Henle, which moves more sodium ions into the medulla and maintains a steeper water-potential gradient over a greater length of the nephron; this allows more water to be reabsorbed from the collecting duct, producing a smaller volume of more concentrated urine — an adaptation that conserves water.

DCT, collecting duct & ADH

❓ Describe the response to a fall in blood water potential, from detection through to the effect on urine.
Osmoreceptor cells in the hypothalamus lose water by osmosis and shrink; this stimulates the hypothalamus to cause the posterior pituitary to release more ADH into the blood; ADH binds to receptors on cells of the distal convoluted tubule and collecting duct; this causes vesicles containing aquaporins to fuse with the cell membrane, increasing the number of aquaporins and so the permeability to water; more water is reabsorbed by osmosis into the blood, producing a smaller volume of more concentrated urine; blood water potential rises back towards normal, so osmoreceptors are no longer stimulated and ADH release falls again.
❓ Explain two symptoms a person with insufficient ADH production would show.
Dehydration/thirst, because less water is reabsorbed by the collecting duct; and an increased volume of dilute/pale urine, because fewer aquaporins are inserted into the collecting duct membrane so less water is reabsorbed by osmosis.
