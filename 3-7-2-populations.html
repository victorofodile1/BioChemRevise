Populations

How a population's genetics is actually defined — its gene pool and allele frequency — and the Hardy–Weinberg model that predicts those frequencies will stay constant, provided five conditions hold. Plus what happens when they don't: how chance alone, through genetic drift, a founder event or a bottleneck, can reshape a population's genetics without any selection happening at all.

Section 1 · spec content point

🌍 Species exist as one or more populations

This single sentence is the reason population genetics is studied at the level of a population, not a whole species — and it's worth understanding properly before the definitions in Section 2 make sense.

📖

What the specification says: "Species exist as one or more populations."

1
2
3

Think of a species as a family surname, like "the Sparrows." There isn't one giant flock of every sparrow on the planet, all mixing together in one place. Instead there are lots of separate sparrow flocks scattered around, each doing their own thing in their own patch of woodland — each flock is a population. What's true of the genes in one flock doesn't have to be true of the genes in a flock the next country over, especially if the two flocks never meet.

  • Species = the whole surname — every sparrow that could, in principle, interbreed with any other
  • Population = one particular flock, living in one place at one time

A species is defined by the ability to interbreed and produce fertile offspring — but in practice, a species is almost never one single group that all mixes together. It's normally split into several separate populations, often living in different places, that mostly breed among themselves. Because each population breeds largely within itself, it can build up its own local mix of alleles, which doesn't have to match another population of the same species living somewhere else. That's exactly why the definitions and calculations in the rest of this page are written in terms of a population's gene pool, not a species' gene pool.

A species is defined by shared characteristics and the potential to interbreed to produce fertile offspring — but that potential rarely plays out as one single interbreeding mass. Real species are almost always split into multiple populations, often geographically separated by distance, terrain or habitat, each of which mates largely among its own members.

  • Because each population breeds mostly within itself, it can develop its own local set of allele frequencies — distinct from another population of the same species elsewhere.
  • This is precisely why gene pool and allele frequency (Section 3) are defined at population level, not species level — asking "what's this species' gene pool?" doesn't have a single answer until you specify which population.
  • Occasional migration between populations (gene flow) can keep their allele frequencies similar over time; if that movement stops and a population becomes genetically isolated, its allele frequencies can drift apart from the rest of the species — the starting point for the population divergence that, over long timescales, can eventually lead to speciation.
🔎

Keep this in mind as you read Sections 2–5: every definition, every condition of the Hardy–Weinberg principle, and every calculation on this page is scoped to one population, not "the species" as a whole.

Section 2 · spec content point

📍 Defining a population

Worth exactly one mark whenever it's asked, but marked as several separately-creditable clauses bolted together — drop one and you lose the mark even if the rest of the sentence reads well.

📖

What the specification says: "A population as a group of organisms of the same species occupying a particular space at a particular time that can potentially interbreed."

1
2
3

A population is basically everyone at your school right now who could theoretically be friends with each other — same "type" of person, same building, same year — even if most of them never actually speak. It doesn't matter whether they actually do interbreed, only that they're close enough, and similar enough, that they could.

A population is a group of organisms that are all the same species, all living in the same area, all at the same point in time, and all capable of breeding with one another if they happened to meet. The key word is "potentially" — a population isn't defined by organisms actually breeding, just by them being able to. Two organisms belong to different populations if they're different species, live in different places, exist at different times, or simply can't interbreed even in principle.

Each part of the definition is independently creditable, so build the answer clause by clause:

ClauseWhat it rules out if missing
Same speciesWithout this, "population" could describe a whole mixed community of different species
Particular spaceWithout this, two separated groups of the same species anywhere on Earth would count as one population
Particular timeWithout this, organisms from completely different generations or eras would count together
Can potentially interbreedWithout this, the definition wouldn't distinguish a population from an arbitrary group sharing a habitat

All three elements — species, space/time, and potential interbreeding — are separately creditable; don't drop one to save time in a written answer.

▶ 🚩 Trap — interbreeding vs inbreeding

These two words are examined as genuinely distinct ideas and are never interchangeable. Interbreeding is mating within the same species — it's the capability that defines a population here. Inbreeding is mating between close relatives, and it reduces genetic diversity (Section 6). Swapping one word for the other changes the meaning of the sentence completely, so never substitute one for the other.

Section 3 · spec content point

🎒 The concepts of gene pool and allele frequency

Two more one-mark definitions, plus the list of five things that can change them — learn this list once and it answers two different-sounding exam questions at the same time, since it's also exactly what the Hardy–Weinberg principle in Section 4 assumes is absent.

📖

What the specification says: "The concepts of gene pool and allele frequency."

1
2
3

Picture a big bag of marbles that belongs to one flock of sparrows — every marble is one copy of a gene version (an allele) that some sparrow in that flock is carrying. That whole bag is the gene pool. If a quarter of the marbles in the bag are red, then the allele frequency of "red" is a quarter, or 25%. Five things can change what's in the bag: someone invents a brand-new marble colour (mutation), some colours get picked more often than others (selection), pure luck sways which marbles get copied (drift), marbles get added or taken out of the bag (migration), or sparrows only pair up with certain colours (non-random mating).

A gene pool is all the alleles present in one population at a given moment — crucially, it has to be tied to a population, not to the species in general. Allele frequency is how common one particular allele is within that gene pool, expressed as a proportion, fraction or percentage — never as a raw headcount of individuals. Five things can change a gene pool: a brand-new allele appearing (mutation), some genotypes surviving or reproducing better than others (natural selection), chance dominating because the population is small (genetic drift), individuals moving in or out (migration), or mate choice being linked to phenotype (non-random mating).

TermPrecise definitionWording risk
Gene poolAll the alleles present in a population at a given timeMust reference the population, not the species as a whole — "alleles present in a species" on its own is not credited
Allele frequencyThe proportion of a particular allele within that gene poolMust be given as a proportion, percentage or fraction — a bare count of individuals is never a frequency

The five things that can change a gene pool

  • A new allele appears → mutation — the only source of genuinely new alleles
  • Some genotypes survive or reproduce better than others → natural selection
  • Chance dominates, most noticeably in a small population → genetic drift (Section 6)
  • Individuals move into or out of the population → migration (gene flow)
  • Mate choice is linked to phenotype → non-random mating
📌

Learn this list once, use it twice. These same five factors reappear, phrased the other way round, as the five conditions the Hardy–Weinberg principle needs in order to hold in Section 4 — "no mutation," "no selection," and so on. One list answers both questions.

▶ 🚩 Trap — "genes" instead of "alleles"

A gene pool is described in terms of alleles, not genes — "all the genes in a population" is not an acceptable substitute where alleles is required, since this whole topic is specifically about how common each version of a gene is, not whether the gene itself is present.

Section 4 · spec content point

⚖️ The Hardy–Weinberg principle & its conditions

A mathematical model that predicts what should happen to allele frequencies if nothing is disturbing them — and, just as importantly, what a mismatch between the prediction and reality is evidence of.

📖

What the specification says: "The Hardy–Weinberg principle provides a mathematical model, which predicts that allele frequencies will not change from generation to generation. The conditions under which the principle applies."

1
2
3

Imagine a huge jar of coloured sweets that never, ever changes its mix of colours — as long as five rules are all followed: nobody adds a brand-new colour, nobody prefers eating one colour over another, the jar is enormous, no sweets get added or taken from outside, and sweets pair up completely randomly. If you actually check the jar one day and the colour mix HAS changed, then you know for certain that one of those five rules got broken somewhere.

The Hardy–Weinberg principle is a maths-based model that predicts allele frequencies will stay exactly the same, generation after generation — but only if five conditions all hold at once: no new mutations, no selection happening, the population is large, it's isolated from migration, and mating is completely random. In reality, a real population almost never meets all five conditions perfectly. So when you compare what the model predicts to what you actually observe and they don't match, that mismatch is itself evidence that one of the five conditions is being broken — most often that selection is acting on the population.

Stating the principle

This is marked as three separate ideas, always built in the same order:

  1. Name the feature being measured — allele frequency;
  2. Say what happens to it — the model predicts it will not change from generation to generation;
  3. State that this holds provided at least one named condition applies — naming one correct condition is usually enough for this mark.

The five conditions, in full

#ConditionWhat it means
1No mutationNo new alleles are being created
2No selectionEvery genotype is equally likely to survive and reproduce — none has a selective advantage
3No migrationNo gene flow into or out of the population — it's effectively isolated
4Random matingMating happens without regard to genotype — no mate choice linked to phenotype
5Large populationLarge enough that chance (genetic drift) has a negligible effect on allele frequency

If even a single one of these five is broken, allele frequencies are free to change between generations.

Why observed frequencies differ from the prediction

Because all five conditions are rarely met simultaneously in a real population, a mismatch between the observed and Hardy–Weinberg-predicted genotype frequencies is itself evidence that one of those conditions has been broken — most often that selection is acting. The precise phrasing worth using: the under-represented genotype is "selected against", or its individuals are "less likely to survive and reproduce." Other legitimate explanations for a discrepancy include a small population (genetic drift), migration, non-random mating, a new mutation, or simply an unrepresentative or too-small sample.

📌

Notice that this is the same five-item list from Section 3, just flipped: whatever changes a gene pool is exactly what the Hardy–Weinberg principle needs to be absent for its prediction to hold.

Section 5 · spec content point

🧮 The Hardy–Weinberg equation & calculation

A fixed, repeatable method — the hard part is never the arithmetic, it's correctly identifying which given number is q² before you do anything else.

📖

What the specification says: "The frequency of alleles, genotypes and phenotypes in a population can be calculated using the Hardy–Weinberg equation: p² + 2pq + q² = 1, where p + q = 1, p is the frequency of the dominant allele of a gene, and q is the frequency of the other (usually recessive) allele of the gene."

1
2
3

It's a treasure hunt with one guaranteed clue: the percentage of the population showing the recessive trait is always your starting point, and it's always equal to q². Square-root that number to get q. Take q away from 1 to get p. Then a special step — multiply p, q and 2 together — reveals the hidden "carriers": people who don't show the trait at all, but are secretly carrying one copy of the recessive allele anyway.

The only number you can ever read straight off a question is the frequency of the recessive phenotype — and that number is always q². From there: square-root it to find q; subtract q from 1 to find p; and multiply 2 × p × q to find the frequency of heterozygotes (carriers). The two genotype equations must never be mixed up: p + q = 1 is about individual alleles, while p² + 2pq + q² = 1 is about whole genotypes — they're two separate equations that both happen to sum to 1.

What each symbol means

SymbolMeaning
pFrequency of the dominant allele
qFrequency of the recessive allele
p²Frequency of the homozygous dominant genotype
2pqFrequency of the heterozygous (carrier) genotype
q²Frequency of the homozygous recessive genotype — and of the recessive phenotype

The fixed method

  1. Start from the given recessive phenotype frequency — this always equals q².
  2. Take its square root to find q.
  3. Find p from p = 1 − q.
  4. Calculate 2pq for the heterozygote (carrier) frequency.

Method marks are awarded for a correct p or q even if the final answer goes wrong later — always show every step of the working.

The full p / q / p² / 2pq / q² route, run on a made-up recessive-condition frequency of 1 in 2500. A different starting percentage always follows exactly the same four arrows.
▶ 🚩 The costliest mix-up on this spec point

Given that, say, 64% of a population shows a recessive phenotype, a very common error is to take the remaining 36% "unaffected" figure as q², instead of the correct 64% affected figure — propagating the mistake through every subsequent number. Always read the question first to establish which phenotype is recessive, and only assign a number to q² once you're sure.

Avoiding the standard errors

  • The dominant phenotype frequency is p² + 2pq, not p² alone — it includes the heterozygotes, who look identical to the homozygous dominant genotype.
  • Never skip the square-root step when finding q from a given q².
  • Genotype frequencies (p² + 2pq + q² = 1) and allele frequencies (p + q = 1) are two separate equations — don't mix them.
  • Never write "pq" for the heterozygote frequency — the credited term is always 2pq.

When you're given counts rather than percentages

For a codominant gene especially, where all three genotypes are directly countable, work out allele frequency by counting alleles directly instead of assuming p + q = 1 from a phenotype percentage:

🔢

Counting method: allele frequency = (2 × number of that homozygote + number of heterozygotes) ÷ (2 × total individuals).

Interpreting a change in allele frequency over time

When a question gives data showing an allele frequency changing across generations, build the answer in this fixed order:

  1. Describe the change, quoting the actual figures from the data;
  2. State that the allele confers a selective advantage in the given conditions;
  3. Link to survival — individuals carrying it are more likely to survive and reproduce;
  4. Link to inheritance — they pass the allele on to their offspring;
  5. Conclude — so its frequency increases in the population.

Precise wording matters here: say "allele," never "gene," and "more likely to," never "will" — natural selection changes probabilities, not certainties.

Section 6 · extension content

🎲 Genetic drift, founder effect & bottleneck

This builds directly on the gene pool and Hardy–Weinberg ideas above, and is examined alongside them — three distinct ways allele frequencies can change by chance alone, with no selection involved at all.

Genetic drift itself

A small or isolated population has a small gene pool and correspondingly low genetic diversity. Purely by chance, only some individuals go on to reproduce in any given generation — so allele frequency changes randomly, generation to generation, with no connection to which allele is "better." Alleles can even be completely fixed (reach 100%) or lost (reach 0%) this way, entirely by chance.

⚖️

Drift vs selection — the distinction examiners want: selection changes allele frequency because the allele itself affects survival or reproduction. Drift changes allele frequency purely by chance — the allele has no effect on survival at all, but which individuals happen to breed in a small population is essentially a lottery.

This is also why drift's effect is far larger in small populations: in a large population, random ups and downs in one individual's offspring average out across thousands of others; in a small population, a handful of individuals reproducing (or not) can swing the whole population's allele frequency in one generation.

The founder effect

A small number of individuals become separated from a larger population — by chance — and go on to found a brand-new, isolated population elsewhere. Because it's only a handful of individuals, they carry only a small, non-representative sample of the original population's alleles. The new population's allele frequencies can differ substantially from the source population from the very first generation, purely because of which individuals happened to be among the founders.

A genetic bottleneck

An existing population is suddenly and drastically reduced in size — by a disease outbreak, habitat loss, or a similar disaster. The survivors are, again, not a representative sample of the original population's genetic diversity, purely by chance. Even once the population size recovers, the alleles that were lost during the crash stay lost — the regrown population carries permanently reduced genetic diversity, despite being back to its original size.

Two distinct chance events with the same outcome — an unrepresentative sample of the original gene pool, with no selection involved.
🧭

Founder effect vs bottleneck, in one line: a founder effect is a few individuals who leave and start a new population elsewhere; a bottleneck is most of an existing population dying in place, leaving survivors behind. Both leave a population's allele frequencies unrepresentative of where it came from, purely by chance.

More broadly, "describe the processes that reduce a population's genetic diversity" answers should state the general principle first — a reduced variety of alleles, or a reduced gene pool, in the resulting population — "fewer alleles" alone, without stating that the variety is reduced, is not itself a full description.

Section 7 · exam technique

🎯 Common mistakes, precision & practical skills

This spec point is testable at high difficulty not because the biology is conceptually hard, but because so much of it depends on exact wording and correctly identifying which number represents which symbol.

Practical skills attached to this spec point

No single numbered required practical is tied to 3.7.2 — instead, two named skills apply directly: sampling techniques in fieldwork, and solving the algebraic Hardy–Weinberg equation from real data.

  • Sampling the frequency of observable phenotypes — typically using quadrats for plants or sessile organisms, or mark-release-recapture for mobile animals.
  • Mark-release-recapture rests on three assumptions worth stating explicitly if asked: no births, deaths or migration during the study; marking doesn't affect an individual's chance of survival or of being recaptured; and there's enough time for marked individuals to disperse and mix fully before the second sample is taken.
  • For quadrat sampling of plants, be ready to explain a valid method for scaling a mean quadrat count up to a total population estimate for the whole study area — this is a frequently weak point in written answers.
▶ Writing a null hypothesis for population-sampling data

When a question asks for a null hypothesis, it must be specific to the exact scenario given — a generic, memorised null hypothesis that doesn't fit the context is not credited. The standard structure is: "there is no significant difference / correlation / association between [the specific variable] and [the specific variable]." A frequent, entirely avoidable error is writing the alternative hypothesis instead — stating that there IS a difference, when the question specifically asked for the null (no difference) version.

▶ Where the real difficulty in this spec point sits

The vocabulary and five conditions are comparatively low-difficulty recall once learned. The calculation is the genuinely demanding part — not because the arithmetic itself is hard, but because correctly identifying which given percentage corresponds to p, q, p² or q² before doing any calculating is the actual skill being tested. Practise this identification step until it's automatic and error-free, and the square-root/subtract/multiply arithmetic that follows becomes routine.

Section 8 · exam technique

✍️ Using this in longer written answers

3.7.2 works well as a supporting, quantifying paragraph inside several different essay titles — the trick is closing it with a sentence that ties the maths back to whatever the title is actually about.

This content supports essay titles built around themes such as the importance of natural selection, the importance of inheritance, or the importance of competition — anywhere a title needs a way to show variation at the population level being measured, not just described.

▶ Example paragraph — angled around measuring selection

A population's gene pool is the total collection of alleles present within it, and allele frequency describes how common a particular allele is within that pool. The Hardy–Weinberg principle provides a mathematical model predicting that these frequencies will stay constant from one generation to the next, provided there is no mutation, no selection, the population is large and genetically isolated, and mating occurs at random. Because a real population almost never meets every one of these conditions simultaneously, a measurable difference between the observed and Hardy–Weinberg-predicted genotype frequencies gives a quantitative, testable way of detecting that a factor such as selection is acting on that population — turning "selection is happening" from a qualitative claim into something that can actually be measured.

Whichever theme this paragraph supports, use the same order every time: define gene pool and allele frequency precisely, state the Hardy–Weinberg principle with at least one condition, then explain that a deviation from its prediction is the quantitative evidence — of selection, drift, migration or non-random mating — that the essay title is really asking about.

5-Minute Cram

Every spec content point, cut down to the facts that carry the most marks. Read this the night before, or right before you walk in.

1 · Species & populations

  • A species is split into multiple populations, not one interbreeding mass — each population can have its own local allele frequencies.
  • Everything on this page (gene pool, allele frequency, Hardy–Weinberg) is defined at the population level, never the species level.

2 · Defining a population

  • Three separately-creditable clauses: same species + particular space/time + can potentially interbreed.
  • Interbreeding (mating within a species) ≠ inbreeding (mating between close relatives) — never swap these.

3 · Gene pool & allele frequency

  • Gene pool = all the alleles in a population at a given time (must reference population, not species).
  • Allele frequency = the proportion of one allele in that gene pool — a proportion/percentage, never a raw count.
  • 5 things change a gene pool: mutation, natural selection, genetic drift, migration, non-random mating — same 5 as the Hardy–Weinberg conditions, reversed.

4 · The Hardy–Weinberg principle

  • Predicts allele frequencies won't change between generations, if 5 conditions hold: no mutation, no selection, no migration, random mating, large population.
  • A mismatch between observed and predicted frequencies is evidence a condition is broken — usually selection: the under-represented genotype is "selected against"/"less likely to survive and reproduce."

5 · The Hardy–Weinberg calculation

  • Fixed route: recessive phenotype % = q² → √ for q → 1−q for p → 2pq for carriers.
  • Dominant phenotype = p² + 2pq, not p² alone.
  • Never write "pq" for carriers — always 2pq. Never mistake the "unaffected" % for q².
🧠

p is Plentiful (dominant), q is Quieter (recessive). Square, root, subtract, double-multiply: q² given → √q² = q → 1−q = p → 2pq = carriers. Drift, founder effect and bottleneck all change allele frequency by chance, never by selection.

Markscheme-Wording Drill

Invented prompts, written in the style of how this spec point actually gets tested — not real past-paper questions. Click a question to reveal a model answer written in the precise wording that earns full marks, then try saying it back in your own words before you check.

Species & populations

❓ Explain why population genetics calculations are carried out at the level of a population rather than a whole species.
A species usually exists as several separate populations rather than one single interbreeding group; each population breeds largely within itself and so can build up its own distinct allele frequencies. A species-wide figure would therefore not accurately represent any one group of interbreeding organisms.
❓ Two groups of the same species live on opposite sides of a mountain range and never meet. Suggest what might happen to their allele frequencies over time, and why.
Without migration between them, each isolated population's allele frequencies can change independently — by selection, drift, or mutation — so the two populations' allele frequencies may gradually diverge from one another over successive generations.

Defining a population

❓ Define the term population.
A group of organisms of the same species, occupying a particular space at a particular time, that can potentially interbreed.
❓ A student defines a population as "organisms that live in the same place and can breed." Identify what is missing from this definition.
The definition omits that the organisms must be of the same species, and omits the time element — "at a particular time" — both of which are separately creditable parts of the full definition.
❓ Distinguish between interbreeding and inbreeding.
Interbreeding is mating within the same species, and is part of the definition of a population. Inbreeding is mating between closely related individuals, and tends to reduce genetic diversity — the two terms are not interchangeable.

Gene pool & allele frequency

❓ Define the terms gene pool and allele frequency.
Gene pool: all the alleles present in a population at a given time. Allele frequency: the proportion of a particular allele within that gene pool, expressed as a proportion, fraction or percentage.
❓ A student answers that a gene pool is "all the alleles present in a species." Explain why this would not gain full credit.
The definition must refer to a population, not to the species as a whole — a species typically consists of several separate populations, each with its own gene pool, so "species" is not an acceptable substitute for "population" here.
❓ Name three factors, other than natural selection, that can change the allele frequencies in a population's gene pool.
Any three of: mutation (the appearance of a new allele); genetic drift (chance dominating in a small population); migration/gene flow (individuals moving into or out of the population); non-random mating (mate choice linked to phenotype).

The Hardy–Weinberg principle

❓ State the Hardy–Weinberg principle, including the conditions under which it applies.
The Hardy–Weinberg principle predicts that the frequency of alleles in a population will not change from generation to generation, provided that: there is no mutation, there is no selection, the population is large, the population is genetically isolated (no migration), and mating occurs at random.
❓ In a population, the observed frequency of a recessive phenotype is significantly lower than the Hardy–Weinberg-predicted frequency. Suggest an explanation.
This suggests one of the Hardy–Weinberg conditions has been broken — most likely that individuals with the recessive phenotype are being selected against, and are less likely to survive and reproduce than individuals with the dominant phenotype, so the recessive allele's frequency is lower than the model predicts.
❓ Give two reasons, other than natural selection, why observed genotype frequencies in a real population might not match the Hardy–Weinberg prediction.
Any two of: the population is small, so genetic drift has a significant effect; migration has introduced or removed alleles; mating is not random; a new mutation has arisen; the sample taken was too small or unrepresentative.

The Hardy–Weinberg calculation

❓ In a population, 9% of individuals show a recessive phenotype. Calculate the percentage of the population that are heterozygous carriers, showing your working.
q² = 0.09, so q = √0.09 = 0.3. p = 1 − q = 1 − 0.3 = 0.7. Heterozygote frequency = 2pq = 2 × 0.7 × 0.3 = 0.42, so 42% of the population are carriers.
❓ A student calculates that in a population, p² = 0.49, 2pq = 0.30 and q² = 0.16. Explain why this cannot be correct.
Genotype frequencies must sum to 1 (p² + 2pq + q² = 1); here they sum to 0.95, so an error has been made somewhere in the working — most likely in calculating p or q from the original data.
❓ A gene has two codominant alleles. In a sample of 200 individuals, 50 are homozygous for allele A, 90 are heterozygous, and 60 are homozygous for allele B. Calculate the frequency of allele A.
Using the counting method: frequency of A = (2 × 50 + 90) ÷ (2 × 200) = 190 ÷ 400 = 0.475, so the frequency of allele A is 0.475 (47.5%).
